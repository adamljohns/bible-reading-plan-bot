@deck
Paul is describing a siege. The fortress is your own reasoning, and the point of taking it is not silence but obedience.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

The imagery is military and it is specific: siege warfare. In the verse before this, Paul says the weapons of our warfare are not fleshly but mighty through God to the pulling down of strongholds. Then this verse tells you what the strongholds are made of. Not demons in the walls. Arguments. Reasonings. Lofty opinions raised up against the knowledge of God.

That is a striking place to locate the battle. Paul is not describing an atmosphere or a spiritual force; he is describing *ideas* -- structures of thought a man has built and now lives inside, defended because they are his. The demolition equipment is aimed at the thing between your ears.

@word G2507
*Kathaireo* is demolition -- taking a structure down, pulling a ruler off a throne. It is the same verb used for taking Jesus' body down from the cross. There is nothing gentle in it. Paul is not proposing that these thoughts be balanced, contextualized, or managed. Strongholds do not get renovated.

@word G1869
The word means to lift up, to raise. It is used for lifting the eyes in prayer and raising hands in worship -- and here for something rearing up against the knowledge of God. That is the anatomy of the problem: a *height*. Anything set higher than what God has said, however reasonable, is a wall. Not everything false in you is stupid. Most of it is clever.

@word G163
A prisoner-of-war word -- *aichmalotizo*, to take captive at spear point. The direction of travel matters. Paul does not say destroy every thought; he says capture it. Prisoners are moved, not annihilated. The mind is not the enemy to be levelled; it is territory to be taken and put to work under new command.

@word G3540
*Noema* is a deliberate thought, a scheme, a mental purpose -- not a passing mood. Paul uses the same word for Satan's devices elsewhere in this letter. So this is not a war on stray impressions or intrusive thoughts you did not choose. It is a war on the reasoning you are actually conducting.

@word G5218
The target of the whole operation, and it is easy to miss. *Hypakoe* is from *hypo* (under) and *akouo* (to hear) -- hearing that places itself under what it hears. Every thought is taken captive *to the obedience of Christ*. Not to orthodoxy in the abstract, not to a correct position, but to a person, and to doing what He says.

@section 2. Where It Sits

2 Corinthians 10 begins the most personal section of the letter. Paul's authority is under attack in Corinth by men who evidently spoke better than he did and were happy to say so. They mocked his physical presence and his speech. Paul answers that he will not fight this on their terms -- he does not war according to the flesh -- and then, precisely there, describes the weapons he *does* use.

So the immediate stronghold in view is not a private struggle with impure thoughts. It is a rhetorical and theological attack on the gospel inside a church, conducted by impressive people with better delivery. That is what the reasonings are: arguments circulating in the congregation about who has authority and what counts as strength.

This is worth holding onto, because the verse is usually applied only inward, to the individual head. That application is legitimate -- what is true of a congregation's reasoning is true of a man's. But the original setting keeps it from becoming purely private and slightly paranoid. Paul is doing public theology under fire, and the thoughts he wants captured are the ones being argued out loud.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed instinct here has been to insist that the mind is neither trustworthy on its own nor to be despised. Both errors have long histories. One says reason is sovereign and Scripture must clear its bar. The other says thinking is the problem and simple feeling is safer.

Paul's verb refuses both. He does not demolish the mind; he demolishes what the mind has raised up, and then conscripts it. A captured thought is still a thought, now working for its captor. That is why the tradition has been able to hold rigorous scholarship and childlike submission together without embarrassment: the intellect is not being asked to leave the room, it is being asked who it reports to.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 13 — Of Sanctification | /lbcf/chapter-13.html
The dominion of the whole body of sin is destroyed, and the several lusts thereof are more and more weakened and mortified, and they more and more quickened and strengthened in all saving graces, to the practice of all true holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord.
@endquote

Two speeds in one sentence, and they match Paul exactly. *Dominion destroyed* is decisive and already accomplished -- the stronghold has fallen, the government has changed. *More and more weakened and mortified* is slow, ongoing, unfinished. The city is taken; the mopping up continues for years.

Read 2 Corinthians 10:5 without that distinction and it becomes a crushing verse -- every thought, captive, and you have not managed one hour. Read it with the confession and it is a description of a campaign you are already winning under a commander who has already taken the capital.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Notice the verse gives you something to *do* with a thought, which is more than most advice manages. You cannot stop a thought from arriving. You can arrest it. That is a concrete action: catch the sentence running in your head, say it out loud or write it down, and then ask the only question that matters -- is this higher than what God has said?

Most of what needs capturing will not feel like sin. It will feel like realism. *I am too far gone. He would leave if he knew. Nothing changes. I am the exception.* Each one is a height raised against the knowledge of God, and each one is defended by how reasonable it sounds given the evidence. That is what makes it a stronghold rather than a stray thought.

So take it prisoner and march it somewhere. To Scripture that says otherwise, to a brother who will say the true thing back to you, to prayer where you say it to God instead of rehearsing it to yourself. And do not wait to feel differently first. Obedience is the destination in this verse, not the feeling — you are moving a prisoner, and prisoners rarely go willingly.
