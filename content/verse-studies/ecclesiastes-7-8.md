Two comparisons in one breath. The finish outweighs the start, and the long spirit outweighs the high one. The Preacher ties them together on purpose: only a patient man lives long enough to see why the end is better.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

Better is the end of a thing than the beginning thereof: and the patient in spirit is better than the proud in spirit. Seven distinct Hebrew words carry the whole line, and two of them come twice. *Better* comes twice. *Spirit* comes twice. The verse is built like a balance with two pans, and the same word sits in the middle of each.

The first half is about time. The second half is about the man living through that time. Solomon is not handing out a motivational poster about finishing strong. He is saying that the man who cannot wait will never find out whether the end was better, because he will quit, lash out, or look back before it arrives.

@word H2896 | Better
*Tov* is the word God spoke over creation: it was good. It covers moral goodness, beauty, benefit and pleasure. Ecclesiastes 7 strings a run of these *tov* comparisons together. A good name is better than precious ointment. The day of death is better than the day of birth. The end is better than the beginning. The Preacher is weighing things on God's scale, not on the scale of how a moment feels.

@word H319 | end
*Acharit* comes from a root meaning behind or after. It is the latter end, the final state, the way a thing comes out. It is the same word behind "a hope and a future" in Jeremiah 29:11 and behind "the end of days" in the prophets. The end of a thing is where its meaning finally shows. Halfway through, you cannot read it yet.

@word H7225 | beginning
*Reshith* is the first in time, rank or quality. It is the opening word of the Bible, and it is the word for firstfruits. Beginnings are not bad in this verse. Firstfruits were holy. But the first sheaf is not the harvest. The verse refuses to let the excitement of a start stand in for the faithfulness that finishes it.

@word H750 | patient
*Arek* means long. Its best-known use is *erek appayim*, long of nostrils, slow to anger, which is how the LORD named Himself to Moses in Exodus 34:6. Here it is *erek ruach*, long of spirit. The patient man has a long fuse and a long view. He borrows his patience from the character of God Himself.

@word H7307 | spirit
*Ruach* is wind, breath, spirit. It is the inner man, the temper and drive of a person. The same word sits in both halves of the second line. Two men can have the same circumstances. What differs is the length or the height of the spirit they bring to them.

@word H1362 | proud
*Gabah* is high, tall, lifted up. It is used of Saul standing a head above the people and of tall trees that God cuts down in Ezekiel 31. Set against *arek*, the picture is plain: one spirit stretches out along the road, the other stands up tall in the moment. Long beats tall.

@section 2. Where It Sits

Ecclesiastes 7 turns from the Preacher's long complaint about vanity to a string of hard-won proverbs. Many of them sound backward at first. Mourning is better than feasting. Rebuke is better than song. Sorrow is better than laughter. The common thread is that God's scale weighs the long view, and the flesh weighs only the present.

Verse 8 sits in the middle of a four-verse unit. Verse 7 names the pressure: oppression makes a wise man mad, and a bribe corrupts the heart. Verse 8 gives the answer: the end is better, and the patient spirit wins. Verse 9 names the first danger: be not hasty in thy spirit to be angry, for anger resteth in the bosom of fools. Verse 10 names the second: say not thou, What is the cause that the former days were better than these? for thou dost not enquire wisely concerning this.

So the verse is flanked by two failures of patience. One is anger, which cannot wait for the end. The other is nostalgia, which keeps looking back at the beginning. Both are the proud spirit in different clothes. Anger says, "I should not have to bear this." Nostalgia says, "It was better when I was in it." The patient spirit says neither. It trusts that God is writing the end.

A few verses later the Preacher adds the theology underneath it: in the day of prosperity be joyful, but in the day of adversity consider, because God has set the one over against the other. Beginnings and endings, good days and hard ones, are not accidents. They are set by the same hand.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

Matthew Henry reads verse 8 as the Preacher's answer to the man under pressure in verse 7, and he puts the whole thing on faith:

@quote Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ecclesiastes 7:7-10 | https://biblehub.com/commentaries/mhcw/ecclesiastes/7.htm
The issue, at length, will be good: Better is the end of a thing than the beginning thereof. By faith see what the end will be, and with patience expect it.
@endquote

He reaches for Exodus to show it:

@quote Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ecclesiastes 7:7-10 | https://biblehub.com/commentaries/mhcw/ecclesiastes/7.htm
Better was the end of Moses’s treaty with Pharaoh, that proud oppressor, when Israel was brought forth with triumph, than the beginning of it, when the tale of bricks was doubled, and every thing looked discouraging.
@endquote

That is the point. The beginning of the Exodus looked like failure. Moses spoke to Pharaoh and the bricks got harder to make. A man judging by the beginning would have quit. The end was the Red Sea.

Henry then joins the two halves of the verse, and this is the line worth carrying:

@quote Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ecclesiastes 7:7-10 | https://biblehub.com/commentaries/mhcw/ecclesiastes/7.htm
That will break a proud man’s heart, which will not break a humble man’s sleep. Mortify pride, therefore, and a lowly spirit will easily be reconciled to a low condition.
@endquote

@quote Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ecclesiastes 7:7-10 | https://biblehub.com/commentaries/mhcw/ecclesiastes/7.htm
The patient in spirit are here opposed to the proud in spirit, for where there is humility there will be patience.
@endquote

Patience is not a separate virtue bolted on beside humility. It grows out of humility. The proud man cannot wait because he thinks he deserves the outcome now. The humble man can wait because he knows the outcome belongs to God.

Calvin says the same thing in his chapter on bearing the cross. Trouble is how God shows a man that his patience was never his own:

@quote Calvin, Institutes III.8 | /institutes/b3-c08.html
Since God has promised that he will be with believers in tribulation, they feel the truth of the promise; while they are upheld by his hand, they endure patiently. This they could never do by their own strength.
@endquote

And on pride he is blunt. Every gift we are tempted to swell over was handed to us:

@quote Calvin, Institutes III.7 | /institutes/b3-c07.html
For it teaches us to remember that the endowments God has bestowed on us are not our own but his free gifts, and that those who pride themselves on them betray their ingratitude.
@endquote

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

The end is better than the beginning for the believer because God does not abandon what He starts. The 1689 says it plainly:

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 17 — Of the Perseverance of the Saints | /lbcf/chapter-17.html
They will certainly persevere in grace to the end and be eternally saved, because the gifts and callings of God are without repentance.
@endquote

That is the deep ground under Ecclesiastes 7:8. A man's career, a season of service, a set of friendships can all close. The work God began in him does not. Philippians 1:6 says it in Paul's words: he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ. The end of the Christian is better than his beginning because Christ is the finisher of what He authored.

The Catechism ties the second half of the verse to the tenth commandment:

@quote The Baptist Catechism, Q. 85 | /catechism.html
The tenth commandment requires full contentment with our own condition, with a right and charitable frame of spirit toward our neighbor, and all that is his.
@endquote

The proud spirit is never content. It is always measuring this season against the last one, or against another man's. The patient spirit has a right frame. It can be glad for what was, grateful for what is, and quiet about what is coming.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Some of you are standing at an end right now. A job you held for years. A unit. A church. A house. A season with men you came to love. The ending is real and the grief is real. Scripture does not ask you to pretend otherwise. When Paul left the Ephesian elders at Miletus, they all wept sore, and fell on Paul's neck, and kissed him. Then they walked him to the ship. Grief and going are not opposites.

But do three things.

**Finish well.** The end of a thing is better than the beginning only if it is a real end and not a walk-off. Close out your work so the man behind you is not cleaning up after you. Say your goodbyes in person. Paul wanted to finish his course with joy, and he took the time to kneel down and pray with them all before he went.

**Do not look back the wrong way.** Ecclesiastes 7:10 sits two verses down for a reason. Gratitude for the old post is good. Saying the old days were better, as if God were done being good, is not wise. The God who gave you those seven years is the same God sending you to the next two.

**Keep the long spirit.** New posts bring new frustrations. A long fuse with new people is how a man shows that his patience came from God and not from comfort. Pride wants to arrive as the expert. Patience is willing to be the new guy again.

And remember where the real end is. Every career ends. Every contract runs out. The man in Christ is headed for an end that is better than every beginning he has ever had, because the One who began the work will finish it. Run the next leg like a man who already knows how the race comes out.
