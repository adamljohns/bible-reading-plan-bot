Two comparisons in one breath: the finish outlasts the start, and the long spirit outpaces the high one. Solomon put them side by side on purpose, because only a patient man stays on the road long enough to find out why the end was better.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

Better is the end of a thing than the beginning thereof: and the patient in spirit is better than the proud in spirit. The line is built like a set of scales. *Better* sits in the middle of the first half and again in the middle of the second, and *spirit* shows up on both sides of the second comparison. Solomon, the second wisest man who ever lived, wrote it that way so you would weigh the two halves against each other.

The first half is about time, and the second half is about the man who has to live through it. Solomon isn't selling a slogan about finishing strong; his point is harder than that. The man who can't wait never learns whether the end was better, because he quits, lashes out, or starts looking back before the end ever comes.

@word H2896 | Better
*Tov* is the word God spoke over His finished work in Genesis 1, when He saw everything He had made and called it very good. It can mean morally good, beautiful, useful or pleasant. Chapter 7 strings a whole run of these comparisons together: a good name over precious ointment, the house of mourning over the house of feasting, the rebuke of the wise over the song of fools. In every one of them Solomon is weighing things on God's scale instead of by how they feel in the moment.

@word H319 | end
*Acharit* comes from a root meaning behind or after, so it points to the latter end of something, the way it finally turns out. It's the same word God uses in Jeremiah 29:11 when He promises a future and a hope, and the prophets use it for the end of days. That is the key to the verse: you can't read the meaning of a thing from the middle of it, because the meaning only shows at the end.

@word H7225 | beginning
*Reshith* means the first, whether first in time, in rank or in quality. It's the first word of the Bible, and it's also the word for firstfruits, the first sheaf that belonged to God. So Solomon isn't running beginnings down; firstfruits were holy. Still, the first sheaf isn't the harvest, and the excitement of starting something is no stand-in for the faithfulness it takes to finish it.

@word H750 | patient
*Arek* simply means long. Its best-known use is the phrase *erek appayim*, literally long of nostrils, which we translate slow to anger. That's how the LORD described Himself to Moses in Exodus 34:6. Here Solomon writes *erek ruach*, long of spirit. A patient man has a long fuse and a long view, and he gets both from the God who has been patient with him.

@word H7307 | spirit
*Ruach* is wind, breath or spirit. Here it means the inner man, his temper and his drive. Because the same word sits on both sides of the comparison, the difference between the two men isn't in their circumstances. Two men can face the same hard season, and what separates them is the kind of spirit each one brings into it.

@word H1362 | proud
*Gabah* means high or tall. Scripture uses it for Saul standing a head above the crowd and for the towering trees in Ezekiel 31 that God cuts down. Put it next to *arek* and the picture comes into focus. One spirit stretches out along the road for the long haul, and the other stands up tall in the moment. Over any real distance, long outpaces tall.

@section 2. The Verse in Context

Ecclesiastes 7 turns from Solomon's long lament over vanity to a string of proverbs, and many of them sound backward on first reading. The house of mourning is better than the house of feasting. Sorrow is better than laughter. The rebuke of the wise beats the song of fools. What ties them together is that God's scale weighs the long view, while the flesh only weighs what's in front of it.

Verse 8 sits inside a tight run of four verses. Verse 7 names the pressure: oppression can drive even a wise man mad, and a bribe corrupts the heart. Verse 8 gives the answer, that the end is better and the patient spirit wins. Then verses 9 and 10 name the two ways a man fails that test. The first is anger: be not hasty in thy spirit to be angry, for anger resteth in the bosom of fools. The second is nostalgia: say not thou, What is the cause that the former days were better than these? for thou dost not enquire wisely concerning this.

So the verse has a failure of patience on each side of it. Anger can't wait for the end, and nostalgia can't stop staring at the beginning. Both come from the proud spirit, just dressed differently. Anger says, "I shouldn't have to put up with this," and nostalgia says, "Things were better back when I was there." The patient man says neither one, because he trusts that God is still writing the end.

A few verses later Solomon gives the theology under all of it. In the day of prosperity be joyful, he says, but in the day of adversity consider, because God has set the one over against the other. Good seasons and hard ones, beginnings and endings, come from the same hand.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

Matthew Henry reads verse 8 as Solomon's answer to the man under pressure in verse 7, and he rests the whole thing on faith:

@quote Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ecclesiastes 7:7-10 | https://biblehub.com/commentaries/mhcw/ecclesiastes/7.htm
The issue, at length, will be good: Better is the end of a thing than the beginning thereof. By faith see what the end will be, and with patience expect it.
@endquote

Then he goes to Exodus to show it:

@quote Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ecclesiastes 7:7-10 | https://biblehub.com/commentaries/mhcw/ecclesiastes/7.htm
Better was the end of Moses’s treaty with Pharaoh, that proud oppressor, when Israel was brought forth with triumph, than the beginning of it, when the tale of bricks was doubled, and every thing looked discouraging.
@endquote

The beginning of the Exodus looked like a failure. Moses went in to Pharaoh, and the only result was that the bricks got harder to make, so anyone judging by that beginning would have told Moses to quit. The end was Israel walking out through the Red Sea.

Henry then ties the two halves of the verse together, and this is the line worth carrying with you:

@quote Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ecclesiastes 7:7-10 | https://biblehub.com/commentaries/mhcw/ecclesiastes/7.htm
That will break a proud man’s heart, which will not break a humble man’s sleep. Mortify pride, therefore, and a lowly spirit will easily be reconciled to a low condition.
@endquote

@quote Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, on Ecclesiastes 7:7-10 | https://biblehub.com/commentaries/mhcw/ecclesiastes/7.htm
The patient in spirit are here opposed to the proud in spirit, for where there is humility there will be patience.
@endquote

In other words, patience grows out of humility. The proud man can't wait because he believes he's owed the outcome right now. The humble man can wait because he knows the outcome belongs to God.

Calvin makes the same point in his chapter on bearing the cross, where he argues that hard seasons are how God shows a man his patience was never his own:

@quote Calvin, Institutes III.8 | /institutes/b3-c08.html
Since God has promised that he will be with believers in tribulation, they feel the truth of the promise; while they are upheld by his hand, they endure patiently. This they could never do by their own strength.
@endquote

He's just as plain about pride. Whatever gift a man is tempted to swell up over was handed to him in the first place:

@quote Calvin, Institutes III.7 | /institutes/b3-c07.html
For it teaches us to remember that the endowments God has bestowed on us are not our own but his free gifts, and that those who pride themselves on them betray their ingratitude.
@endquote

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

For the believer, the end really is better than the beginning, because God doesn't abandon what He starts. The 1689 Confession says so plainly:

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 17 — Of the Perseverance of the Saints | /lbcf/chapter-17.html
They will certainly persevere in grace to the end and be eternally saved, because the gifts and callings of God are without repentance.
@endquote

That's the bedrock under Ecclesiastes 7:8. A career can close, and so can a season of service or a circle of friends, but the work God began in a man doesn't close with them. Paul says it in Philippians 1:6: he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ. Hebrews 12:2 calls Jesus the author and finisher of our faith, and what He authors, He finishes.

The Baptist Catechism ties the second half of the verse to the tenth commandment:

@quote The Baptist Catechism, Q. 85 | /catechism.html
The tenth commandment requires full contentment with our own condition, with a right and charitable frame of spirit toward our neighbor, and all that is his.
@endquote

The proud spirit is never content, because it keeps measuring this season against the last one or against some other man's. The patient spirit has the right frame the catechism describes. It can be glad for what was, thankful for what is, and at peace about what's coming.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Some of you are standing at an ending right now. Maybe it's a job you held for years, a unit, a church, a house, or a season with men you came to love. The ending is real and so is the grief, and Scripture never asks you to pretend otherwise. When Paul said goodbye to the Ephesian elders at Miletus, they all wept sore, and fell on Paul's neck, and kissed him. Then they walked him down to the ship. Grieving and going belong together.

As you go, do three things.

*Finish well.* An ending is only better than the beginning if it's a real finish and not a walk-off. Close out your work so the man behind you isn't left cleaning up after you, and say your goodbyes face to face. Paul wanted to finish his course with joy, and before he left Miletus he knelt down and prayed with them all.

*Don't look back the wrong way.* Verse 10 sits two verses down for a reason. Being thankful for the old post is right. Insisting the old days were better, as though God were finished being good to you, is not wise. The God who gave you the years behind you is the same God sending you into the years ahead.

*Keep the long spirit.* A new post brings new frustrations and new people to learn. Keeping a long fuse with them is how a man shows his patience came from God and not from being comfortable. Pride wants to walk in as the expert, while patience is willing to be the new guy again.

Last, remember where the real end is. Every career ends and every contract runs out, but the man in Christ is headed toward an end that's better than any beginning he's ever had, because the One who began the work will finish it. Run the next leg like a man who already knows how the race comes out.
