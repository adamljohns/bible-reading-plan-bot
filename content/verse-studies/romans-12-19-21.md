A command about revenge that refuses to leave justice undone. Paul takes the sword out of your hand and puts it in God's, then tells you what to do with the empty hand.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

Three sentences, and they are not three subjects. Do not avenge yourselves. Make room for wrath. Then a quotation from the Law: vengeance is mine, I will repay. *Therefore* -- if your enemy hungers, feed him. Then the last line, which is the whole paragraph in one stroke: do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good.

The *therefore* is doing real work. Feeding the enemy is not a second topic after the vengeance question is settled. It is what a man does once he has believed that the repayment is not his. Paul does not leave a vacuum. He takes one action away and names another.

Notice also who is addressed. *Dearly beloved.* These are not spectators of someone else's feud. They are people close enough to the injury to want the sword back.

@word G1556 | avenge
*Ekdikeo* is to avenge, to vindicate, to carry out justice for a wrong. It is not a petty word. Paul is not forbidding a man to notice that he was wronged. He is forbidding him to sit as the court. The verb assumes a real injury and a real debt. What it denies is that you are the one appointed to collect.

@word G5117 | place
*Topos* is a place, and then an opportunity, a seat that can be occupied. *Give place unto wrath* is not "calm down" and it is not "pretend you do not care." It is: vacate the chair. Someone else is going to sit there. The wrath in view is not yours. If you occupy the place, you have crowded out the one whose place it is.

@word G3709 | wrath
*Orge* is settled, purposeful anger -- not a flash of temper. In Paul it is most often God's. Romans has been using this word since 1:18, where the wrath of God is revealed against all ungodliness. So "give place unto wrath" in this letter already has a definition. You are being told to step aside for the wrath the letter opened with, not to nurse a smaller private version of it.

@word G2190 | enemy
*Echthros* is an adversary, active hostility, not a person you merely dislike. Paul quotes Proverbs 25 almost word for word: if he hungers, feed him; if he thirsts, give him drink. The command is not to feel warmth. It is to do the next concrete mercy -- food, water -- toward a man who means you harm. Feeling can wait. The plate cannot.

@word G2556 | evil
*Kakos* is evil as what is bad, harmful, missing the good -- the thing done to you and the thing you are tempted to become. Verse 21 uses it twice in one breath. *Do not be overcome by evil* -- do not let the injury set the terms of your next move. *Overcome evil with good* -- the only victory on offer in this paragraph is not the enemy's defeat at your hand. It is that the evil did not get to reproduce itself in you.

@h3 The coals and the quotation
Verse 19 quotes Deuteronomy 32: *Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord.* That is the Song of Moses, God speaking over a people who cannot settle their own scores cleanly. Verse 20 quotes Proverbs 25. Paul is not inventing a Christian extra. He is stacking the Law on the Law and then telling a Gentile church it applies to the man who hurt them last week.

The coals of fire on the head are the image Proverbs already used, and the verse does not pause to unpack them. Kindness that burns -- shame that might turn a man, or a burning that leaves him without excuse -- both have been read here. What the sentence will not let you do is withhold the food until you have decided which. The feeding is the obedience. The coals are God's to assign.

@section 2. Where It Sits

Romans 12 began with a body on an altar: present your bodies a living sacrifice. Then: do not be conformed to this world. Then the gifts. Then a run of short commands that sound like the Sermon on the Mount because they are: bless those who persecute you; repay no one evil for evil; as far as it depends on you, live at peace with all men. Our verses are that list reaching its hardest case. Peace with all men, *if it is possible*. Sometimes it is not. Then this.

What follows is not a change of subject. Chapter 13 opens with the magistrate, God's servant, who *does not bear the sword in vain*, a revenger to execute wrath on him who does evil. Read 12:19 without 13:4 and you will think Paul has banned justice. He has banned *private* justice. The wrath you make room for includes the sword God put in another man's hand, and the last assize He has not yet held. You are not told that the wrong will go unpaid. You are told you are not the payee's collector.

That order is the doctrine. The living sacrifice of 12:1 includes this: your right to collect, laid down, because the Lord said the collection is His.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin took the quotation at full force. It is not advice for the especially devout. It is the Law, still in force, and the Lord is not a counsellor you may decline.

@quote Calvin, Institutes II.8 | /institutes/b2-c08.html
Is not the word of the Lord eternally true: "Vengeance is mine, I will repay?"
@endquote

He is arguing, in that chapter, against the schoolmen who had turned enemy-love into a counsel for monks. If vengeance belongs to God, then refusing to avenge is not a specialty of the cloister. It is ordinary obedience. The same pages reach for Proverbs 25 and Exodus 23 -- feed the enemy, return his ox -- which is exactly the stack Paul uses here. Calvin is reading our verses as Moses still speaking.

On the other side of the sword he is just as plain. Private refusal of revenge is not a ban on courts.

@quote Calvin, Institutes IV.20 | /institutes/b4-c20.html
Meanwhile, they must do good to those who injure them, and pray for those who curse them, and (this is their only victory) strive to overcome evil with good.
@endquote

*This is their only victory.* Calvin will not let the church pretend that turning the other cheek is a way of secretly winning the feud. The win is that evil did not write the next line. Magistrates still punish. Paul himself appealed to Caesar. The man who will not take the sword home is not a man who believes nothing should be done. He is a man who believes God has already assigned the doing.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

The confession's chapter on the magistrate is the other half of this paragraph. If God has not armed anyone, then "I will repay" is only a future, and a wounded man is being asked to live with a vacuum. The divines would not leave that vacuum.

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 24 — Of the Civil Magistrate | /lbcf/chapter-24.html
God, the supreme Lord and King of all the world, has ordained civil magistrates to be under him, over the people, for his own glory and the public good. To this end he has armed them with the power of the sword, for the defense and encouragement of those who do good, and for the punishment of evildoers.
@endquote

*Armed them with the power of the sword* is what "give place unto wrath" looks like in a commonwealth. You may call the constable. You may not become one in your kitchen. The same confession that forbids you to avenge yourself refuses to baptize the crime by calling all force unchristian.

And the feeding is not a gesture. It is a good work, which the confession will not let you treat as optional decoration.

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 16 — Of Good Works | /lbcf/chapter-16.html
These good works, done in obedience to God's commandments, are the fruits and evidences of a true and lively faith; and by them believers manifest their thankfulness, strengthen their assurance, edify their brethren, adorn the profession of the gospel, stop the mouths of the adversaries, and glorify God.
@endquote

*Stop the mouths of the adversaries* is close to the coals. A plate of food will not always convert the man. It will sometimes shut the mouth that was waiting to say you are no different. Paul thinks that is worth doing even when the heart has not caught up.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

The first obedience is negative and it is the harder one: you do not get to collect. Not in the truck on the way home. Not in the story you tell at the table, trimmed so that you come out clean and he comes out finished. Not in the quiet campaign to make sure other people know what he is. That is vengeance with better manners, and the verse does not have a clause for it.

Then the positive one, which is embarrassingly small. If he hungers, feed him. Paul does not say, arrange a reconciliation. He does not say, feel something new first. He names the mercy you can do with your hands while the anger is still in your chest. That is how you give place. You fill the hands with something that is not a weapon.

You are allowed to use the courts when the matter belongs there. Paul is about to say so. What you are not allowed is to confuse the court's work with your need to feel paid. Plenty of men have used a lawful process as a cover for a private hunt. The test is whether you could stop, if the magistrate said stop, and still leave the wrath in God's place.

Overcome is the last word, and it is a war word. Evil wins a man in two ways: by crushing him, or by recruiting him. The second is the usual way in a feud. You become the kind of man who would have done what was done to you, and you call it spine. Paul calls it being overcome. The good that overcomes is not a mood and it is not a technique. It is the next commanded mercy, done because the Lord said the rest of the account is His, and He does not default.
