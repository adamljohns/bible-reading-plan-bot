@deck
The most misquoted comfort in the New Testament. Paul is not promising you can handle it. He is promising there is a door.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

You have heard this verse used as a compliment: God won't give you more than you can handle. That is not what it says, and the difference is not academic. Read it again slowly. It does not say God will not give you more than you can handle. It says He will not let you be *tempted* beyond what you are able, and that He will make a way out. Those are different sentences. Life will hand you far more than you can handle -- grief, diagnosis, the phone call at 2 a.m. Paul is talking about something narrower and more urgent: the pull toward sin, and whether you are ever cornered into it.

The context is a warning, not a pat on the back. Paul has just walked through Israel in the wilderness -- idolatry, grumbling, bodies in the sand -- and said these things were written down for us. Verse 12 is the sentence immediately before this one: let him who thinks he stands take heed lest he fall. Then comes verse 13. The comfort is handed to a man who has just been told he is in more danger than he thinks.

@word G3986
The noun carries both meanings at once and the verse needs both. *Peirasmos* is a test and an enticement -- the same event from two directions. A trial is what God is doing in it; a temptation is what the devil is doing in it; the circumstance is one circumstance. Which is why the verse can promise God's faithfulness inside something that also feels like an ambush.

@word G442
A quietly deflating word, and a mercy. Whatever has hold of you is *human-sized*. It is not a unique affliction, not a special category, not evidence that you are uniquely broken. Other men have stood exactly where you are standing. Sin does most of its work by convincing you that no one would understand, and this word takes that lie away.

@word G4103
The load-bearing word in the sentence. Notice where Paul puts the guarantee. Not in your willpower, not in your record, not in your maturity -- in the character of God. *Pistos* means reliable, trustworthy, the kind of thing you can put weight on. The verse is not a statement about your strength. It is a statement about His consistency.

@word G1545
This is the picture that changes the verse. *Ekbasis* is *ek* (out of) plus a stepping -- an exit, a way out, the pass through the mountains. It is a geographic word before it is a spiritual one. God does not promise to make the pressure smaller. He promises there is a door in the room, and that He put it there.

@h3 What "with the temptation" is doing
The preposition is easy to skim and it carries the weight. The way out arrives *with* the temptation, not after it. It is not issued later as a reward for holding out; it is already in the room when the pressure walks in. Which means the honest question in the moment is never "can I survive this?" It is "where is the door?" — and that question has an answer.

@section 2. Where It Sits

1 Corinthians 10 is Paul preaching from the wilderness. Israel came out of Egypt under the cloud, through the sea, eating the same spiritual food -- every one of them baptized into Moses, every one of them a member of the covenant community -- and with most of them God was not pleased. They fell in the desert. Paul's point is that religious membership is not a force field. You can be in the congregation, in the sacraments, in good standing, and still go down.

So the paragraph is written to a confident man. The Corinthians thought their knowledge protected them; they were eating at idol feasts and reasoning that an idol is nothing, so what harm. Paul answers that the danger is not metaphysical, it is spiritual, and that presumption is precisely how men fall. Verse 12 knocks the confidence out. Verse 13 catches what falls.

That sequence is the pastoral genius of the passage. Warn him hard, then hold him up. A warning without the promise produces despair; a promise without the warning produces the carelessness that got Israel killed. Paul refuses to separate them, and neither should you when you carry this verse.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed reading of this verse has always run through providence rather than through psychology. The question is not what you are capable of enduring but what God is governing. Nothing reaches you that has not passed through His hand first, including the pressure -- and a God who governs the pressure can be trusted to have built the exit.

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 5 — Of Divine Providence | /lbcf/chapter-05.html
God the good Creator of all things, in his infinite power and wisdom, does uphold, direct, dispose, and govern all creatures and things, from the greatest even to the least, by his most wise and holy providence.
@endquote

*From the greatest even to the least* is the phrase that matters at 2 a.m. The temptation in front of you is not an oversight, not a gap in the security. It is inside the government. That is a harder comfort than the sentimental reading, and a far more durable one, because it does not depend on the pressure being mild.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 17 — Of the Perseverance of the Saints | /lbcf/chapter-17.html
Many storms and floods may rise and beat against them, but they shall never be torn from the foundation and rock to which faith has fastened them.
@endquote

Notice what the confession does *not* claim. It does not say the storms will be small, or few, or that you will feel secure while they hit. It says you will not be torn off. The verb is passive on purpose -- the holding is not being done by your grip.

This is the doctrinal shape of 1 Corinthians 10:13. The verse guarantees an exit, and the confession explains why the guarantee holds: because the One who set the limit is the One who keeps you, and He does not lose people. Perseverance is not a prediction about your stamina. It is a statement about His.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Stop asking whether you are strong enough. That question has never once helped a man in the moment of temptation, because the honest answer is usually no, and the answer arrives with shame attached. Ask the other question. *Where is the door?* Then go look for it, because the verse says it is there.

Usually it is embarrassingly physical. Leave the room. Hand over the phone. Call the man who will ask you the direct question. Drive the other way home. The way of escape is almost never a feeling of renewed spiritual strength; it is a boring, undignified, practical exit that you can take while still wanting the sin. You do not have to stop wanting it first.

And take the warning as seriously as the promise. The men who fall are rarely the ones who knew they were in danger. They are the ones who had this verse memorized and used it as insurance -- who walked into the room confident, because God would provide a way out. He does. Through the door you refuse to look for, right up until you are no longer looking for anything.
