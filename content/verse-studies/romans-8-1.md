@deck
Paul has spent seven chapters proving you are guilty. Then he opens the eighth by telling you the case is closed.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

The sentence is a verdict. Not a feeling, not an encouragement, not a hope that things will work out -- a verdict, handed down, in a courtroom, about you. Everything in it turns on that.

Notice first what Paul does *not* say. He does not say there is no sin. He does not say there is nothing left in you to grieve over; he has just spent the back half of chapter 7 describing a man at war with himself, doing what he hates. He says there is no *condemnation*. The sin is real. The sentence is gone.

@word G686
This is the hinge of the letter. *Ara* does not introduce a new subject; it collects a debt. Everything Paul has argued from 1:18 forward -- that the pagan is guilty, that the moralist is guilty, that the man with the law is guilty, that no one is justified by works -- has been building toward a bill someone has to pay. The particle says: given all that, here is what follows.

@word G2631
This is the word that decides the verse. *Katakrima* is not the feeling of being condemned; it is the sentence itself, written down and enforceable. A man can feel condemned while standing acquitted, and he can feel fine while standing under sentence. Paul is not addressing how you feel about your record. He is telling you what the court has done with it.

@word G1722
Two words carry the weight of the sentence, and this small one is the second: *in* Christ Jesus. Not near Him, not approved by Him, not working for Him. The acquittal does not attach to your performance. It attaches to your location.

@word G4043
Walking is the Bible's picture for the ordinary direction of a life -- not a single step, but where the steps are going. Paul is not describing a man who never stumbles. He is describing which way a man is headed when he gets up.

@word G4561
Worth being careful here, because the word gets misused. *Sarx* in Paul is not the body, and this verse is not a hint that matter is dirty and spirit is clean. That is a Greek idea, not a Hebrew one, and Genesis 1 already ruled it out. The flesh is the self running on its own resources, which is precisely the man of chapter 7.

@h3 A note on the longer reading
If you carry a modern translation, you may have noticed it stops early: "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" -- and nothing about walking. The second half of the verse in the King James is absent from the oldest manuscripts, and most editors judge that a scribe carried it up from verse 4, where Paul says the same thing. It is worth knowing and it is worth not panicking about: the words are genuinely Paul's, they simply belong three verses later. Nothing in the doctrine moves either way. Both readings are printed above so you can see exactly what is and is not in question, rather than discovering the discrepancy alone with your study Bible and concluding someone was hiding it.

@section 2. Where It Sits

You cannot read this verse straight if you skip what it is standing on. Romans 7 ends with a man in pieces. He knows the law is good. He wants to keep it. He watches himself not keep it, and he cries out asking who will deliver him from this body of death. That is where chapter 7 leaves you -- a believer, genuinely converted, genuinely losing fights.

Then comes *therefore*. Paul does not answer the man of chapter 7 by telling him to try harder, and he does not answer him by questioning whether he was ever saved. He answers him with a legal fact. The war is real, the verdict is already in, and the verdict was never contingent on how the war was going.

That order matters, because we habitually reverse it. We assume the acquittal arrives once the walking improves. Paul puts the acquittal first and the walking second, and the next verse explains why that is not a loophole: the same Christ who removed the sentence sent the Spirit who changes the direction. You are not handed a pardon and left where you were.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin's account of justification is built on exactly this courtroom picture, and he is careful to keep the verdict outside the man rather than inside him.

@quote Calvin, Institutes III.11 | /institutes/b3-c11.html
He, on the other hand, is justified who is regarded not as a sinner, but as righteous, and as such stands acquitted at the judgment-seat of God, where all sinners are condemned.
@endquote

Note the phrase *where all sinners are condemned*. Calvin is not saying the bench went soft. The court that acquits you is the same court that condemns; it has not lowered its standard, and the man walking out of it is not innocent. He is covered.

@quote Calvin, Institutes III.11 | /institutes/b3-c11.html
But herein is the wondrous method of justification, that, clothed with the righteousness of Christ, they dread not the judgment of which they are worthy, and while they justly condemn themselves, are yet deemed righteous out of themselves.
@endquote

"While they justly condemn themselves" is the clause to sit with. Calvin grants that your own verdict on yourself may be perfectly accurate. You may be right about what you did. He simply denies that your verdict is the one that counts, and that is the whole difference between honest self-knowledge and despair.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 19 — Of the Law of God | /lbcf/chapter-19.html
Although true believers are not under the law as a covenant of works, to be thereby justified or condemned, yet it is of great use to them as well as to others. As a rule of life, informing them of the will of God and their duty, it directs and binds them to walk accordingly.
@endquote

The divines reached for this verse when they had to answer the obvious objection: if there is no condemnation, why obey anything? Their answer is that the law changes office rather than disappearing. It is no longer the covenant you are being measured by. It remains the description of the life you were bought for. A rule of life is not a sentence hanging over you; it is a map of the country you now live in.

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 11 — Of Justification | /lbcf/chapter-11.html
Those whom God effectually calls, he also freely justifies; not by infusing righteousness into them, but by pardoning their sins, and by accounting and accepting their persons as righteous; not for anything wrought in them, or done by them, but for Christ's sake alone.
@endquote

*Not by infusing righteousness into them* is doing precise work. The confession is refusing the idea that God first makes you good enough and then acquits you on that basis. He pardons, and He accounts. If it were infusion, the verse would have to read "there is therefore now less condemnation," and it would be a progress report. It is not a progress report.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Here is what it costs to believe this. You have to give up the private arrangement where you pay for your own failures with a stretch of feeling terrible. That arrangement is comfortable, because it is yours and you control it, and because misery feels like a kind of payment. Paul's sentence takes it away from you. There is no condemnation left to serve. Sitting in the cell after the door is open is not humility; it is a refusal to accept that someone else settled the account.

And here is what it does not license. It does not say the flesh no longer matters. The second half of the verse -- wherever it properly belongs -- is still Paul, and he says the men who are in Christ are walking a different direction. What you do on Tuesday matters enormously. It simply is not what is holding up the verdict.

So the practical shape of a Monday is this. When you fail, you tell the truth about it, quickly and without drama, because you are not defending a record that has to stay clean. Then you get up. The man who knows he is not on trial is the only man free enough to look straight at what he did.
