The exile letter's first command about the city is not to escape it. Seek its peace, pray for it, and take your own peace as a share of that -- not a private store.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

Three times the verse says *peace,* and all three are the same Hebrew word. Seek the *shalom* of the city. Pray for it. In the *shalom* thereof you shall have *shalom.* The King James and the World English Bible keep that repetition, and they are right to. Later English often reaches for "welfare" or "prosperity," which are not false, but they hide the bind. Your well-being is not a separate account. It is located inside the city's.

The sender is not Jeremiah's pastoral advice. Verse 4 has already named the speaker: the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel, to the captives *whom I have caused to be carried away.* The city in this verse is Babylon. The God of Israel is telling His people to seek the good of the empire that burned their temple. If that does not startle you, you have not yet heard it.

@word H1875 | seek
*Darash* is to seek with care -- to beat a path, inquire, require. It is not a glance. It is the verb for seeking the LORD, and for the kind of study that leaves a track. So "seek the peace of the city" is not "wish the city well." It is an assignment: go after the city's wholeness as a man goes after a thing he is responsible to find.

That is harder than opposition and harder than withdrawal. Opposition at least keeps your identity clean. Withdrawal lets you call the place a judgment and wash your hands. *Darash* puts your hands on the city's good while you are still a captive in it.

@word H7965 | peace
*Shalom* is wholeness -- complete well-being, not the mere stopping of war. Jeremiah's whole book is a fight over this word. False prophets cry peace when there is none. Here the true prophet tells exiles to seek *shalom* for Babylon, of all places. The word has not gone soft. The object has changed. The people who lost Jerusalem's peace are now commanded to seek Babylon's, because their lives are now bound up with it.

Do not let the later verse in this chapter steal this one. Verse 11 will speak of thoughts of peace. Verse 7 has already defined what peace looks like for the next seventy years: the city's, then yours inside it.

@word H1540 | carried away captives
*Galah* is to uncover, to reveal, and to go into exile. The two senses share a picture: something displaced from its place, laid bare. Jeremiah will not let the exiles tell the story as if Nebuchadnezzar merely happened. *I have caused you to be carried away.* The verb is God's. Babylon is not an interruption of the plan. It is, for this stretch, the place of the plan.

That is why the command can be issued without apology. If God caused the carrying, then seeking that city's peace is not collaboration against Him. It is obedience to Him in the place He named.

@word H6419 | pray
*Palal* is to pray, to intercede. The verse does not stop at civic effort. Seek, *and pray unto the LORD for it.* The city's *shalom* is not something you can finally secure by gardens and trade. You ask God for it. You ask the God of Israel to do good to Babylon. That will feel like a contradiction in the mouth until you notice the last clause: in its peace you shall have peace. Your petition for them is not charity from a safe distance. It is how your own life will go.

Prayer here is also a guardrail. Seeking a city's peace can turn into making the city your god. The verse sends the seeking *through* the LORD. You work for Babylon. You do not pray *to* it.

@section 2. Where It Sits

This is a letter, not a proverb. Jeremiah writes from Jerusalem to the elders, priests, prophets, and people already taken to Babylon with Jeconiah. False hope is the air in both cities: prophets saying the exile will be short, the vessels coming back, two years and home. Chapter 28 has just had Hananiah break a yoke in public and die for the lie.

Into that, the LORD says: build houses, dwell in them, plant gardens, eat the fruit, take wives, bear children, increase there and do not diminish. Then verse 7. Then, immediately: do not let your prophets and diviners deceive you. I have not sent them. After seventy years I will visit you and bring you back. *Then* the thoughts of peace, the calling, the seeking with the whole heart, the gathering.

Verse 7 is what you do *during* the seventy years. Verse 11 is what God thinks toward the end of them. Read 11 without 7 and you get a slogan for a move you have not been asked to make yet. Read 7 without 10 and you get a call to settle forever in Babylon as if there were no promise. The letter holds both. Plant the garden. Pray for the city. Do not believe the man who says you will be home by spring.

The "I have caused" also sits against the politics of the moment. Some in Jerusalem were still playing Egypt against Babylon. The letter tells the already-deported the truth the stay-behinds would not hear: this carrying is from the LORD, and the next faithfulness is local -- houses, marriages, a pagan city's peace -- until He says otherwise.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed reading put this verse in the doctrine of the civil magistrate, not in a general spirituality of "be nice where you live." God had given the kingdom to Nebuchadnezzar. The people stripped, displaced, and bound were nevertheless ordered to pray for the victor's tranquillity, so that they themselves might live. Calvin is explicit that this is not only the ordinary command to pray for persecutors. It is a prayer for the *preservation* of the regime under which they had been placed, because their peace was now located there.

That is a harder obedience than most uses of the verse on a civic Sunday. It does not baptize every policy of the city. It forbids the fantasy that holiness means you owe the place nothing. The same tradition would quote Paul: prayers for kings and all in high places, that we may lead a quiet and quiet life. Jeremiah had already said it to people who had more reason than we do to refuse.

They also refused the reading that makes seeking the city's peace a path to forgetting Zion. Psalm 137 is still in the canon: if I forget you, O Jerusalem. Jeremiah 29 is not the cancellation of that grief. It is what to do with your hands while you remember. Build. Plant. Pray. Seventy years is long enough to raise children who have never seen the temple, and still short enough that God has numbered it. The witnesses would not let either number be fudged -- neither the long stay nor the promised end.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 24 — Of the Civil Magistrate | /lbcf/chapter-24.html
Civil magistrates having been set up by God for the ends just stated, we ought to render to them subjection in all lawful things commanded by them — in the Lord, not only for wrath but also for conscience' sake. We ought also to make supplications and prayers for kings and all who are in authority, that under them we may live a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and honesty.
@endquote

*Supplications and prayers for kings* is Jeremiah 29:7 carried into the church's ordinary life. The confession keeps the same logic as the letter: under them we may live a quiet and peaceable life. Your piety is not an alternative country you disappear into. It is godliness and honesty *under* an authority God has set up, including when that authority is not Israel and did not ask your permission.

*In all lawful things* and *in the Lord* keep the other rail. Seeking the city's peace is not a blank check for the city's sins. You do not pray Babylon into your bones. You pray for it, you seek its *shalom,* and you still know -- verse 10 -- that this city is not the last one.

@quote Calvin, Institutes IV.20 | /institutes/b4-c20.html
There is in Jeremiah another command in which the Lord thus orders his people—“Seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the Lord for it: for in the peace thereof shall ye have peace” ( Jer. 29:7 ). Here the Israelites, plundered of all their property, torn from their homes, driven into exile, thrown into miserable bondage, are ordered to pray for the prosperity of the victor, not as we are elsewhere ordered to pray for our persecutors, but that his kingdom may be preserved in safety and tranquillity, that they too may live prosperously under him.
@endquote

Calvin will not let you file this under optional kindness. Plundered, torn, driven, thrown -- and still ordered to pray that the victor's kingdom be preserved. The verse is that sharp. If it only binds people whose city is already friendly, it was never this verse.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Name your Babylon without romance and without contempt. It is the actual place you have been put: the town, the shop, the jurisdiction, the neighbors who do not share your creed. Seek its *shalom.* That will look like honest work, paying what you owe, refusing to enjoy a collapse that would take widows with it, and praying for officials you did not vote for. If your Christianity has no civic content except complaint, you are living the opposite of this letter.

Then pray it out loud, because the verse will not let seeking float free of petition. Pray for the city's peace as a man who will share in that peace, not as a critic standing off to the side. 1 Timothy will say the same thing with kings in the chair. You already knew the text. Jeremiah told you why: in the peace thereof shall ye have peace. Your household's quiet is not a separate miracle. It is downstream of whether the place is kept from coming apart.

Do not use verse 11 to dodge verse 7. Thoughts of peace, an expected end -- those are God's, on His timetable, after seventy years, after gardens, after prayers for a pagan city. The false prophets were the ones offering a better end on a shorter clock. If you will not seek the peace of the place where you actually live, you are not waiting on the promise. You are waiting on an exit, and the letter calls that a dream you caused to be dreamed.

And keep the limit. Seventy years, then return. Seeking a city's peace does not mean the city gets your final loyalty. Plant the garden. Do not plant your hope. The God who caused the carrying is the God who will gather. Until He does, the next faithfulness is small, local, and for people who may never thank you: houses, children, a prayed-for city, and a refusal to diminish there.
