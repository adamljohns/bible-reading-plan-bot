The verse everyone quotes for friendship, and almost always with the friction sanded off. Iron does not sharpen iron by sitting in the same drawer.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

The picture is a workshop, not a living room. Two pieces of the same hard metal, pressed and drawn against each other until an edge appears. Solomon does not say that iron *encourages* iron, or *affirms* iron, or keeps iron company. He says it *sharpens*. Something is being removed. Heat is involved. The face of the friend is different when the work is done.

Keep the word the King James and the World English Bible both kept: *countenance*. The sharpening is not an invisible interior event. It shows. A man who has been properly met looks different than a man who has only been liked.

Note also the scale. One man. His friend. Not a crowd, not a platform, not a feed of voices. The proverb is small on purpose. Two pieces of iron. That is the whole apparatus.

@word H1270 | iron
*Barzel* is the hard metal of tools, weapons, and shackles -- the material that changed what a people could cut and what they could not. In Israel's story it is never a soft word. Egypt was an iron furnace. A neck can be iron. A yoke can be iron. So the friendship in this verse is not being compared to oil, or to bread, or to a bandage. It is being compared to the metal you use when you need an edge that will last. Two soft things can polish each other and still have no point. This verse will not let you stop at polish.

@word H2300 | sharpeneth
*Chadad* is to be sharp, to be keen -- blade, point, tongue. The lexicon's own range is honest: a sharpened sword in a soldier's hand is one thing, a sharpened tongue in a wicked mouth is another. The verb does not decide whether the edge will be used well. It only tells you that an edge is being made. Which is why this proverb cannot be sentimental. A dull friend leaves you comfortable and useless. A real one costs you metal.

The verb is doing the same work on both sides of the couplet. Iron *chadad* iron. A man *chadad* the face of his friend. The second half is not a looser metaphor. It is the same action, named twice so you cannot miss the correspondence.

@word H376 | man
*Ish* is the particular man, not the species. Hebrew has *adam* when it wants humanity in the lump. Here it is one male person, standing in relationship. The proverb will not let you hide inside a group. The work is done man to man, the way a blade is laid to a blade and not to a pile of scrap.

That is also why the verse cannot be farmed out to a program. A church can host this. It cannot replace it. Iron is not sharpened by being stored with other iron. It is sharpened by contact under pressure.

@word H6440 | countenance
*Panim* is face, presence, the front of a man. It is always plural in form, which is a grammatical fact and not a sermon, but it does mean that what gets sharpened here is the *presented* self -- the part of you other people actually meet. Modern ears hear "character" and think of a private interior. Solomon pointed at the face. The friend is not sanding your hidden life for later. He is putting an edge on the man who walks into the room.

That is why verse 19 sits two lines later: as in water face answers to face, so the heart of man to man. Chapter 27 is not finished with faces. The countenance in 17 and the reflection in 19 are doing the same work from two sides. You find out what you are when another man is willing to look at you and not flatter what he sees.

@word H7453 | friend
*Rea* is friend, neighbor, companion -- the same word that stands in "love your neighbor as yourself." It is wider than the English word *friend* and narrower than a brand. Proverbs uses it for the man who wounds faithfully and for the man who sticks closer than a brother, and also for the false companion who kisses and leaves. The word itself does not guarantee quality. The rest of this chapter has already told you how to tell the difference: open rebuke is better than hidden love, and faithful are the wounds of a friend.

So the *rea* in this verse is not the man who makes you feel accompanied. He is the man whose presence puts a point on you. If your friendships have never drawn blood in the proverb's sense -- never named a lie, never refused a vanity, never made the next right thing sharper and more costly -- you have companions. You do not yet have this verse.

@section 2. Where It Sits

Proverbs 25 opens a new collection: the proverbs of Solomon which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out. Chapters 25-29 are not a father's long speech the way chapters 1-9 are. They are short sayings, clustered more tightly than they first appear. Chapter 27 is a friendship chapter before it is anything else.

Start a few verses up. Open rebuke is better than secret love. Faithful are the wounds of a friend; the kisses of an enemy are deceitful. The sweetness of a man's friend by hearty counsel. Do not forsake your own friend or your father's friend. Then, almost as a warning against the cheap version: he who blesses his friend with a loud voice, rising early in the morning, it shall be counted a curse to him. Noise is not this work. Volume in the morning is not iron.

Only after that does Solomon give you the workshop image. Iron sharpens iron. Then, immediately: as in water face answers to face. The chapter is teaching you how men make each other, for good and for ill, and it will not let you confuse encouragement with formation.

Read verse 17 as if verses 5 and 6 had already been cut, and you will get the poster. Read it where it sits, and the poster dies. The friend who sharpens you is the same kind of friend whose wounds are faithful. The metal has to meet metal. Hidden love leaves you dull and calling it peace.

The proverb is also doing work the New Testament will later name outright. Evil companionships corrupt good morals -- the same physics, run in reverse. And the church is told to consider how to provoke one another to love and good works. Proverbs got there with a blacksmith's picture and no further commentary. The later writers did not replace it. They assumed you already knew what iron does to iron.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed reading refused to treat this as a technique for self-improvement. A man is not a tool who sharpens himself by selecting the right peers, as if the church were a market of useful contacts. The tradition put the verse under the communion of saints: gifts are not private property, and a Christian who will not be met by another Christian is not protecting his freedom. He is refusing one of the means God actually uses.

Calvin's argument in the Institutes is not about friendship as a mood. It is about infirmity. Left to himself, a man does not stay sharp. Ignorance, sloth, and the vanity of the mind all run the same direction -- toward dullness that still feels like insight. God therefore added *helps*: the church, the preached word, the communion of those united to Christ. The point is not that friends are nice to have. The point is that God does not ordinarily sanctify a man in a locked room.

The tradition also kept the friction. Mutual edification in the Reformed churches was never a synonym for mutual admiration. It included rebuke, the ordinary ministry of the word, and the slow pressure of people who know your name and are not impressed by your excuses. A community that cannot wound you faithfully cannot sharpen you either. It can only polish you, and polish is how a blade looks when it has already lost its edge.

They likewise refused the careerist reading that treats other men as whetstones for your advancement. The countenance in the verse is not your reputation. It is your face. The question is not whether you are better positioned. It is whether you are keener -- more precise, more honest, less able to carry a lie in comfort. If a friendship has made you more impressive and less true, it has used the wrong metal.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 27 — Of the Communion of Saints | /lbcf/chapter-27.html
All saints who are united to Jesus Christ their head by his Spirit and by faith — although not thereby made one person with him — have fellowship in his graces, sufferings, death, resurrection, and glory. And being united to one another in love, they have communion in each other's gifts and graces, and are bound to the performance of such duties, public and private, as in an orderly way conduce to their mutual good, both in the inward and outward man.
@endquote

*Communion in each other's gifts and graces* is the doctrine underneath the proverb. The edge on your friend's life is not his private store. It is, in the confession's sense, already bound up with yours. You do not get to admire it from a distance and call that fellowship. You are bound to the duties -- public and private -- that actually do each other good. Iron that never touches iron is still iron. It is not this verse.

The confession is careful about the union. United to Christ, not made one person with him. United to one another in love, not fused into a blob with no names. The proverb is just as careful. Two pieces. Each remains itself. The contact is what makes the difference.

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 26 — Of the Church | /lbcf/chapter-26.html
Those thus called he commands to walk together in particular societies or churches, for their mutual edification and for the due performance of that public worship which he requires of them in the world.
@endquote

*Walk together* is the church's version of the workshop. Not a network. Not an audience. Particular societies, because the sharpening Solomon described does not happen in the abstract. It happens in a room, over years, with people who can see your countenance change. Mutual edification is not a feeling the group has about itself. It is the work Christ commands when he calls a people out of the world and then refuses to leave them alone in it.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Name the man. If you cannot, this verse has not yet started. A feed of voices is not iron. A room full of pleasant men who will not touch the dull place in you is not iron. You need one *ish* who is hard enough to meet you, and you need to be hard enough to be met. Soft on soft produces no edge. It produces a story about how close you all are.

Then tell the truth about what sharpening costs. You will lose some metal. A faithful friend will take off the burr you were using as a personality -- the sarcasm that keeps people from getting close, the competence that never asks, the private arrangement you have with a sin you have renamed a temperament. If you only keep men who leave that intact, you are not protecting your soul. You are storing the knife in the block and calling it maintenance.

Be iron yourself. The proverb runs both ways. A man who only wants to be sharpened, never to sharpen, is asking his friends to do a work he will not return. That is not humility. It is a kind of laziness. Open rebuke, hearty counsel, a wound that is faithful -- those are things you owe, not only things you wait to receive. The countenance you are told to sharpen is *his*. Look at him. Say the thing. Stay in the room after you say it.

And do not confuse volume with this. Verse 14 is still on the page: the loud early blessing counted as a curse. Cheer is cheap. Presence that will not flinch when the truth would cost you the friendship -- that is the metal. If your words always land as praise and never as a file, you are kissing, and the chapter has already told you whose kisses those look like.

Carry the picture into the ordinary week. Two men, same hardness, regular contact, an actual edge. Not a retreat. Not a thread. The workshop is the walk after church, the table you keep, the man who has permission to say *that is not true* and does not have to send a meeting invite to do it. If you have no such man, the next obedience is not to admire the proverb. It is to become someone who can stand that close, and to give another man the same right to your face.
