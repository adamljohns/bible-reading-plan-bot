@deck
Every clause in this sentence exists to close a door. Paul is not describing salvation so much as sealing off every route by which a man might claim it.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

Read it as a series of shut doors. *By grace* -- so not by merit. *Through faith* -- so not by works. *And that not of yourselves* -- so not even the faith is your contribution. *It is the gift of God* -- so the whole thing arrives from outside.

Paul could have stopped after "by grace you have been saved." He kept going for three more clauses, each one anticipating an escape route. That tells you how strong the instinct is that he is fighting. Men will find a way to take credit for grace itself if you leave them a gap.

@word G5485
*Charis* is the New Testament's central word for unmerited favor. In ordinary Greek it meant charm, or a favor done for someone. The New Testament takes it and makes it the whole description of how God deals with sinners -- not on merit, not on potential, not on a projection of what you might become. Grace by definition cannot be responded to with a resume.

@word G4982
*Sozo* has real range -- to rescue, to heal, to preserve, to make whole. Note the tense Paul uses: *you have been saved*, a completed action with a standing result. Not you are being saved, not you will be if you hold out. Something happened, and it is still true.

@word G4102
*Pistis* is trust, reliance, firm conviction. And here is where the verse gets interesting, because faith is the one element in the sentence that a man actually does. You believe; God does not believe for you. Paul knows this, which is exactly why he adds the next clause.

@word G1435
*Doron* is a gift, and specifically the word used for an offering brought to God in the temple. There is a quiet irony in Paul's choice. The word for what men carry to the altar is used for what God hands to men. The direction of the offering has been reversed.

@h3 What "that not of yourselves" refers to
Greek scholars have argued about this for centuries, because the pronoun is neuter while both *grace* and *faith* are feminine. The most common conclusion is that Paul is pointing at the whole arrangement -- the entire salvation-by-grace-through-faith package -- rather than at one noun.

Which, for practical purposes, settles the matter more firmly than picking one word would. If the whole thing is not of yourselves, then neither the grace nor the faith nor the arrangement that joins them originated with you. Every candidate for the thing you contributed is inside the clause being denied.

@section 2. Where It Sits

Ephesians 2 opens with the bluntest description of the human condition in Paul: *you were dead in trespasses and sins*. Not sick, not struggling, not disadvantaged. Dead, walking according to the course of this world, by nature children of wrath.

Then verse 4: *But God, being rich in mercy.* Everything after that is what God did to corpses -- made us alive together with Christ, raised us up, seated us with Him.

That is the setting, and it decides the meaning of our verse. A dead man does not cooperate with his resurrection. The reason salvation cannot be of yourselves is not that God has a rule against it; it is that the condition described in verse 1 makes it impossible. Paul is not being austere. He is being consistent.

And do not stop at verse 9. Verse 10 is the other half: *we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them.* Works are excluded as the root and required as the fruit. The same paragraph that removes them from the foundation puts them in the building.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

This is the passage the Reformation was fought over, and the Reformers' concern was pastoral before it was polemical. If any part of salvation rests on the believer's contribution -- however small, however assisted -- then assurance becomes impossible, because a man can never know whether his part was sufficient.

The Reformed doctrine of effectual calling grew directly out of Ephesians 2. If men are dead, then the call that saves cannot be merely an invitation offered to a corpse; it must be a call that does something. That is why the confession's language about this verse is so physical.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 10 — Of Effectual Calling | /lbcf/chapter-10.html
This effectual call is of God's free and special grace alone, not from anything at all foreseen in man, nor from any power or agency in the creature. Man is wholly passive in it, being dead in sins and trespasses, until he is brought to life and renewed by the Holy Spirit; thereby he is enabled to answer this call and to embrace the grace offered and conveyed in it, and that by no less power than that which raised up Christ from the dead.
@endquote

The confession cites this very verse, and it does something careful that is worth slowing down for. *Man is wholly passive in it* -- and then, immediately, *thereby he is enabled to answer this call and to embrace the grace.*

Passive in the raising. Active in the answering. The confession is not describing a man who is dragged unconscious into the kingdom; it is describing a man who could not respond, was made able to respond, and then genuinely responded. That is precisely Paul's structure: faith is real and it is yours, and it is not from you.

*By no less power than that which raised up Christ from the dead* is the phrase to keep. Whatever happened when you believed was resurrection-grade.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

The practical test of whether you believe this is what happens to your standing when you have a bad month. If a poor stretch makes you feel less saved, you have quietly relocated the foundation from grace to performance -- and the verse's tense is your answer: *have been saved*, done, standing.

There is also a subtler version, and it is the one that catches serious men. Not taking credit for works, but taking credit for *faith* -- a private sense that you saw it, you responded, you were spiritually perceptive enough to believe when others did not. Paul closed that door too, and it is the reason he added the third clause.

What is left, when every door is shut, is gratitude and work -- gratitude because nothing was earned, work because verse 10 says you were made for it. Those two are not in tension. A man who knows the house was given to him is the freest man alive to spend his life maintaining it.
