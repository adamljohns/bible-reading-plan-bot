The same book that spent two chapters on the Day of the LORD ends with God singing. The remnant in the middle of that turn is not the proud. It is the poor who trust His name.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

The verse is five claims, and none of them is an assignment for you. The LORD your God is in the midst. He is mighty. He will save. He will rejoice over you with joy. He will rest in His love. He will joy over you with singing. You are the object of every verb after the first. If you reverse that, you will try to generate the song, and you will miss that the singer is a warrior who has already taken the field.

The King James margin on *rest* is the honest difficulty: *Heb. be silent.* WEB says He will calm you in His love. Both public-domain lines are on the table. Hebrew *kharash* can sit still, or it can quiet someone. Either way the lawsuit-noise has stopped. Love here is not a mood layered onto a still-open case. It is love after the judgments have been taken away (verse 15).

@word H1368 | mighty
*Gibbor* is a mighty one, a warrior. David's heroes were *gibborim.* Isaiah's child is named Mighty God. Zephaniah puts the word on the LORD in the midst of a people who have just been told not to let their hands go slack. The one among them is not a chaplain. He is a fighter who *will save.* Sentiment that cannot survive this noun is not this verse. The singing later in the line is a warrior's singing, after a deliverance.

@word H3467 | save
*Yasha* is to save, deliver, rescue -- the root in Joshua, and in the name the New Testament will bring over as Jesus. Here it is future and sure: he *will* save. Verse 15 has already said the enemy is cast out and the King is in the midst. Verse 19 will say He will save her that halteth. The salvation in 17 is not a vague wellness. It is rescue, by the Mighty One, of a remnant that cannot rescue itself.

@word H2790 | rest
*Kharash* is to be silent, to hold one's peace -- and, in other contexts, to plow or engrave. The KJV's *rest in his love,* with the margin *be silent,* is the quieter of the two public-domain readings. A God silent in love is a God whose accusation has ended. He is not muttering leftover charges. He is done speaking against, and the love is the atmosphere of that silence.

WEB's *calm you* puts the quiet on the people. That is not a different religion. A warrior who has saved, rejoicing, can still a frightened remnant. What neither line will support is a love that is noisy with your performance. The silence, or the calming, is *in his love,* not in your improvement.

@word H160 | love
*Ahavah* is love as chosen attachment, the affection-word, not only the covenant-loyalty word. Zephaniah dares it of God toward this people after chapters of wrath. That is the shock. The Day of the LORD in chapter 1 is not cancelled by a nicer deity in chapter 3. The same LORD who promised to devour the earth with the fire of His jealousy now rests, or silences, in *ahavah.* Jealousy and this love are not two gods. They are one God, finished with the proud in the midst, and now attached to the poor remnant He left there.

@word H7440 | singing
*Rinnah* is a ringing cry -- shout of joy, and in other texts a cry of pain. Context decides. Here it is paired with *gil,* the spinning, exulting joy. He will joy over you with a ringing cry. The Maker of the remnant is not giving a polite nod. He is loud with delight. Luke will later say there is joy in heaven over one sinner who repents. Zephaniah said it first at remnant-scale: the LORD sings.

If that embarrasses you, good. It is supposed to. A man who can only imagine God as a commander will need this verse. A man who can only imagine God as a feeling will need the *gibbor* two clauses back.

@section 2. Where It Sits

Zephaniah is a short, hard book. Chapter 1: the Day of the LORD, a feast of sacrifice, Judah swept. Chapter 2: gather yourselves, seek meekness, it may be you shall be hid. Nations judged. Chapter 3 opens with woe on the filthy, oppressive city, her princes, her prophets, her priests. Then God in the midst as righteous judge. Then the turn.

Verse 11-12 name who survives the turn. I will take away out of the midst of thee them that rejoice in thy pride. I will leave in the midst of thee an afflicted and poor people, and they shall trust in the name of the LORD. Verse 13: the remnant shall not do iniquity. Verse 14: *now* sing, daughter of Zion. Verse 15: judgments taken away, enemy cast out, the King of Israel, the LORD, in the midst of thee. Verse 16: fear not; let not thine hands be slack. Then our verse.

So the God who sings is in the midst of the people He has just stripped of pride. He is not singing over the old Jerusalem as it was. He is singing over the afflicted remnant that trusts His name, after He has taken the haughty off the holy mountain. Read 3:17 as a blanket warmth for unrepentant religion and you have joined the people verse 11 removes.

The "in the midst" is already in verse 15 as King, and in verse 5 as the just LORD who will not do iniquity. Presence is not new in 17. What is new is the tone of the presence: warrior-salvation, joy, silence in love, singing. The remnant is told to sing in 14 because He will sing in 17. Their song is answering, not starting.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed reading would not let this verse float free of 3:11-12. Calvin takes those two verses as the Bible's own definition of the humble: not a moderate, well-mannered pride, but people lying afflicted by a knowledge of their poverty, trusting the name of the LORD. The proud *rejoice* in verse 11 -- same family of joy-words -- and they are removed. God's *rejoicing* in verse 17 is over a different company. That distinction is the difference between gospel and flattery.

They also held the two attributes together that the confession names in one breath: most loving, and most just and terrible in His judgments. Zephaniah is that sentence in narrative. If you need God to be only one of those, you will either not survive chapters 1-2, or you will not believe 3:17. The Mighty One who saves is the same one who would not clear the guilty on the old terms. The singing is what love sounds like on the far side of that justice, not a cancellation of it.

And they read the joy as God's, not as a project for your self-esteem. You are not told, first, to feel delightful. You are told that He delights. Hands had gone slack with fear (v. 16). The cure is not a lecture about self-worth. It is a warrior in the midst who will save, and a love that has stopped prosecuting.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote Calvin, Institutes III.12 | /institutes/b3-c12.html
When in Zephaniah the Lord speaks thus, “I will take away out of the midst of thee them that rejoice in thy pride, and thou shalt no more be haughty because of my holy mountain. I will also leave in the midst of thee an afflicted and poor people, and they shall trust in the name of the Lord,” ( Zeph. 3:11, 12 ), does he not plainly show who are the humble—viz. those who lie afflicted by a knowledge of their poverty?
@endquote

That is the "you" of verse 17. Not the haughty on the mountain. The poor who trust the name. If the singing lands on you as a man who still rejoices in his pride, you have not yet been the remnant. You have been the ones taken out of the midst. The way into the song is poverty known, and the name trusted -- not a better self-image.

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 12 — Of Adoption | /lbcf/chapter-12.html
They are pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened by him as by a Father, yet never cast off, but are sealed to the day of redemption, and inherit the promises as heirs of everlasting salvation.
@endquote

*Pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened -- yet never cast off.* That is *ahavah* with a spine. Zephaniah's remnant gets the warrior and the song, and they also got the stripping of pride two verses earlier. Adoption is not God becoming sentimental. It is Fatherhood: pity and protection and the refusal to cast off, after He has taken the haughty out. The quiet in His love is not the absence of fathering. It is the end of the lawsuit, and the beginning of the household.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

If your hands are slack, start at verse 16, not at the singing. Fear not. The Mighty One is in the midst, and He will save. Slack hands are what a man does when he thinks the next move is all his and he has nothing left. This verse says the warrior is already among you. Your job is to trust the name, like the poor remnant, and pick up what a saved man picks up. Fear that makes you idle is not humility. Verse 12's humility still trusts, and then verse 16 tells the hands to work.

Do not steal the song for an unrepentant life. The proud in this chapter also knew how to rejoice. They rejoiced in the wrong thing and were removed. The test of whether 3:17 is yours is not whether you like the idea of God singing. It is whether you have been reduced to the people He leaves: afflicted, poor, no deceitful tongue, trusting His name, not haughty because of the holy mountain. If that has not begun, you need chapter 2 -- seek meekness -- more than you need a framed 3:17.

Then let the silence in His love do its actual work. You may be waiting for God to keep talking as prosecutor, because that at least feels like you are still in the case. The margin says He is silent in love. The judgments are taken away. The King is in the midst. You may now work, and rest, without bargaining. That is harder than it sounds for a man who has lived on his own defense.

And if you are in Christ, the Mighty One who saves has a name you already know. The joy in heaven over a sinner who turns is this verse at one-soul scale. You are not told to make Him sing. You are told He does. Your answering song in verse 14 is obedience, not performance. Sing because He has cast out the enemy, not because you have finally become easy to delight in. He delights in His own work -- a remnant, a salvation, a people with His name on them -- and He is not embarrassed to be loud about it.
