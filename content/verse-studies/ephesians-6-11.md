Armor you put on, not armor you are. Every piece of it belongs to someone else, which is exactly why it works.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

Paul is writing from custody. There was a Roman soldier attached to him, and the temptation is to read this passage as a man idly describing the equipment in front of him. That is not where the imagery comes from. Every piece Paul lists in the verses that follow -- the breastplate of righteousness, the helmet of salvation -- is lifted out of Isaiah, where it is *God's* armor. Isaiah 59 describes the LORD putting on righteousness as a breastplate and a helmet of salvation on His head, arming Himself to come and save a people who could not save themselves.

That changes the verse. Paul is not telling you to assemble a kit of virtues. He is telling you to put on what God wore when He came to fight for you. The armor of God is a genitive of possession before it is a genitive of source. It is His, and He is lending it out.

@word G1746
*Endyo* is the ordinary word for getting dressed, and the New Testament makes it one of its great metaphors -- put on the new man, put on Christ, be clothed with humility. The point of a clothing verb is that the thing is external to you. You did not grow it and you cannot lose it by failing; you can only fail to wear it. A man who oversleeps is not less protected than a man who was never issued armor. He is a man with armor on the floor.

@word G2476
The objective of the whole passage, and it is smaller than we want it to be. *Histemi* is to stand, to hold position. Not to advance, not to conquer, not to take ground -- to still be there when it is over. Paul says it four times in this paragraph. The measure of a man in spiritual warfare is not the territory he took; it is that the position he was given is still held.

@word G1228
The enemy is named, and named as a slanderer rather than a monster. That matters for how you expect the attack to arrive. *Diabolos* work is mostly verbal and mostly indirect -- accusation, insinuation, the reframing of what is true into what is unbearable. Armor is the right image for a fight like that. You do not out-argue a slanderer; you make yourself unpierceable.

@h3 The word behind "wiles"
The Greek behind *wiles* is *methodeia* -- the word our "method" comes from. It means a scheme, a settled procedure, an approach used because it has worked before. That is a more useful picture of temptation than a random assault. You are not being attacked at random; you are being attacked along a line that has already worked on you, probably more than once. A man who knows his own methodeia knows where to reinforce.

@section 2. Where It Sits

This paragraph is the last thing in Ephesians, and everything before it has been about what God has already done. Three chapters of doctrine: you were dead, He made you alive; you were far off, He brought you near; there is one body, one Spirit, one Lord. Then three chapters of consequence: walk worthy, put off the old self, wives and husbands, children and parents, servants and masters.

Only then does Paul mention the devil. That order is deliberate and it is pastoral. He does not open the letter with the enemy; he opens it with the inheritance. By the time the armor appears, you already know who you are and whose you are, and the fight is described as defending something you have been given rather than earning something you lack.

Note also who is addressed. The armor is issued to the church, plural, standing together. English hides this -- every "you" in the passage is plural. Roman soldiers did not fight as individuals; the shield line worked because it overlapped. A man alone in this armor is wearing it wrong.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformers read this passage as the practical outworking of union with Christ, not as a separate discipline of spiritual combat. The armor is His; you are in Him; therefore you fight in what belongs to Him. That kept the doctrine from drifting in two dangerous directions at once -- toward a superstitious view of the devil, in which the Christian life becomes a series of confrontations with the dark, and toward a bare moralism in which the armor is just a memorable list of virtues to cultivate.

The confession's account of sanctification holds the same tension. The war is genuine and permanent in this life, and the strength for it is supplied rather than generated.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 13 — Of Sanctification | /lbcf/chapter-13.html
They who are united to Christ, effectually called, and regenerated, having a new heart, and a new spirit created in them, through the virtue of Christ's death and resurrection, are also farther sanctified, really and personally, through the same virtue, by his Word and Spirit dwelling in them.
@endquote

*Through the same virtue* is the phrase to catch. The power that raised Christ and the power that gets you through Tuesday are not two supplies. The confession will not let sanctification become a self-improvement project running on different fuel than justification.

That is the theology under Ephesians 6:11. The armor is not a second system bolted onto salvation for the advanced Christian. It is the same Christ, applied to the part of the day where you are under fire.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Put it on. That is the command, and it is a morning command. Armor is not useful at the moment of the ambush if it is still in the locker; the whole logic of the image is that the dressing happens before the fighting. Which in practice means the unglamorous things -- the Word before the phone, the prayer before the drive, the confession made on a Tuesday rather than after the collapse.

Then aim at standing. You will want a more impressive objective, and the passage will not give you one. Some seasons the entire assignment is that you are still married, still sober, still in church, still telling the truth, still there. That is not a small outcome. That is the stated objective.

And take the *methodeia* seriously. You know the approach that works on you. You know the hour of the day, the state of body, the specific lie. Stop being surprised by it. A man who is astonished every time by an attack that has come the same way for fifteen years is not fighting a clever enemy; he is refusing to look at a pattern.
