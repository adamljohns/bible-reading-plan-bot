A command about money answered with a promise about presence. The writer thinks those two things are the same subject, and he is right.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

The logic looks like a non sequitur until you see it. Stop loving money. Be content with what you have. *For* — and then a quotation about God never leaving you.

Why is that the reason? Because greed is rarely about greed. Money is the most available substitute for security, and a man reaching for more of it is usually reaching for a guarantee he does not have. The writer does not answer covetousness with a rebuke about materialism. He answers it with the only thing that actually competes: a guarantee.

@word G866
*Aphilargyros* is built out of three pieces — the negative *a-*, *philos* (loving), and *argyros* (silver). Not-money-loving. The King James renders the clause "let your conversation be without covetousness," and *conversation* in 1611 English meant your whole manner of life, not your talk. The word is about the shape of a life, not a wallet balance.

Note also it is not *money* that is condemned but the *loving* of it. Same distinction Paul makes to Timothy.

@word G714
*Arkeo* means to be sufficient, to be enough. It is the word Philip uses when he says eight months' wages would not be *enough* to feed the crowd. Contentment in this vocabulary is not a mood you work up; it is a judgment that what you have is sufficient — a conclusion about arithmetic, arrived at because of who is in the equation.

@word G2046
*Ereo* is the future form of *lego* — "he has said," a completed act of speech with standing force. The writer is not offering his own reassurance. He is producing a quotation, which is why the sentence has authority rather than warmth.

@h3 The double negative the Greek insists on
English cannot quite carry the last clause. The Greek stacks negatives — the World English Bible tries with *"I will in no way leave you, neither will I in any way forsake you."* There are five negative particles in the two clauses.

Greek does not treat a double negative as a cancellation the way English grammar teachers do; it treats it as emphasis. This is the strongest possible way to say never. The writer has reached for the most emphatic construction available and then used it twice.

@h3 Where the quotation comes from
It is not a single verse. The words are the promise God made to Joshua at the Jordan — *I will not fail you nor forsake you* — and before that to Israel through Moses in Deuteronomy 31, in the same commissioning scene.

That matters. The writer of Hebrews takes a promise made to a terrified man about to lead an invasion and applies it, without adjustment, to ordinary Christians worried about money. The promise was never about the size of the crisis. It was about the character of the promiser.

@section 2. Where It Sits

Hebrews 13 is the letter's landing: a run of short, practical instructions after twelve chapters of sustained argument about Christ's superiority.

Verse 1: let brotherly love continue. Verse 2: do not neglect hospitality to strangers. Verse 3: remember the prisoners, as bound with them. Then verse 4 on marriage. Then ours.

That list tells you the setting. These are people under pressure — some in prison, some who had their possessions plundered, as chapter 10 says they joyfully accepted. The temptation to grip money tightly is not abstract for a congregation that has already lost property for the name.

And the response in verse 6 completes the thought: *so that we boldly say, The Lord is my helper, and I will not fear what man shall do to me.* The chain runs greed → promise → boldness. A man who believes the promise has less to lose, and a man with less to lose is harder to frighten.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition treated contentment as a learned skill rather than a temperament, following Paul's *I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content.* That framing kept it from being an accusation against men who are anxious by nature; it is something acquired, usually slowly, usually through the loss of things one thought were necessary.

The tradition also read this verse as evidence that the doctrine of providence is intensely practical. A God who governs everything and has promised never to leave is the only rational ground for a man to stop hoarding. Take away the providence and hoarding is simply prudent.

And it consistently connected greed to idolatry, as Paul does explicitly in Colossians. That connection explains the structure of our verse. If covetousness is idolatry, then the cure is not discipline but a better God — which is exactly what the *for* clause supplies.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 5 — Of Divine Providence | /lbcf/chapter-05.html
God the good Creator of all things, in his infinite power and wisdom, does uphold, direct, dispose, and govern all creatures and things, from the greatest even to the least, by his most wise and holy providence.
@endquote

*From the greatest even to the least* is the clause that does the work on a household budget. A providence with a floor below which God does not attend would leave a man reasonably anxious about small things — and small things are what most financial fear is actually made of.

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 17 — Of the Perseverance of the Saints | /lbcf/chapter-17.html
Many storms and floods may rise and beat against them, but they shall never be torn from the foundation and rock to which faith has fastened them.
@endquote

And this is the confession's version of the double negative. Note again what it concedes — the storms come, the floods rise, and the confession does not promise otherwise any more than Hebrews promises prosperity. What it denies is the separation. *Never leave, never forsake* is a promise about company in the trouble, not exemption from it.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Test yourself on the diagnosis rather than the symptom. Almost nobody identifies as a lover of money. But run the honest question: what number, in the account, would make you feel safe? If there is a number, you have located the thing you are trusting, and the amount is not the issue — the trusting is.

Then notice what the writer does *not* prescribe. No budget, no giving target, no vow of simplicity. Those may all be wise. But his cure for greed is a promise, because he understands that you cannot discipline your way out of a security problem. You can only replace the security.

And take the quotation's origin as the encouragement it is. Those words were first spoken to a man staring at a river with fortified cities on the other side, and to a nation about to lose the only leader it had ever known. The writer of Hebrews looked at ordinary believers worried about money and judged that they needed the identical promise — not a smaller one scaled to a smaller crisis. Whatever your version of the Jordan is this month, the sentence you have been given is the one Joshua got.
