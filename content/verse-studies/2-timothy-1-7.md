@deck
Paul is not telling a frightened young pastor to cheer up. He is telling him where the fear came from, which is the only useful thing to say to a man who is afraid.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

The sentence is diagnostic before it is encouraging. *God did not give us a spirit of fear.* That is a statement about *origin*. Whatever this timidity is, Paul says, trace it back and you will not find God at the other end of it. He did not issue it.

Then the inventory of what He did issue: power, love, and a sound mind. Three things, and the third is the one nobody remembers, though it may be the one Timothy needed most.

Note also the *us*. Paul does not stand outside this and prescribe. He includes himself -- the man writing from a cell, who in a few verses will admit that many in Asia have deserted him.

@word G1325
*Didomi* is simply to give. But the tense matters: God *gave*, past, completed. Paul is pointing back to something already handed over, not to a resource Timothy might request if he prays hard enough. The Spirit was issued. The question is not supply; it is inventory.

@word G4151
*Pneuma* ranges across wind, breath, the human spirit, and the Holy Spirit — and translators split on which is meant here. Is it a disposition ("a spirit of timidity") or the Holy Spirit Himself, characterized by power and love? The Greek allows both and the difference is smaller than it looks: a disposition of power and love and self-control is not something Timothy generates, it is what the indwelling Spirit produces. Either way the source is outside him.

@word G1167
*Deilia* is the sharp word here and it is not neutral. It is not the ordinary word for fear (*phobos*); it is cowardice — timidity that makes a man shrink back from a duty he knows is his. That is a much narrower charge and a much fairer one. Paul is not telling Timothy never to feel afraid. He is naming the specific thing where fear turns into retreat from a post.

@word G1411
*Dynamis* — power, ability, might. It heads the list, and in Paul it is almost always God's power operating rather than a quality a man possesses. Which is why the verse is not a pep talk: the antidote to shrinking back is not self-confidence but the actual working power of God in a weak man.

@word G26
Second in Paul's list, and its placement is deliberate. *Agape* is love that originates in the lover rather than in the worth of the beloved. Power without love produces a bully — and Timothy's problem was never that he might become domineering. The pairing is Paul's guard on how the power gets used.

@word G4995
The third, and nearly untranslatable. The King James has "a sound mind," modern versions "self-control" or "self-discipline." *Sophronismos* carries the sense of a mind soberly ordered — the opposite of a mind running away with itself.

Which is exactly what fear does. Timidity is not only a failure of nerve; it is a disordering of judgment, in which the threat swells and everything true shrinks. What Paul says was issued against it is a mind put back in proportion.

@section 2. Where It Sits

This is the last letter Paul wrote, and the tone is unmistakable from the opening. He longs to see Timothy, remembering his tears. He recalls the faith that lived first in Timothy's grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice.

Then verse 6, immediately before ours: *stir up the gift of God which is in you through the laying on of my hands.* The fire needs fanning. Something in Timothy had banked down.

And immediately after, verse 8: *do not be ashamed of the testimony of our Lord, nor of me his prisoner.* There is the actual pressure. Paul is in chains, association is costly, and a younger man in Ephesus has every practical reason to keep his distance.

So the fear in this verse is not abstract anxiety. It is the specific temptation to go quiet because your mentor is a convict and standing with him has a price. Verse 7 sits between "stir it up" and "don't be ashamed" — which is where it does its work.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition read this verse as a statement about the Spirit's work rather than about personality, and that distinction has done a great deal of pastoral good. Timidity is often temperamental. Some men are simply built more cautious, and the tradition never treated that as sin.

What Paul names is narrower: the point where temperament becomes desertion of a duty. And the answer offered is not a change of temperament but a supply — the same Spirit who was given at the beginning, still given. A quiet man full of the Spirit is not less equipped than a bold one.

The tradition also noticed the trio and refused to break it up. Power alone makes a tyrant, love alone a sentimentalist, self-control alone a stoic. They are issued together.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 12 — Of Adoption | /lbcf/chapter-12.html
They have his name put upon them, receive the Spirit of adoption, have access to the throne of grace with boldness, and are enabled to cry, Abba, Father. They are pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened by him as by a Father, yet never cast off, but are sealed to the day of redemption.
@endquote

The confession puts *the Spirit of adoption* against exactly what Paul denies. Paul says the spirit you received was not one of cowering; the confession says what it was instead — the Spirit by which a man calls God Father and comes to the throne with boldness.

And the phrase that answers a frightened man most directly is *yet never cast off*. Fear of the kind Paul names usually runs on a suspicion that you are one failure away from being dropped. The confession grants the chastening and denies the casting off, in the same breath.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Use the verse as a source check. When you find yourself shrinking back from something you know is yours to do — the conversation, the stand, the association that will cost you — Paul's first move is not to shame you but to ask where the impulse came from. God did not issue it. That does not make it disappear, but it does strip it of authority. You are not obeying orders when you go quiet.

Then take *sophronismos* seriously, because it is the practical one. Fear is a disordering of judgment: the risk looks enormous, the cost of speaking looks final, and your own resources look like nothing. A sound mind is not the absence of fear but the restoration of proportion. Say the thing out loud to another believer and watch the size of it change. That is the sober mind coming back online.

And notice what Paul asked of Timothy in the verse before: *stir up* what is already there. Not acquire, not request, not wait for. The fire is banked, not out. Most men in this position are waiting to feel equipped before they act, and the sequence in this letter runs the other way — fan it, then go, on the grounds that it was given a long time ago and has not been withdrawn.
