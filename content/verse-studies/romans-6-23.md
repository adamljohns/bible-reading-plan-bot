Two nouns doing all the work. One is earned, one cannot be. Paul chose both words on purpose, and the contrast is the gospel in miniature.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

The sentence is built as a balance that refuses to balance. Wages against gift. Death against eternal life. Sin against God. The first half is a transaction; the second half is not, and Paul deliberately breaks the parallel to make the point.

If he had wanted symmetry he would have written *the wages of sin is death, but the wages of righteousness is life*. That would be a religion, and it would be a familiar one. Instead the second half changes the category. Nobody is paid eternal life. It is handed over.

@word G3800
*Opsonion* is not a general word for pay. It is the soldier's ration and stipend -- the provisions and coin issued to a man in the ranks. That is a precise and unsettling image. Sin is not depicted here as a master who cheats you; it is depicted as a paymaster who pays exactly, on time, what was earned. Death is not sin's betrayal of its servants. It is sin's honest wage.

@word G2288
*Thanatos* covers more than the end of biological life -- physical, spiritual, and eternal death together. In Romans it is the reign Paul has been describing since chapter 5: death entered through one man and ruled. So the wage is not merely a future event but a present regime.

@word G5486
*Charisma* -- a grace-thing, from *charis*, grace. Not a wage, not a bonus, not a settlement. The word has the gratuity built into its root, which is why English translations reach for "free gift" even though "free" is not a separate word. It is a gift by definition; saying "earned charisma" would be nonsense in Greek.

@word G166
*Aionios* belongs to the age to come. Worth noting because "eternal life" is usually heard as duration -- life that goes on. The word is more about *quality and realm* than length: the life proper to God's age, breaking into this one. Which is why John can say believers already have it.

@word G2222
*Zoe*, not *bios*. Not biological existence or livelihood, but life as a kind of thing -- the life of God imparted. Paul is not offering more of what you already have. He is offering a different order of it.

@h3 "In Christ Jesus our Lord"
The last phrase is not a devotional flourish, it is the location clause. The gift is not deposited into a person's general account; it is held in a place, and the place is Christ. That is the same structure as Romans 8:1 -- *no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus* -- and it is the reason Paul can be so absolute in both directions. Outside, wages. Inside, gift.

@section 2. Where It Sits

Romans 6 answers an objection Paul knew was coming. He has just said that where sin abounded, grace abounded much more. The obvious retort is: then let us sin more, so grace can increase. Paul's answer opens the chapter -- God forbid; how shall we who died to sin live any longer in it?

The chapter's argument is that a Christian has changed masters, and it uses slavery as its controlling image: you were slaves of sin, you have become slaves of righteousness. Freedom in Romans 6 is never freedom from all masters; it is being owned by the right one.

Verse 23 is the summary of that argument, and it must be read as the end of the chapter about slavery rather than as a standalone gospel tract. The wage is what the old master pays. The gift is what the new one gives. And the point of the whole chapter is that a man who has been given the gift does not go back to work for the paymaster who settles in corpses.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition has always treated this verse as one of the clearest statements that justice and mercy are not in competition. The wage is genuinely owed -- God is not indulgent about sin, and the verse says so in the strongest terms. The gift is genuinely free. Both are true because the wage was paid, in full, by someone who did not owe it.

That is why the tradition resisted every attempt to make grace a lowered standard. Grace in Scripture is never God deciding the debt was smaller than it looked. It is God satisfying it Himself. A cheap grace and a cheap view of sin always arrive together.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 15 — Of Repentance unto Life and Salvation | /lbcf/chapter-15.html
Such is the provision which God has made through Christ in the covenant of grace for the preservation of believers unto salvation, that although there is no sin so small but it deserves damnation, yet there is no sin so great that it shall bring damnation on them that repent.
@endquote

The confession cites this very verse, and its two clauses map exactly onto Paul's two halves.

*No sin so small but it deserves damnation* is the wage. The confession will not let you grade sins into ones that earn death and ones that do not. The paymaster's rate is uniform.

*No sin so great that it shall bring damnation on them that repent* is the gift. And notice it does not say the great sin was minor after all. It says it will not bring damnation on a repentant man -- not because the wage was waived, but because it was collected elsewhere.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Get clear which side of the sentence you are living on, because most men live functionally on the first while formally believing the second. The tell is what you do after you fail. If your instinct is to work off the debt -- a stretch of discipline, a season of trying harder, a private penance nobody knows about -- you are back on the payroll. Wages are what you go earn. Gifts are what you receive.

And take the first half seriously enough to be honest about it. This verse is not sentimental about sin. It says the arrangement pays out in death, reliably, and it does not distinguish between the respectable sins and the disgraceful ones. Every man reading this has worked shifts for that employer. The reason the second half is good news is that the first half is true.

The practical form of believing this is simple and hard: stop negotiating. You cannot get a better rate from sin, and you cannot get a better deal from God than free. The only thing left to do with a gift is take it, and the only thing that keeps a man from taking it is usually his preference for having earned something.
