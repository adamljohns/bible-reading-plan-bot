Peter had watched a man be sifted. He is writing to people who will be, and the first order he gives is to stay awake.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

Two imperatives, then a reason. Be sober. Be watchful. *Because* -- and then the picture. Peter does not lead with the lion. He leads with the posture, and the lion is the argument for it.

That order is worth defending, because we naturally reverse it. Told there is a predator, a man's instinct is to arm himself or to panic. Peter's instruction is neither. It is to be clearheaded and awake -- the two states a predator most dislikes in prey, and the two states most easily lost by a Christian who is comfortable, exhausted, or slightly numbed.

@word G3525
*Nepho* is to be un-intoxicated. Literally sober, metaphorically clearheaded -- not dulled, not stupefied, not operating at three-quarter perception. The metaphor should not be softened. Peter is naming a specific vulnerability: the man whose judgment is fogged does not see the approach. Most of us are not drunk. Most of us are dulled, which produces the same blind spot by a slower road.

@word G1127
*Gregoreo* is the sentry's word -- awake, alert, on guard. It is the verb Jesus used in Gethsemane when He asked three men to watch with Him and came back to find them asleep. Peter was one of them. He is not writing this from theory.

@word G476
This is the sharpest word in the verse and it is usually flattened to "enemy." *Antidikos* is a legal term: *anti* (against) plus *dike* (justice, lawsuit). It is your opponent in court -- the party arguing the case against you. That tells you the shape of the attack. Not primarily a monster in the dark, but a prosecutor with a file, and the file is not fabricated.

@word G2666
*Katapino* is *kata* (down) plus *pino* (to drink) -- to swallow down completely, to engulf. Not to wound, not to harass. The stated aim is total. Peter is not describing an inconvenience to be managed but an appetite to be escaped.

@h3 A lion that roars
Worth noticing what a roaring lion actually is. A hunting lion is silent; the roar comes before or after, to terrify, to scatter a herd, to drive prey toward something. Peter says the devil goes about *as* a roaring lion -- the simile is about noise and terror as much as teeth. Much of what frightens a Christian is sound designed to move him, and a man who is sober and awake is much harder to stampede.

@section 2. Where It Sits

1 Peter is written to Christians scattered across Asia Minor and under real social pressure -- slandered, excluded, in some places worse. The letter's whole burden is how to suffer well without becoming bitter or ashamed.

Chapter 5 begins with instructions to elders: shepherd the flock, not for dishonest gain, not lording it over them. Then to the younger: be subject. Then to all: clothe yourselves with humility, because God opposes the proud and gives grace to the humble. Then verse 6, humble yourselves under the mighty hand of God. Then verse 7 -- and this is the sentence directly before ours -- casting all your anxiety on him, because he cares for you.

That is the immediate context, and it changes the tone. Verse 7 is the tenderest sentence in the letter, and verse 8 is the most alarming. They are one paragraph. The man who has just thrown his anxiety onto God is told, in the next breath, to stay awake. Peter does not think comfort and vigilance are opposites.

Note also the very next verse: resist him, *steadfast in the faith*, knowing that the same sufferings are being experienced by your brothers throughout the world. The antidote to the lion includes the knowledge that you are not singled out.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition treated this verse with a particular restraint. The devil is real, personal, and malicious -- and he is a creature, permitted, bounded, and already defeated. That balance produced a spirituality that took the enemy seriously without becoming preoccupied with him.

The legal word *antidikos* fits that reading exactly. The accuser's power is not raw force; it is accusation, and accusation is answered in court by an advocate. The tradition's answer to Satan has never been to out-shout him but to point at Christ -- which is why the doctrine of justification does more practical work against the accuser than any technique.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 13 — Of Sanctification | /lbcf/chapter-13.html
This sanctification is throughout the whole man, yet imperfect in this life; there abide still some remnants of corruption in every part, from which arises a continual and irreconcilable war.
@endquote

*Continual* is why Peter commands a permanent posture rather than an occasional exercise. There is no season in which the sentry may stand down, because the confession will not promise you a season in which the war pauses.

And *remnants of corruption in every part* explains why the prosecutor's file is not empty. The accusation against a Christian is usually, in its particulars, true. That is what makes it frightening. The answer is not that you are innocent; it is that you are represented.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Take the sobriety command literally first, then metaphorically. There is a reason so many collapses begin with a man who was simply tired, drinking a little more, sleeping a little less, coasting. You do not have to be seduced to be devoured. You only have to be dulled.

Then handle the accusation correctly. When the file is opened on you -- and it will be, usually at night, usually accurate -- notice that the enemy's title is prosecutor, and that a prosecutor is answered by counsel, not by argument from the defendant. You are not required to litigate your own case. You are required to name what is true, agree with it, and point at the One who settled it.

And read verse 7 and verse 8 as the single paragraph Peter wrote. Cast your anxiety on Him *and* stay awake. A man who only does the first becomes soft and gets eaten. A man who only does the second becomes brittle and exhausted, defending a case that was closed before he took it up. Peter, who slept in the garden and was sifted by morning, put both in the same breath on purpose.
