@deck
Two commands, one order. Reverse them and you will spend your life losing fights you were never meant to pick alone.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

Almost everyone remembers the second half. Resist the devil and he will flee -- that is the part that gets stitched on things and shouted from platforms. Almost no one remembers that it is the back half of a sentence, and that James put something in front of it.

Submit yourselves therefore to God. *Then* resist. The order is the teaching. A man who tries the resisting without the submitting is a private who has decided to hold a position he was never assigned, with no supply line and no orders, and he will be overrun -- not because the enemy is impressive, but because he is out there alone.

@word G5293
A military word, and James means it that way. *Hupotasso* is to arrange under -- to take your place in a formation under a commander. It is not a synonym for going limp. Soldiers under orders are not passive; they are the most dangerous men on the field, precisely because their action is coordinated by someone with a view of the whole battle. Submission here is not the opposite of fighting. It is what makes fighting work.

@word G3767
The hinge, and it points backward. James has just quoted the proverb that God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble. *Therefore* -- because that is how God is oriented toward pride and humility -- submit. The command is not arbitrary; it is a response to the way things actually are. Fighting the devil from a posture of pride means you have God's opposition and Satan's attention at the same time.

@word G1228
The name is a job description. *Diabolos* is from *dia* (across) and *ballo* (to throw) -- the one who throws accusations across, the slanderer. Worth holding onto, because it tells you what most of his work sounds like. Not usually a temptation to spectacular evil. Usually a voice, running commentary, an accusation about who you are, delivered in your own voice so you will mistake it for self-knowledge.

@word G5343
The promise is not that he will be defeated by you; it is that he will *leave*. *Pheugo* is flight, retreat, breaking contact. It is the same verb the New Testament uses when it tells you to flee immorality -- an unheroic word, and a strategic one. James is describing a bully's response to a man who is visibly not alone.

@section 2. Where It Sits

James 4 opens with an ugly question: where do the fights among you come from? His answer is that they come from inside -- from desires at war within you. Then he escalates. You ask and do not receive because you ask wrongly. You are friends with the world, which makes you an enemy of God. He calls his readers adulteresses. This is not a gentle chapter.

So the command in verse 7 lands on people who have been diagnosed, not flattered. The submission being demanded is not a general posture of piety; it is a specific surrender of the desires James just exposed. And it is bracketed by grace on both sides -- the promise before it is that God gives grace to the humble, and the promise after it, two verses later, is that if you draw near to God He will draw near to you.

That is the paragraph. Diagnosis, surrender, resistance, nearness. Take any one of those out of sequence and you get a different religion: resistance alone is willpower, submission alone is fatalism, nearness alone is sentiment.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition has been careful with this verse for a specific reason: it can be read as though the Christian life were a fair fight between two comparable powers, with your effort as the tiebreaker. It is not. The devil in Scripture is a creature -- powerful, malicious, and on a leash. He flees not because you have out-muscled him but because you have moved to ground he cannot hold.

The old writers pressed the point that the war is real *and* asymmetric. The remnant of corruption in a believer means the conflict never fully stops in this life. That is not a defect in your conversion; it is the standard condition of a man being sanctified.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 13 — Of Sanctification | /lbcf/chapter-13.html
This sanctification is throughout the whole man, yet imperfect in this life; there abide still some remnants of corruption in every part, from which arises a continual and irreconcilable war.
@endquote

*Continual and irreconcilable* is the honest phrase. The confession refuses to promise you a truce. There is no arrangement to be reached with what remains in you, and no stage of maturity at which the fighting stops -- which means that losing a fight is not evidence that you were never in the army.

Hold that beside James. He does not say resist once and the devil is gone for good. He says resist and he will flee *from you* -- this time, from this position. Tomorrow you take the position again. A war that is continual is fought by men who show up continually, and the reason they can is verse 7's first clause: they are under orders, not out on their own.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Run the diagnostic in that order the next time you are losing. Not "how do I fight this harder," but "what have I not handed over?" Almost always there is something -- a decision you are keeping for yourself, a plan you have not submitted, a grievance you are still administering personally. The resistance goes limp because the submission was never done.

Then resist concretely. Resistance in Scripture is not a mood, it is speech and movement: saying no out loud, saying the true thing to another believer, putting the object out of reach, quoting the Word back the way Jesus did in the wilderness. The devil is not impressed by your intentions and cannot read your resolutions. He responds to a man who has taken a position under a commander and will not move off it.

And when he flees, expect him back. James promises retreat, not surrender. The man who thinks one victory ends the war is the man who stops posting a guard.
