@deck
The last thing Jesus says before He leaves. It is not a command, grammatically -- it is a forecast, and that is more demanding than an order.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

Read the grammar carefully, because it is often preached wrongly. There is no imperative in this verse. *You will receive power.* *You will be witnesses.* Both are futures, statements of fact about what is going to happen.

That is not a softening. A command can be disobeyed and the world stays as it was. A forecast from the risen Christ is a description of the future, and the only question is whether you are in it gladly. Jesus is not asking the eleven to undertake a program. He is telling them what they are about to become.

@word G1411
*Dynamis* -- power, ability, might. And note the sequence: power first, witness second. The order is the entire pastoral point. The disciples are not told to go and to be given help if they find it hard; they are told that something will arrive, and that testimony will follow from it. Verse 4 has already made this explicit -- do not depart from Jerusalem, but wait.

@word G4151
*Pneuma* covers wind, breath, spirit -- like Hebrew *ruach*, one word for the invisible thing that moves. It matters that the power promised is a Person and not a force. Everything in Acts that follows is the Spirit's initiative: He falls, He speaks, He forbids Paul from entering Bithynia, He sends Barnabas and Saul. The book is usually called the Acts of the Apostles; on the evidence it is the acts of the Spirit through them.

@word G1904
*Eperchomai* is *epi* (upon) plus *erchomai* (to come) -- to come upon, often suddenly and with overwhelming force. Luke uses the same verb in his Gospel for the angel's word to Mary: the Holy Spirit will come upon you. That echo is deliberate. What happened to bring Christ into the world is the same verb for what will happen to bring His body into the world.

@word G3144
*Martus* is a witness -- one who testifies from firsthand knowledge. It is also, notoriously, the root of "martyr," and the semantic drift happened for a reason: in the first centuries, testifying frequently ended in dying. That drift is already visible inside Acts. The first man Luke calls a *martus* after this promise is Stephen, and he says it while being stoned.

A witness is not a salesman and not an expert. A witness testifies to what he has seen, and is not responsible for the verdict.

@word G2078
*Eschatos* is the furthest extent, the last -- the root of "eschatology." The phrase *the uttermost part of the earth* is not a vague gesture at faraway places. It is lifted from Isaiah, where the Servant is given as a light to the nations, that salvation may reach the end of the earth. Jesus is telling a small group of Galileans standing on a hill outside Jerusalem that Isaiah's promise is now their itinerary.

@h3 The geography is the outline
Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, the ends of the earth. That is not a list; it is the table of contents of Acts. Chapters 1-7 are Jerusalem. Chapter 8 is Judea and Samaria. From chapter 13 the gospel moves out into the empire, and Luke ends the book with Paul in Rome, preaching without hindrance.

Note that Samaria is in the sequence, and note who is being told. These are Jewish men, and Samaritans were the neighbors they most despised. The promise routes the gospel deliberately through the one place on the map they would have preferred to skip.

@section 2. Where It Sits

This is the last recorded conversation before the ascension. The disciples have just asked their question -- *Lord, will you at this time restore the kingdom to Israel?* -- and it is a political question about timing and national restoration.

Jesus does not rebuke the hope, and He does not answer it. It is not for you to know the times or the seasons, He says, which the Father has put in His own authority. *But* -- and then verse 8.

That *but* is doing enormous work. They asked when the kingdom comes; He tells them what they will be doing while it does. The answer to the speculative question is a job. Then, immediately after, He is taken up, and two men in white ask the disciples why they are standing there looking at the sky.

The whole scene is a redirection: away from timetables, away from the heavens, out toward the map.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition read this verse as the charter of the church's mission and, crucially, as evidence that the mission runs on the Spirit rather than on human strategy. The Puritans and their heirs argued at length that no amount of eloquence converts anyone -- that the same sermon falls dead on one man and raises another, and the difference is not the preacher.

That conviction produced a particular kind of missionary: one who worked with enormous diligence and expected nothing from the diligence. Both halves matter. A theology of the Spirit's sovereignty that produces passivity has misread Acts, where the men who most believed it walked the furthest.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 10 — Of Effectual Calling | /lbcf/chapter-10.html
This effectual call is of God's free and special grace alone, not from anything at all foreseen in man, nor from any power or agency in the creature. Man is wholly passive in it, being dead in sins and trespasses, until he is brought to life and renewed by the Holy Spirit.
@endquote

Put this beside Acts 1:8 and the pressure comes off the witness in exactly the right place.

If the calling is *of God's free and special grace alone*, then the outcome of your testimony was never your department. You are a *martus* -- you report what you have seen. Whether the dead man in the room is raised is the Spirit's work, and the confession says he is *wholly passive* in it. Nobody was ever argued into life.

That is not a reason to speak carelessly or rarely. It is the only thing that makes speaking survivable, because it means a man can testify without needing to win.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

The order in this verse rules out two familiar failures.

The first is going without waiting -- witness as technique, scripts and strategies and a nagging sense that if you were better at this, more would happen. Jesus told men who had walked with Him for three years and seen Him raised to sit still in Jerusalem until power came. If they needed to wait, the assumption that you can generate this is not humility.

The second is waiting without going. Power is promised *for* something, and the something is testimony. There is no reading of this verse in which the Spirit arrives to make you feel spiritually equipped in private.

And take the geography personally, because it is the practical part. Jerusalem is where you already are -- the house, the shop floor, the men who know you. Samaria is the place on your map you would rather route around: the relative you have written off, the neighbor whose politics you find contemptible. It is in the sentence, between your city and the world, and it is not optional. Most men who want to reach the ends of the earth have simply not looked hard at who is next door.
