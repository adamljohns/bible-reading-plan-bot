One sentence that rules out every way of pleasing God but one, and then tells you exactly what that one way believes.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

The verse is built like a lock. The first half shuts a door: without faith, it cannot be done. The second half hands you the key, and the key has two teeth. You must believe that God *is*, and you must believe that He *rewards*. Both, not one.

Notice that the writer does not say it is hard to please God without faith, or rare, or that it takes a special kind of man. He says it is impossible. That is not a pulpit exaggeration. It is a statement about what pleasing God is.

@word G102
*Adynatos* is a flat word: not able. There is no sliding scale in it. A man without faith may be honest, generous, brave and disciplined, and the writer does not deny any of that. He denies that any of it reaches God as pleasing. The barrier is not the size of the effort. It is the direction of it.

@word G2100
This is the same word the writer used one verse earlier of Enoch, who before he was taken had the testimony that he had been well pleasing to God. Verse 6 is the explanation of verse 5. Enoch pleased God; nobody pleases God without faith; therefore Enoch's walk was a walk of faith. The word carries delight, not mere approval. God is not putting up with the man. He is pleased with him.

@word G4334
*Proserchomai* is the drawing-near word of the whole letter. It is the word behind "let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace" (4:16), behind those who "come unto God" by Christ (7:25), and behind "let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith" (10:22). In Hebrews it is worship language: a man approaching God the way a priest approached the holy place. Verse 6 tells you what that man must carry with him when he comes.

@word G3406
This word appears once in the whole New Testament, here. Strong's renders it *remunerator* -- one who pays back. The writer could have said God is good, or kind, or generous. He chose a word from the world of wages. Faith does not only believe God exists. It believes He answers.

@word G1567
Not *seek* but *seek out*. The prefix intensifies the verb. This is not the man who glances toward heaven when the doctor calls. It is the man who goes looking and keeps looking. The reward is promised to that kind of seeking.

@section 2. Where It Sits

Hebrews 11 is usually read as a hall of fame. It is better read as an argument. Chapter 10 ends with a warning and a quotation: "Now the just shall live by faith: but if any man draw back, my soul shall have no pleasure in him." The writer is speaking to men tempted to drift back from Christ into something older and more visible. Chapter 11 is his answer. He opens with a definition -- faith is "the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen" -- and then he walks through the ancients one by one to prove that this is how God's people have always lived.

Abel comes first, then Enoch. Enoch is a strange witness. Genesis gives him a few lines: "Enoch walked with God, and he was not found, for God took him." No speech, no battle, no miracle on his part. The writer of Hebrews reads that walk and draws the conclusion of verse 6: a man who walked with God and pleased Him must have been a man of faith, because there is no other kind of man who pleases God.

Then he moves on to Noah, who built an ark for a flood no one had yet seen. That is the pattern of the whole chapter. "These all died in faith, not having received the promises," he says at verse 13. Every witness in the list staked his life on a God he could not see and a payment he had not yet received. That is exactly what verse 6 says faith believes: that He is, and that He rewards.

So the verse is not a stray proverb. It is the rule the writer pulls out of Enoch's life and then proves on everyone who comes after him.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin turns to this verse more than once, and he uses it for two very different jobs.

The first job is to close a door. In his chapter on the beginning of justification he argues that nothing a man does before he is reconciled to God can buy him favor, and he lands the argument on this verse:

@quote Calvin, Institutes III.14 | /institutes/b3-c14.html
Hence we easily perceive that every thing which man thinks, designs, and performs, before he is reconciled to God by faith, is cursed, and not only of no avail for justification, but merits certain damnation. And why do we talk of this as if it were doubtful, when it has already been proved by the testimony of an apostle, that "without faith it is impossible to please God?"
@endquote

That is hard reading, and it is meant to be. Calvin is not saying the unbeliever's good deeds are worthless to his neighbor. He is saying they cannot be presented to God as payment. The verse removes the ledger.

The second job is to open a door. In his chapter on prayer, Calvin reads the same verse as an invitation. He is explaining why the Lord's Prayer begins by naming the Father in heaven:

@quote Calvin, Institutes III.20 | /institutes/b3-c20.html
"He that cometh to God," says the Apostle, "must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him," ( Heb. 11:6 ). Here Christ makes both claims for his Father, first, that we place our faith in him; and, secondly, that we feel assured that our salvation is not neglected by him, inasmuch as he condescends to extend his providence to us.
@endquote

Put the two passages side by side and you have the whole verse. Faith shuts out every attempt to please God on your own account. And the same faith walks straight up to Him in prayer, expecting to be heard.

Calvin adds one more warning, against men who would turn "rewarder" into a wage scale for merit. Answering the schoolmen who used this very verse that way, he says:

@quote Calvin, Institutes III.11 | /institutes/b3-c11.html
They quote from an Apostle: "He that comes to God must believe that he is, and that he is the rewarder of them that diligently seek him," ( Heb. 11:6 ). But they observe not what the method of seeking is.
@endquote

The reward is real. But it is promised to seekers who come by faith, not to workers who come with an invoice.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

The Baptist Catechism puts this verse under its second question, before it has said a word about Christ or the law:

@quote The Baptist Catechism, Q2 | /catechism.html
Everyone ought to believe there is a God; and it is their great sin and folly who do not.
@endquote

That is the first tooth of the key: believe that He is. The 1689 Confession picks up the second tooth in its chapter on God, using the verse's own phrase among His attributes:

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 2 — Of God and of the Holy Trinity | /lbcf/chapter-02.html
most loving, gracious, merciful, longsuffering, abundant in goodness and truth, forgiving iniquity, transgression, and sin, the rewarder of those that diligently seek him; and withal most just and terrible in his judgments, hating all sin, and who will by no means clear the guilty.
@endquote

Note what sits on either side of *rewarder*. On one side, forgiveness. On the other, judgment that will by no means clear the guilty. The God who rewards seekers is not soft. He is the same God who judges. The reward is grace from a just God, not a tip from an indulgent one.

And the Confession draws the hard consequence in its chapter on good works, citing this verse:

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 16 — Of Good Works | /lbcf/chapter-16.html
Works done by unregenerate men, although for the matter of them they may be things which God commands, and of good use both to themselves and others; yet because they proceed not from a heart purified by faith, nor are done in a right manner according to the Word, nor to a right end, the glory of God, they are therefore sinful and cannot please God, nor make a man fit to receive grace from God; and yet their neglect of them is more sinful and displeasing to God.
@endquote

The last clause matters. The divines did not let a man off the hook because his works cannot please God. Neglecting them is worse. Faith does not cancel duty. It is what makes duty acceptable.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Most men who drift from God do not stop believing He exists. They stop believing He rewards. They keep the first tooth of the key and lose the second. They still say God is real. They have simply quit expecting Him to answer, so they quit seeking, and their prayers shrink to a few words at meals.

The verse will not let you split the key. Believing God is there but does not pay attention is not faith in the God of Hebrews 11. It is a polite form of unbelief.

So this verse costs you something. It costs the hope of pleasing God by being a decent man. Your work ethic, your marriage, your service record -- good things, all of them, and none of them a ticket. You come by faith in Christ or you do not come.

And it gives you something. It tells you that seeking God is never wasted motion. The man who gets up early to read, who prays when nothing seems to move, who keeps coming back to the Word through a dry season -- that man is doing exactly what the verse names, and God has bound Himself to be his rewarder. Enoch did not get a long chapter. He got a walk, and at the end of it God took him.

Practically: pray like a man who expects an answer. Read like a man looking for something, not checking a box. And when you are tempted to show God your record, remember the verse has already ruled it out, and be glad of it -- because the door it opens is wider than the one it shuts.
