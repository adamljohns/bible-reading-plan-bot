Paul takes a farm law and turns it on the heart. Do not get into harness with a man who is pulling for another master. The command is older than the church and wider than marriage, and it has edges on both sides.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

The King James Version spends four words, *unequally yoked together with*, on what Paul says in one verb. Everything in the verse hangs on that verb and on the five questions that follow it. Paul gives the command once and then argues for it with a string of pairs that cannot be made into partners: righteousness and lawlessness, light and darkness, and in the next two verses Christ and Belial, the believer and the infidel, the temple of God and idols.

Read the pairs closely and one thing stands out. None of them is a picture of two things standing near each other. Every one is a picture of two things sharing something.

@word G1096 | Be ye
The verb is the common word for becoming. With the negative in front of it, Paul forbids entering a condition: do not become men yoked this way. He does not say, do not sin once you are in the yoke. The command lands before the first furrow is cut.

That shapes every later question. If the yoke itself is the thing forbidden, a man cannot wait for the first wicked order to find out whether he should have been in harness.

@word G2086 | unequally yoked together
This is the word the verse is known by. It is built from *heteros*, different, and *zygos*, a yoke: to be yoked with one of another kind. The picture is two unlike animals under one wooden beam.

Think about what a yoke does. It joins two to one load. It sets both in one direction. It puts both under one driver. Neither animal sins by being what it is. The wrong is in the joining, and it is there before the team has moved a foot.

@word G571 | unbelievers
Paul names who stands on the other side of the beam. *Apistos* is the man without faith. In Corinth that meant the pagan neighbor, the idol-worshipper, the man at the temple feast. Paul uses the same word for the unbelieving husband or wife in 1 Corinthians 7 and for the host who invites a Christian to dinner in 1 Corinthians 10:27.

So the word does not mean a brother who differs on a lesser matter. It means one who does not believe on Christ. Verse 15 says it again as plainly as it can be said: *what part hath he that believeth with an infidel?*

@word G2842 | communion
*Koinonia* is holding something in common. It is the word Paul used in the first letter for the cup and the bread, the sharing of the Lord's table. Here he asks what share light has with darkness.

Across verses 14 to 16 he asks five such questions and uses a different word in each: fellowship, communion, concord, part, agreement. He is piling up the vocabulary of partnership. Light and darkness may be found on the same street. They cannot hold shares in the same undertaking.

@word G458 | unrighteousness
The King James has *unrighteousness*; the word is closer to lawlessness, life without God's law. Set against righteousness, it tells us what makes the two animals unlike. The ox and the ass differ in size and gait. The believer and the unbeliever differ in law, in whose word finally settles a matter. Under one yoke that difference does not stay private. It decides which way the team turns at the end of the row.

@section 2. Where It Sits

The paragraph runs from 6:14 to 7:1, and it sits inside an appeal. Paul has just told the Corinthians that his heart is wide open to them and theirs is cramped: *be ye also enlarged* (6:13). Then comes the command about the yoke. Then, in 7:2, he picks the appeal up again and asks them to make room for him. The order is not an accident. Hearts that had grown narrow toward the apostle had grown wide toward the idol world. Paul asks for both to be put right at once.

After the five questions he gives the ground: *for ye are the temple of the living God* (6:16). Then he strings together promises from the Old Testament. *I will walk among you, and will be your God, and ye shall be my people* is Leviticus 26:12, and Ezekiel 37:27 says the same of God's dwelling place among his people. *Touch no unclean thing* is Isaiah 52:11, spoken to exiles leaving Babylon with the vessels of the LORD in their hands. *I will be his father, and he shall be my son* is 2 Samuel 7:14, God's word about David's heir, which Paul widens to *sons and daughters*. Then 7:1 draws the conclusion: *Having therefore these promises, dearly beloved, let us cleanse ourselves.*

Mark the logic. The separation Paul commands does not rest on disgust at the neighbors. It rests on a presence and a promise. God lives among this people. He has called them sons. A temple is not shared.

Now the setting. Corinth was full of temples, and the temples had dining rooms. Trade guilds, family feasts and civic occasions met there, with the meat offered to the god first. Paul had already dealt with it in the first letter. He warned the man who would *sit at meat in the idol's temple* (1 Corinthians 8:10). He said that what the Gentiles sacrifice *they sacrifice to devils, and not to God*, and then: *Ye cannot drink the cup of the Lord, and the cup of devils* (1 Corinthians 10:20–21).

But the same letter fences the command on the other side, and this is the part men forget. In 1 Corinthians 5:9–10 Paul says he never meant them to avoid the fornicators and idolaters of this world, *for then must ye needs go out of the world*. In 10:27 he tells them to go to dinner at an unbeliever's house if they are asked and want to go. In 7:12 he tells the believer who already has an unbelieving wife to stay with her.

So Paul himself has marked two edges. He forbids the table of devils. He permits the neighbor's table. The yoke lies somewhere between, and the work of applying the verse is finding where.

The picture comes from the law. *Thou shalt not plow with an ox and an ass together* (Deuteronomy 22:10). That law stands among others of the same kind: no vineyard sown with mixed seed, no garment of linen and wool (Deuteronomy 22:9; Leviticus 19:19). In field and loom Israel was taught that God makes distinctions and expects his people to keep them. Paul takes the plow law and applies it to persons.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin, in his commentary on this verse, says that many take Paul to be speaking of marriage, and that the context shows they are mistaken. The fault at Corinth was the idol banquets, and the command is general. He is careful about what it does not forbid:

@quote Calvin, Commentary on 2 Corinthians 6:14 | https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom40.xii.ii.html
For one sun shines upon us, we eat of the same bread, we breathe the same air, and we cannot altogether refrain from intercourse with them; but Paul speaks of the yoke of impiety, that is, of participation in works, in which Christians cannot lawfully have fellowship.
@endquote

That is a narrower reading than some would like, and it should be heard. Calvin places the yoke in shared works, not in shared air. A man who says the verse does not forbid standing beside an unbeliever in a lawful task has Calvin with him so far.

But Calvin does not stop there. He goes on to say that marriage falls under the same rule, because it is a snare that draws a man into agreement with impiety. And he adds a sentence that reaches further than the act itself:

@quote Calvin, Commentary on 2 Corinthians 6:14 | https://www.ccel.org/ccel/calvin/calcom40.xii.ii.html
Hence, too, we infer, that even those that do not in their hearts approve of superstitions are, nevertheless, polluted by dissimulation if they do not openly and ingenuously stand aloof from them.
@endquote

A man may keep his heart clear and still be defiled by what his presence says. The inward reservation does not cancel the outward sign. That is Calvin's answer to the Corinthian who reclined in the temple and told himself an idol was nothing.

Matthew Henry reads the verse in three widening circles: stated relations that we choose, common friendship, and religious communion, which he says is forbidden most of all. Of the first he writes:

@quote Matthew Henry, Commentary on the Whole Bible, 2 Corinthians 6:14 | https://www.ccel.org/ccel/henry/mhc6.iiCor.vii.html
It is wrong for good people to join in affinity with the wicked and profane; these will draw different ways, and that will be galling and grievous.
@endquote

Henry sees the harm in the pull, before any crime. The two will draw different ways.

In the *Institutes* Calvin states the duty and guards it in one sentence. He is answering men who wanted to leave the church because sinners were in it:

@quote Calvin, Institutes IV.1 | /institutes/b4-c01.html
I do not deny that it is the duty of a godly man to withdraw from all private dealings with the wicked, and not to entangle himself with them by any voluntary tie; but it is one thing to shun the company of the wicked, and another to renounce the communion of the Church out of hatred for them.
@endquote

*Any voluntary tie* is the yoke. And the second half is a warning to the zealous. The same Calvin who forbids the tie says in the next section that a harsh spirit of separation comes more often from pride than from holiness. The witnesses hold both edges, as Paul did.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

The 1689 Confession takes up Paul's own phrase when it speaks of marriage:

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 25 — Of Marriage | /lbcf/chapter-25.html
It is lawful for all sorts of people to marry who are able with judgment to give their consent; yet it is the duty of Christians to marry in the Lord. Therefore those who profess the true religion should not marry with unbelievers or idolaters; neither should the godly be unequally yoked by marrying those who are wicked in their lives, or who maintain damnable heresy.
@endquote

Three things are worth seeing. The rule is stated as a duty and not as advice. It is grounded in Paul's words to the widow, *only in the Lord* (1 Corinthians 7:39). And the confession names three kinds of unequal match: the unbeliever or idolater, the man wicked in life, and the one who holds damnable heresy. Our fathers did not think a Christian name on the other party settled the matter. For the closest bond they looked at what was believed and how it was lived.

The confession brings the same passage to the Lord's table. Its chapter on the Supper cites 2 Corinthians 6:14–15 for this:

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 30 — Of the Lord's Supper | /lbcf/chapter-30.html
All ignorant and ungodly persons, as they are unfit to enjoy communion with Christ, so are they unworthy of the Lord's table, and cannot, without great sin against him, while they remain such, partake of these holy mysteries or be admitted to them.
@endquote

So the church's own fellowship is guarded by this verse. The table is the place where the yoke of Christ is shown, and it is not to be shared with unbelief.

On civil life the confession speaks differently:

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 24 — Of the Civil Magistrate | /lbcf/chapter-24.html
It is lawful for Christians to accept and execute the office of a magistrate when called to it.
@endquote

It says this of offices held in kingdoms and commonwealths that were never made up of saints alone. The confession does not treat civil service in a mixed nation as an unequal yoke. It guards the marriage bed and the Lord's table with Paul's verse, and it sends Christian men into public office. Any application of the verse to politics has to keep both of those in view.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Start with the question the verse forces. What makes a bond a yoke? The picture itself gives four marks.

A yoke sets a shared direction. It does not join two animals for one step. It binds them for the field. Ask whether the bond commits you to go where the other goes, past the one task you agreed on.

A yoke is seen. Two animals under one beam are one team to every man who looks over the fence. Ask whether you stand under a common name, so that what your partner says is heard as said by you.

A yoke has a driver. Ask who gives the orders. Can the other man, or a board he sits on, bind what you do or spend your name where you would not?

And among Christians a yoke may carry a banner. Ask whether the bond itself claims to speak for the faith. A thing done in Christ's name is under his rule in a way that a business deal is not.

Now weigh the ordinary cases. Buying milk from an unbeliever is an exchange, not a harness. No direction is shared, no name, no driver, no banner. Paul says so: to avoid such dealings you would have to leave the world. Working for an unbelieving employer is service under authority. Daniel served a pagan king and served him well. He did not choose a partnership with Babylon; he was carried there. And he drew his lines at the king's meat and the king's image (Daniel 1:8; 3:18). Citizenship is the same kind of thing. The exiles were told to *seek the peace of the city* (Jeremiah 29:7), and the confession sends Christians into the magistrate's office.

Then there is the harder case, the common cause. A believer and an unbeliever both want one just thing done, and they work for it side by side. Scripture has room for this. Abram went to war to rescue Lot, and the Amorite brothers Mamre, Eshcol and Aner *were confederate with Abram* (Genesis 14:13). He let them take their share of the spoil. In the council Paul sided with the Pharisees against the Sadducees on the resurrection (Acts 23:6). So the man who says a Christian may stand with an unbeliever on a single matter, so long as he is asked to do no sin, is saying something true, and Calvin's phrase *participation in works* is on his side.

But the same chapter of Genesis shows the line. When the king of Sodom offered Abram the goods, he refused *from a thread even to a shoelatchet*, and gave his reason: *lest thou shouldest say, I have made Abram rich* (Genesis 14:23). He would fight beside Amorites for a kinsman. He would not be bound to Sodom, or let Sodom put its name on what God had given him. No one had asked him to sin. He judged the bond by what it would say.

Jehoshaphat is the warning on the other side. He was a godly king who *joined affinity with Ahab* (2 Chronicles 18:1). The prophet met him afterward: *Shouldest thou help the ungodly, and love them that hate the LORD?* (19:2). Later he joined the next king of Israel in a shipping venture. The record names no idolatry demanded of him in it. It says only this: *Because thou hast joined thyself with Ahaziah, the LORD hath broken thy works* (20:37). The joining was the fault.

So the rule "only if it requires sin" is true as far as it goes and it does not go far enough. Paul forbids becoming yoked. He does not wait for the first sinful order. A bond can be unequal in its direction, its name, its driver or its banner before anyone has been asked to do a wrong thing. The right question has two parts. Has this asked me to sin? And what is this bond, and what does it say about whose I am?

Apply it in three places.

Marriage has all four marks at full strength: one direction for life, one name, one household government, one witness before the children. That is why the church has always put this verse here first. A believer is free to marry *only in the Lord*. But the believer who is already married to an unbeliever is told to stay (1 Corinthians 7:12). The verse forbids entering the yoke. It gives no leave to break a covenant already made.

The church is under Christ's banner by its nature. When the neighbors came to Zerubbabel and said, *Let us build with you: for we seek your God, as ye do*, the answer was, *Ye have nothing to do with us to build an house unto our God* (Ezra 4:2–3). It was not rudeness. The house was God's. Joint worship, a shared pulpit, a common mission, a joint statement of what the gospel is: these speak for Christ, and they cannot be shared with those who do not hold his gospel.

Civil and political alliances need the most care, because good men place them differently. A vote, a petition, a brief filed beside others, a coalition for one bill: in these each man answers for his own reasons and no one speaks for another's faith. That is common cause, and it is lawful. It becomes a yoke as the marks appear. The work takes a common religious name. Its leaders begin to speak for Christ on your behalf. Staying in requires you to keep quiet about the gospel, or about a partner's error. The bond stops being one task and becomes a standing union that goes wherever its leaders go.

The verse says *unbelievers*. Whether a man who professes Christ in another communion is one is not settled by this verse. It is settled by the gospel he confesses, and that question must be argued from Scripture on its own ground. What the verse does settle is that a Christian cannot sign a common confession with a man while believing that man's gospel is another gospel. He can stand beside him at the statehouse for the life of an unborn child and say plainly, when asked, where they differ. He cannot let the alliance say they are one in Christ if he does not believe it.

Use the test on yourself before you use it on your brother. Calvin's warning stands: the hunger to separate is more often pride than holiness. Be slow to call a man compromised for standing where you would not, and slow to call a man a schismatic for refusing a platform you would take.

And remember why the second yoke is refused. It is not that we are too clean for our neighbors. It is that we are already in harness. *Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me* (Matthew 11:29). A man can pull under one beam. Christ's is easy, and his burden is light, and the one who wears it is free to love every neighbor he has without belonging to any of them.
