Four chapters to settle the verdict. Then five verses on what it is like to live on the far side of it -- including the hard days.

@section 1. The Text and Its Words

These five verses move in a straight line, and the line runs through trouble rather than around it. Justified, therefore peace. Peace, and access. Access, and a standing place. A standing place, and a hope. Then Paul does what no motivational speaker would do: he puts tribulation in the middle of the chain and says it belongs there.

Read the verbs and you see the shape. "We have peace." "We have access." "We stand." These are settled things, stated as fact. Then "we rejoice," "we glory," "knowing." The first set is what God has done. The second is how a man lives once he knows it.

@word G1344
Everything hangs on this word, and it is a courtroom word. *Dikaioo* is not a process of becoming good. It is a verdict pronounced. Paul has spent the opening chapters proving that Jew and Gentile alike stand guilty, and then that God "justifieth the ungodly" (4:5) by faith. The participle here looks back on that verdict as done. You are not being justified a little more each week. You have been.

@word G1515
Not a feeling of calm, though calm may follow. Peace *with God* is a changed relationship: the war is over. Paul will say a few verses later that "when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son" (5:10). Enemies is the right word for what came before. Peace is what replaced it.

@word G4318
This word appears only three times in the New Testament: here, and twice in Ephesians, where Paul says that through Christ "we both have our access in one Spirit to the Father" (2:18) and have "boldness and access in confidence" (3:12). It is the word for being brought in -- admitted. The justified man is not left standing outside the door with a pardon in his hand. He is let in.

@word G2744 | rejoice / glory
This is the boast word. The King James renders it "rejoice" in verse 2 and "glory" in verse 3, but it is one Greek verb in both places, and again in verse 11. Paul has spent chapter 3 shutting down every human boast ("Where is boasting then? It is excluded"). Now he gives it back, aimed at a new target. A man may boast in the hope of God's glory. Then, stranger still, he may boast in his troubles.

@word G2347
Pressure. The word is used of things crushed under weight. Paul does not mean bad luck or a hard week. In his own life it meant beatings and prisons. He does not say believers escape it. He says they boast in it, and then he tells you why.

@word G5281
*Hupomone* is staying under the load. Not passive waiting, but holding your ground while the weight is on you. Tribulation produces it the way a load produces strength in a man who keeps carrying it.

@word G1382
The King James calls it "experience," and the old word fits better than it first looks. *Dokime* is proof by testing -- the metal that has been through the fire and come out still metal. It is character that has been tried and found real.

@word G2617
To be put to shame -- the shame of a man who trusted something that failed him in public. Paul says this hope will not do that to you. The ground for that promise is not your grip on the hope. It is the next clause: the love of God already poured into your heart.

@word G1632
Poured out. It is not a trickle, and it is not a measured ration. The love Paul has in mind is God's love for us, not our love for God, and he says the Holy Spirit has poured it in. The past tense matters. It is already in you.

@section 2. Where It Sits

"Therefore" reaches back over everything before it. In chapter 3 Paul proves that "all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God," and that a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the law. In chapter 4 he proves it from Abraham, who believed God and had it counted to him for righteousness, and he ends on Christ, "who was delivered for our offences, and was raised again for our justification."

Chapter 5 turns from the verdict to its fruit. Verses 1 to 5 are the first harvest: peace, access, hope, and a new way of reading suffering. Verses 6 to 11 then ground that hope in the cross: "while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us." If God did that for enemies, Paul argues, much more will He save those He has already reconciled. Then from verse 12 Paul widens the frame to Adam and Christ, the two men in whom the whole race stands or falls.

So this paragraph is the hinge between the doctrine of justification and the security of the justified. Notice where it ends: on the love of God. Paul started in chapter 1 with the wrath of God revealed from heaven. Five chapters later, the love of God is poured out in the heart. That is the distance the gospel covers.

@section 3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin saw that the peace in verse 1 and the love in verse 5 are bound together. In his chapter on what free justification secures, he writes:

@quote Calvin, Institutes III.13 | /institutes/b3-c13.html
Paul uniformly declares that the conscience can have no peace or quiet joy until it is held for certain that we are justified by faith. And he at the same time declares whence this certainty is derived—viz. when "the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost," ( Rom. 5:5 ); as if he had said that our Souls cannot have peace until we are fully assured that we are pleasing to God.
@endquote

That is the pastoral point of the whole paragraph. A man who thinks his standing depends on his performance can never have peace, because his performance is never finished. Peace comes only when the verdict is settled outside of him.

On the chain of verses 3 and 4, Calvin is just as practical. In his chapter on bearing the cross he explains how tribulation actually does its work:

@quote Calvin, Institutes III.8 | /institutes/b3-c08.html
This Paul teaches when he says that tribulation worketh patience, and patience experience. God having promised that he will be with believers in tribulation, they feel the truth of the promise; while supported by his hand, they endure patiently. This they could never do by their own strength. Patience, therefore, gives the saints an experimental proof that God in reality furnishes the aid which he has promised whenever there is need.
@endquote

Notice the mechanism. Trouble does not make a man better on its own; plenty of men are only made bitter by it. The trouble works because God's promise is tested inside it and holds. The "experience" of verse 4 is the man's own proof, earned under load, that God keeps His word. That is why it produces hope rather than cynicism.

@section 4. Confessional Anchor

The 1689 Confession builds its chapter on assurance with these verses in hand. It even borrows Paul's wording:

@quote 1689 LBCF, ch. 18 — Of the Assurance of Grace and Salvation | /lbcf/chapter-18.html
Although temporary believers and other unregenerate men may vainly deceive themselves with false hopes and carnal presumptions of being in the favor of God and in a state of salvation — whose hope shall perish — yet such as truly believe in the Lord Jesus, and love him in sincerity, endeavoring to walk before him in all good conscience, may in this life be certainly assured that they are in the state of grace, and may rejoice in the hope of the glory of God. Such hope shall never make them ashamed.
@endquote

The Confession sets two kinds of hope side by side. One is false and will perish. The other will never make a man ashamed. The difference is not how strongly a man feels it. It is what the hope rests on.

The Baptist Catechism lists the fruit of these verses in one plain sentence, with Romans 5:1-2 and 5 among its proof-texts:

@quote The Baptist Catechism, Q39 | /catechism.html
The benefits which in this life do accompany or flow from justification, adoption, and sanctification, are assurance of God's love, peace of conscience, joy in the Holy Spirit, increase of grace, and perseverance in it to the end.
@endquote

Read that list against the paragraph: assurance of God's love (verse 5), peace of conscience (verse 1), joy (verses 2 and 3), increase of grace (the chain of verses 3 and 4), perseverance (the standing of verse 2). The catechism did not invent its list. It read it off Romans 5.

@section 5. For the Man Reading This

Start with what you have. Peace with God is not a goal for the mature. It belongs to every man who has been justified by faith, on his first day as much as his last. If you are in Christ, the war is over. Stop living as though you are still negotiating terms.

Then deal with the hard part. Paul says we glory in tribulations, and most men hear that as a demand to fake a smile. It is not. Paul is not asking you to enjoy the pressure. He is telling you what the pressure is for. The layoff, the diagnosis, the son who will not speak to you, the long season where prayer feels like talking to a wall -- if you stay under it, holding on to the promise, it produces endurance. Endurance produces a man who has been tested and found real. That man has hope that will not embarrass him, because he has watched God keep His word under load.

This also tells you what not to do with suffering. Do not treat it as proof that God has turned against you. The verdict of verse 1 does not change when the bank account does. Do not treat it as a payment, either, as though suffering earns you something. It is training, not currency.

And when the hope runs thin, do not try to whip it up. Go back to verse 5. The ground of your hope is not how hard you are holding on. It is the love of God already poured into you by the Spirit. You did not pour it. You cannot drain it.

A man who carries these five verses has an answer for the two questions that wear men down: Where do I stand with God? And what is this trouble for? He stands at peace. The trouble is making something in him that will last.
