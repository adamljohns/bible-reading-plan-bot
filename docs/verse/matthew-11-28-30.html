Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 11:28-30

The invitation arrives after a woe and a hiding. Jesus does not first make Himself small. He claims the Father’s hand, and then tells the exhausted to come to Him.

“Come to me, all you who labor and are heavily burdened, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and humble in heart; and you will find rest for your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.”— World English Bible (public domain)

Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Three sentences, and they have to be kept together.

First: Come to me. Not to a program, not to a feeling, not to a cleaned-up version of yourself. The destination is a person. The people named are not the impressive ones from two verses earlier. They are the ones who labour and are heavy laden. The promise attached is rest, given, not achieved.

Second: Take my yoke and learn from me. Rest in this saying is not a hammock. A yoke is what you work under. The rest is found on the other side of getting under His government and becoming His student, and the reason you can bear that is the character of the teacher: meek and lowly in heart.

Third: the explanation. My yoke is easy, and my burden is light. He does not say there is no yoke. He says whose it is, and what it weighs compared with the one you are already carrying.

  • δεῦτε deute · “Come” · Strong’s G1205

    The Greek deute (plural imperative form of deuro) is an urgent invitation: 'Come!' or 'Come here!' — addressed to more than one person. It is the word Jesus uses for some of His most powerful invitations throughout the Gospels.

    Deute is a plural command: Come. Come here. It is urgent and it is addressed to a crowd, not to a private favourite. The same mouth that has just pronounced woe on cities is now calling the worn-out. The invitation is not a change of mood. It is the next sentence of the same authority.

  • κοπιάω kopiao · “ye that labour” · Strong’s G2872

    From kopos ('labor/fatigue'). Kopiao means to work to the point of exhaustion — hard, sustained, wearying toil.

    Kopiao is work that has gone on until the man is spent — farm labour, ministry labour, the kind of toil that leaves you empty. Jesus is not recruiting the bored. He is naming people who have already been working, and working at something that has not given them what it promised. Religion can do that. So can a job. So can the project of holding your own life together.

  • Anapauō · “will give” · Strong’s G373

    The Greek verb anapauō means to give rest, refresh, or cause to cease from labor. Occurring 12 times in the NT, it combines ana (up/again) with pauō (to stop).

    Anapauo is to give rest, to cause to cease, to refresh. Jesus does not say you will rest yourselves. He will give it. Verse 29 then says you will find rest for your souls — a second rest, found under the yoke. The gift in verse 28 and the finding in verse 29 are not two religions. One is the welcome. The other is what a man discovers once he is actually under the teacher.

  • ζυγός zugos · “yoke” · Strong’s G2218

    A noun meaning yoke — the wooden frame joining two animals for plowing — and also a pair of scales or balance for weighing. In its literal sense it represents the labor of agriculture.

    Zugos is a yoke: the wooden frame that joins two animals to a plow. It is also, in the speech of the rabbis, the yoke of the law and the yoke of the kingdom. Jesus does not offer an unyoked life. He offers His yoke in place of the one grinding you. A yoke that is not there is not freedom. It is an animal that cannot plow. The question this verse asks is not whether you will wear one. It is whose.

  • μανθάνω Manthano · “learn” · Strong’s G3129

    "Take my yoke upon you, and learn (manthano) from me" (Matthew 11:29) — Jesus' great invitation is essentially "come, be my student." Paul writes in Philippians 4:11: "I have learned (manthano) in whatever situation I am, to be content" — a statement that places contentment in the category of acquired knowledge, not natural temperament. Even the author of Hebrews writes that Jesus Himself "learned (manthano) obedience through what he suffered" (Hebrews 5:8), establishing suffering as one of God's primary classrooms.

    Manthano is to learn as a disciple learns — by attaching yourself to a teacher, not by collecting sayings. “Learn from me” is not “take notes on my lectures.” It is apprenticeship. The meekness and lowliness are not extra information about Jesus' personality. They are what you are to learn, because they are what the teacher is. A harsh master can teach a trade. He cannot give you rest while you learn it.

What the yoke is not

Easy here does not mean trivial, and light does not mean imaginary. A light burden is still a burden you carry. The contrast is with the loads being tied onto people’s backs in this Gospel — heavy, and not moved with one of the teachers' fingers. Jesus' load is the one He carries with you. A double yoke has two necks in it. That is why the meekness of the other neck is the whole argument.

2. Where It Sits

Do not start this saying in a vacuum. Matthew 11 has been a hard chapter.

John is in prison and sends to ask if Jesus is the one. Jesus answers with the works, then tells the crowd that the least in the kingdom is greater than John, then says this generation is a pack of children who will dance for no one. Then the woes: Chorazin, Bethsaida, Capernaum — cities that saw the works and did not repent. It will be more tolerable for Sodom.

Then verse 25. At that time Jesus answered, I thank you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that you hid these things from the wise and understanding and revealed them to infants. All things have been delivered to me by my Father. No one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son, and anyone to whom the Son wills to reveal Him.

Then come. The invitation is spoken by the one who hides and reveals, the one to whom all things have been handed. That order is the doctrine. A soft Jesus who is merely a rest-stop for tired people is not who is talking. The Son who has the Father’s authority is offering Himself as the place the exhausted may stand.

Jeremiah had already said, ask for the old paths, where is the good way, and walk in it, and you will find rest for your souls. They would not. Jesus takes that sentence and puts Himself in the middle of it. The good way has a name, and He is telling you to come.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition heard this as the gospel’s own voice, not as a footnote for people who had already found their way in. The called are the labouring and the laden. That is who the Messiah was sent to.

Only we observed, that those who labour and are heavy laden are called by Christ, seeing he was sent “to preach good tidings to the meek;” “to bind up the broken-hearted; to proclaim liberty to the captives, and the opening of the prison to them that are bound;” “to comfort all that mourn.”

Calvin, Institutes III.4

Calvin’s point in that chapter is that the soul does not get rest by staring at its own tears. It fixes both eyes on the mercy of God. The invitation is not “come once you have felt sorry enough.” It is “come, because He calls the ones who are already spent.”

The tradition also refused to let the yoke vanish. Rest in Christ is not the end of obedience. It is the end of earning. You take a yoke. You learn. You carry a burden. What dies is the other burden — the one you were never meant to carry, the one that said your standing was the next performance. Easy and light are words about a Master who is meek, not about a life with no commands in it.

4. Confessional Anchor

Faith in Jesus Christ is a saving grace, by which we receive and rest upon him alone for salvation, as he is offered to us in the gospel.

The Baptist Catechism, Q. 91

Rest upon him alone. The catechism uses the same verb the verse is built on. Coming to Jesus is not a mood and it is not a decision to try harder. It is faith doing what faith does: receiving, and resting, on a person who is actually offered.

As he is offered to us in the gospel is the other half. You do not come to a Jesus of your own design — a Jesus without a yoke, or a Jesus without meekness, or a Jesus who is only a teacher and not a Saviour. You come to Him as He is preached. This paragraph is that offer, in His own mouth.

The principal acts of saving faith have immediate relation to Christ, accepting, receiving, and resting upon him alone, for justification, sanctification, and eternal life, by virtue of the covenant of grace.

1689 LBCF, ch. 14 — Of Saving Faith

Immediate relation to Christ. Not to a system first, and then to Him if the system works. The confession and the verse agree on the direction: you come to Him. Justification is in that rest, and so is sanctification. The yoke is not a second stage for serious people. It is the same Christ, now as teacher and king, which is why the confession’s next chapter on His offices will not let you take the priest and skip the king.

5. For the Man Reading This

If you are tired, start with the first verb. Come. Not after you have fixed the thing that tired you. The people Jesus names are tired now. The rest is given to people who arrive still carrying it.

Name the yoke you are actually under. Most men are already in harness — a standard they cannot meet, a parent’s voice they never buried, a religious pace they think is holiness, a career that will not forgive a weak quarter. Jesus does not say drop every responsibility. He says take Mine. The swap is the command. You cannot wear both.

Then become a student again. Learn from me is humiliating for a competent man, which is part of why it is here. The teacher you need is meek. If the voice training you is harsh, frantic, or endlessly disappointed, it is not His. Sit under the one who is lowly in heart and do the next ordinary obedience without the extra weight of proving yourself.

And do not hear easy as permission to go slack. A light burden still has to be picked up tomorrow. The test of this passage is not whether you felt soothed when you read it. It is whether you came, whether you are under His yoke, and whether your soul has somewhere to rest that is not your own performance.
