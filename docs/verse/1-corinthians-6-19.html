Verse Study · 1 Corinthians

1 Corinthians 6:19

Paul does not tell you to treat your body as a temple. He tells you it already is one, and then he tells you whose.

Or don’t you know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit who is in you, whom you have from God? You are not your own,— World English Bible (public domain)

What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own?— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The verse is a question with a verdict inside it. Know ye not — as if they did know, and were living as if they had forgotten. Your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost who is in you, whom you have from God. Then the line that does the damage: you are not your own.

He does not say the Spirit would like to be welcomed. He does not say the body could become a temple if you were serious enough. The building is already occupied. The argument is from a fact, not from an aspiration.

Verse 20 is the next breath — for you were bought with a price — and this verse is incomplete without it. Ownership is the whole point. A temple does not belong to the stone. A purchased man does not belong to the man.

  • σῶμα sōma · “body” · Strong’s G4983

    Sōma appears 142 times in the NT. In Greek thought, body and soul were often sharply divided — the soul was the true self trapped in an inferior body.

    Soma is the physical body, and in Paul it is never a prison the soul is trying to escape. Greek religion often treated the body as the inferior half of a person. Paul will not. The body is God’s creation, destined for resurrection, and in this paragraph it is the present site of the Spirit’s dwelling. What you do with it is not a side issue. It is the issue the verse was written to name.

  • ναός naos · “temple” · Strong’s G3485

    The inner temple, the sanctuary — the dwelling place of God. Distinguished from hieron (the entire temple complex), naos specifically means the sacred inner chamber where God's presence dwells.

    Naos is the inner sanctuary, not the whole temple courts. The New Testament keeps hieron for the complex and naos for the shrine — the chamber where the presence is. Paul takes that word off the building in Jerusalem and sets it on the bodies of a church that has been sleeping around. The shock is the location. The Holy One is not waiting in a holy precinct. He is in them.

  • ἅγιος hagios · “Holy” · Strong’s G40

    The Greek equivalent of Hebrew qadosh (H6918), appearing 233 times. Hagios means set apart, consecrated, and morally pure.

    Hagios is set apart, consecrated, morally pure — the word the New Testament uses for God, for the Spirit, and then for the people. Here it is attached to the Spirit, not to the Corinthian. The holiness in the sentence is not a quality they have achieved. It is the character of the Occupant. That is why fornication in this chapter is not merely a bad choice. It is a desecration.

  • πνεῦμα pneuma · “Spirit” · Strong’s G4151

    A word of remarkable range: wind, breath, the human spirit, an evil spirit, or — most significantly — the Holy Spirit of God. 379 times.

    Pneuma ranges from wind to breath to spirit, and here it is the Holy Spirit of God, in you, whom you have from God. Two clauses, both possessive. He is in you, and you have Him, and you have Him from God — so even the Gift is not a trophy. The Spirit is not a power you wield. He is a Person who was given, and the body is His address.

  • ἑαυτοῦ heautou · “your own” · Strong’s G1438

    The reflexive pronoun heautou (and its forms heautō, heauton, heautēs, etc.) means of/to/by himself/herself/itself — the subject acting upon or referring back to itself. It appears ~319 times in the NT and is essential for expressing self-denial, self-giving, and mutual submission.

    Heautou is the reflexive: yourselves, your own. Paul uses it to land the claim. You are not your own. The body you have been treating as private property is not a private holding. That is the sentence that turns a sexual ethic into a property dispute, and Paul thinks the property dispute is the more honest frame.

The question he keeps asking

Know ye not is a Corinthian refrain. Paul has already used it in this letter about being a temple in chapter 3 — there the temple is the congregation. Here it is the body. Both are true and they do not cancel. God dwells among the people, and God dwells in the person. A man can affirm the first and still live as if his own flesh were a rented room he may use as he likes. Chapter 6 will not let him.

2. Where It Sits

The paragraph is about fornication. Verse 13: the body is not for fornication, but for the Lord, and the Lord for the body. Verse 15: shall I take the members of Christ and make them members of a harlot? Verse 16: the two shall become one flesh. Then our verse, then the purchase, then therefore glorify God in your body.

So this is not a wellness text. It is not a gym verse. It is an argument against joining what belongs to Christ to a union that does not. The temple image is doing anti-porneia work. If you lift it out of that paragraph you will make it say something softer than Paul meant.

The chapter had another problem first: believers suing each other before pagan courts. That also is a belonging problem — you belong to each other, so why are you handing your grievance to outsiders? Then he turns to the body. Same logic, closer in. You belong to the Lord, so why are you handing the body to someone He did not join you to?

Corinth knew temples. The city was famous for them. Paul takes their most loaded religious word and applies it to the bodies of men who thought Greek freedom meant the body was a matter of appetite. All things are lawful for me, they said. Paul answers with a shrine.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin read this verse as a resurrection text as much as a purity text. The body that is a temple now is the body that will be raised. Chastity is not a temporary lease on a disposable frame.

Meanwhile, we see how he connects the resurrection with chastity and holiness, as he shortly after includes our bodies in the purchase of redemption.

Calvin, Institutes III.25

That is the right pairing. Verse 14 has already said God raised the Lord and will raise us. Verse 19 says the Spirit is in the body now. Verse 20 says the body was in the purchase. The tradition refused to let anyone treat holiness as a soul project that leaves Tuesday’s body out of it.

It also refused the opposite error: treating the body as dirty by nature. If the Spirit indwells it, matter is not the problem. Misuse is. The Gnostic instinct — spirit good, flesh regrettable — cannot survive this sentence. Paul will not save you from fornication by teaching you to despise the flesh. He will save you from it by telling you whose the flesh is.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession’s doctrine of sanctification is an indwelling doctrine. The holiness required of the body is not a self-improvement program. It is what happens when the Spirit actually lives there.

They who are united to Christ, effectually called, and regenerated, having a new heart, and a new spirit created in them, through the virtue of Christ’s death and resurrection, are also farther sanctified, really and personally, through the same virtue, by his Word and Spirit dwelling in them; the dominion of the whole body of sin is destroyed, and the several lusts thereof are more and more weakened and mortified, and they more and more quickened and strengthened in all saving graces, to the practice of all true holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord.

1689 LBCF, ch. 13 — Of Sanctification

Word and Spirit dwelling in them is 1 Corinthians 6:19 in confessional English. Really and personally — not as a metaphor you visit on Sundays. The several lusts thereof are more and more weakened — that is what a occupied temple does to the old tenant. The confession will not say the war is over. It will say the Occupant is not leaving.

The purchase sits one chapter earlier:

The Lord Jesus, by his perfect obedience and sacrifice of himself, which he through the eternal Spirit once offered up unto God, has fully satisfied the justice of God, procured reconciliation, and purchased an everlasting inheritance in the kingdom of heaven, for all those whom the Father has given unto him.

1689 LBCF, ch. 8 — Of Christ the Mediator

Purchased. Verse 20’s word. You are not your own because a price was paid, and the price was a death. A man who talks about his body as if the only question were his happiness has not yet done the arithmetic of this verse.

5. For the Man Reading This

The first thing to do with this verse is to stop using it as a slogan for sleep and vegetables. Those may be wise. Paul is talking about who you join your body to. The presenting issue in Corinth was sexual. It still is, for a great many men who can recite the verse and have not yet let it near the phone.

You are not your own is not an insult. It is a relief, once you hear it. The modern instinct is that the body is the last private property — the one thing no one else gets a claim on. Paul says that claim has already been filed, and it was filed at a cross. The question is not whether you will be owned. It is whether you will admit it.

So the practical shape is this. Before you take the body somewhere, you ask whose temple you are walking into the room with. Not as a scare. As a fact. The Spirit is already there. He was given. He did not arrive on the condition that you keep the place tidy; He arrived, and the tidying is what you do because He is there.

And take the resurrection seriously enough to be chaste. If this body is going to be raised, then what you do with it is not going to be left behind as if it were a costume. Calvin was right: Paul connects the raising with the holiness. A man who believes he will get this body back has fewer illusions about what he can spend it on tonight.
