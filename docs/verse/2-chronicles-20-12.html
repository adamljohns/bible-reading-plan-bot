Verse Study · 2 Chronicles

2 Chronicles 20:12

The honest sentence in a crisis: we have no might, we do not know what to do, and our eyes are still on You. The last clause is the only one that is not a confession of bankruptcy.

Our God, will you not judge them? For we have no might against this great company that comes against us. We don’t know what to do, but our eyes are on you.”— World English Bible (public domain)

O our God, wilt thou not judge them? for we have no might against this great company that cometh against us; neither know we what to do: but our eyes are upon thee.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Jehoshaphat does not dress the situation. He asks God to judge the invading company. Then he names two vacancies: no might, and no plan. Then he names the one remaining asset: our eyes are upon thee.

The verse is a prayer, not a slogan. It is spoken in public, in the house God chose, with Judah standing there. The king is not performing helplessness. He is telling the truth about the ratio: a great company coming, and a people who cannot match it.

Notice the order. Judge them. We have no might. We do not know what to do. But our eyes. The but is doing the work. The last clause does not cancel the first three. It stands beside them. A man can be empty of strength and empty of a plan and still be looking in the right direction.

  • שָׁפַט Shaphat · “wilt thou not judge” · Strong’s H8199

    Shaphat means to judge, govern, decide, or vindicate. It appears about 200 times.

    Shaphat is to judge, govern, decide, or vindicate. Jehoshaphat is not asking God to take a side in a private feud. He is asking the Judge to do what a judge does: decide a case that Judah cannot try by force. The question wilt thou not is not unbelief. It is covenant memory. This is the God who promised to hear when His people prayed toward this house. The king is holding Him to His own word.

  • כֹּחַ koach · “we have no might” · Strong’s H3581

    Hebrew word for strength or power, about 130 times. Describes physical strength, capacity, or resources.

    Koach is strength, power, capacity, resources. Physical strength, and the means to act. Jehoshaphat does not say we have a little and need a boost. He says we have none against this company. That is a king’s sentence. Most men in his seat would inflate the inventory. He empties it. The verse will not let you treat “I can handle this” as faith.

  • יָדַע Yada · “neither know” · Strong’s H3045

    The Hebrew verb yada means to know, perceive, discern, or be acquainted with. It is one of the most frequently used verbs in the Old Testament, occurring nearly 950 times.

    Yada is to know, perceive, discern. Not trivia. The knowledge that lets a man act. Judah does not have a strategy that matches the field. The king says so out loud, in front of the people who are looking at him for one. That is rarer than it sounds. A leader’s usual sin here is to invent a plan so that he will not have to say yada in the negative.

  • Asah · “what to do” · Strong’s H6213

    The Hebrew verb asah (עָשָׂה) is one of the most common verbs in the Old Testament, appearing over 2,600 times. It means to do, make, create, accomplish, or work.

    Asah is to do, make, accomplish. The gap is not only information. It is action. They do not know what to do. Prayer that stops at feelings has not reached this verb. Jehoshaphat is asking for a next step he does not have, from a God who does.

  • Ayin · “our eyes” · Strong’s H5869

    The Hebrew noun ayin (עַיִן) means eye, but also spring or fountain of water. It appears over 880 times, making it one of the most frequent nouns in the Hebrew Bible.

    Ayin is the eye — also a spring, a fountain. Here it is the organ of attention. The eyes are upon God. Not upon the company, not upon the inventory, not upon the missing plan. Attention is the one thing they still have, and they aim it. The rest of the chapter will prove whether that aiming was theater. It was not. They go out with singers, not with a new weapon.

2. Where It Sits

Chapter 20 opens with a report: a great multitude from beyond the sea, Moab and Ammon, and others with them. Jehoshaphat feared. That is the narrator’s word. He set himself to seek the LORD and proclaimed a fast throughout Judah. The people gathered. The king stood in the assembly, in the house, and prayed.

The prayer before verse 12 is not panic. It is history. Are you not God in heaven? Did you not drive out the inhabitants of this land before your people Israel, and give it to the seed of Abraham? They have built you a sanctuary. If evil comes — the sword, judgment, pestilence, famine — we will stand in this house and cry, and you will hear and save. Then: see the children of Ammon, Moab, and Mount Seir. We will not. You would not let Israel invade them when Israel came out of Egypt. Now they reward us by coming to cast us out of your possession.

Verse 12 is the end of that argument. The case has been laid. The inventory has been shown empty. The eyes go up.

What follows is not a committee. The Spirit of the LORD comes on Jahaziel, a Levite, in the midst of the congregation. The battle is not yours, but God’s. Tomorrow go down. You will not need to fight. Stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD. Jehoshaphat bows. Judah worships. They appoint singers. When they begin to sing, the LORD sets ambushes. The enemy destroys itself. They name the valley Berachah — blessing.

So the verse is not a technique for getting a miracle. It is the last honest sentence before God speaks. The eyes were on Him before the prophecy, not after. That order matters. Most men reverse it. They want the plan first, then they will look.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed reading of a prayer like this is not that God needs information. It is that the empty man must go beyond himself. Calvin opens the great chapter on prayer there.

From the previous part of the work we clearly see how completely destitute man is of all good, how devoid of every means of procuring his own salvation. Hence, if he would obtain succour in his necessity, he must go beyond himself, and procure it in some other quarter.

Calvin, Institutes III.20

That is Jehoshaphat’s koach and yada in doctrinal form. Destitute. Devoid of means. Go beyond yourself. The eyes are the going. They are not a feeling. They are the refusal to procure help in the only quarter that still looks open: your own head.

Calvin’s third rule of prayer is the same emptying:

The third rule to be added is: that he who comes into the presence of God to pray must divest himself of all vainglorious thoughts, lay aside all idea of worth; in short, discard all self-confidence, humbly giving God the whole glory

Calvin, Institutes III.20

Jehoshaphat discarded it in public. A king who says “we have no might” in front of an army has laid aside the idea of worth. The glory goes to the Judge he asked to judge.

The tradition also refused to turn this verse into a license for sloth. After the word came, they still went out in the morning. They still appointed singers. Standing still was obedience to a specific command, not a personality. Eyes on God did not mean staying in the house. It meant going out without a sword-plan because the plan had been given.

4. Confessional Anchor

God the good Creator of all things, in his infinite power and wisdom, does uphold, direct, dispose, and govern all creatures and things, from the greatest even to the least, by his most wise and holy providence

1689 LBCF, ch. 5 — Of Divine Providence

From the greatest even to the least. The company coming against Judah is not outside the government. Neither is the empty inventory. Jehoshaphat’s prayer assumes this paragraph. If providence has gaps, the eyes have nowhere to land.

Prayer, with thanksgiving, being one part of natural worship, is by God required of all men. But that it may be accepted, it is to be made in the name of the Son, by the help of the Spirit, according to his will — with understanding, reverence, humility, fervency, faith, love, and perseverance

1689 LBCF, ch. 22 — Of Religious Worship and the Sabbath Day

Humility is in the list. Verse 12 is that word made audible. Fervency is there too. This is not a cool report. It is a king asking whether God will judge, with a multitude on the road.

Moreover, solemn humiliation with fastings, and thanksgivings upon special occasions, ought to be used in a holy and religious manner.

1689 LBCF, ch. 22 — Of Religious Worship and the Sabbath Day

That sentence is this chapter. Jehoshaphat proclaimed a fast. Then, after the deliverance, they blessed the LORD in the valley they named for blessing. Fasting, then thanksgiving. The confession is not adding a piety. It is describing what Judah actually did.

The conclusion of the Lord’s Prayer, which is, For yours is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, forever. Amen, teaches us to take our encouragement in prayer from God only

The Baptist Catechism, Q. 114

From God only. Not from the remaining might. Not from the plan you do not have. Encouragement in prayer is located. Jehoshaphat took his from the God whose kingdom, power, and glory he had just rehearsed. The catechism will not let you look at your eyes as if they were the help. The eyes are the empty man’s last honest act. The help is His.

5. For the Man Reading This

Say the middle of the verse out loud before you pray the end. We have no might against this. We do not know what to do. If you skip those sentences, “our eyes are upon thee” is a caption, not a prayer. Most men invert it. They look at God as a supplement to the plan they already like.

You will not feel ready. Jehoshaphat feared. The narrator said so. Fear did not disqualify the prayer. It occasioned the fast. The question is not whether you are calm. It is whether your eyes have a lawful object now that the inventory is empty.

Do not use this verse to refuse the next obedience. Judah still marched in the morning. They still posted singers. If the word for you is to make the call, make it. Eyes on God is not a substitute for the thing He has already told you to do. It is the posture in which you do it when you still cannot see how it works.

And do not wait for a Jahaziel before you look. The prophecy came to people who were already assembled, already fasting, already telling the truth about their might. The looking was not a reward for having a strategy. It was what they did instead of inventing one.

The company in front of you may not scatter this week. The verse did not promise a timetable. It located the eyes. Keep them there when the field is still full. The Judge was asked. The account is His. Your job is not to become a great company. It is to remain a man whose last true sentence is still the last clause.
