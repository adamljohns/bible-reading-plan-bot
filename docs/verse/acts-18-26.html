Verse Study · Acts

Acts 18:26

Accurately, then more accurately: a true teacher made complete.

1. The Text and Its Words

And he began to speak boldly in the synagogue: whom when Aquila and Priscilla had heard, they took him unto them, and expounded unto him the way of God more perfectly.

— King James Version (public domain)

He began to speak boldly in the synagogue. But when Priscilla and Aquila heard him, they took him aside, and explained to him the way of God more accurately.

— World English Bible (public domain)

The two public-domain texts give the couple's names in opposite orders. The KJV reads Aquila and Priscilla. The WEB reads Priscilla and Aquila. Nothing in this study rests on the order.

The Strong's numbers below are the ones the Strong's-tagged King James text puts on each word, and each links to its lexicon entry. The verse cannot be read apart from the one before it, so two words come from verse 25.

  • instructed (v.25) — katēcheō, G2727. The lexicon defines it as "to instruct, teach orally, or inform", and notes that English gets catechism and catechize from it. Apollos had been taught. He was no self-made preacher.

  • fervent (v.25) — zeo, G2204. The lexicon gives "to boil, be hot, or be fervent". He boiled in spirit. Paul uses the same verb in Romans 12:11, where fervency is commanded.

  • diligently (v.25) — ἀκριβῶς, akribōs, G199. The lexicon gives "carefully, accurately, diligently, or exactly". The KJV says diligently and the WEB says accurately. Either way, what Apollos taught was true.

  • expounded (v.26) — ektithēmi, G1620. The lexicon gives "to set forth, expose, or explain in detail". Aquila and Priscilla did more than correct one point. They laid the way of God out in order.

  • more perfectly (v.26) — akribesteron, G197. The lexicon gives "more carefully, more precisely, or more exactly". This is the hinge word, and the tags show why. Verse 25 has G199. Verse 26 has G197. The lexicon's own definitions give the same core meaning, accurately and then more accurately. The WEB brings this into English: "taught accurately" in verse 25, "more accurately" in verse 26. The KJV's diligently … more perfectly hides it.

Two more words matter: "speak boldly" and "took him unto them". Both are read here from the plain English. He was bold in public. They took him aside in private.

2. Where It Sits

Acts 18 opens with Paul at Corinth. There he "found a certain Jew named Aquila, born in Pontus, lately come from Italy, with his wife Priscilla" (18:2, KJV). They had been driven out of Rome by Claudius. They worked with their hands: "by their occupation they were tentmakers" (18:3). Paul "abode with them, and wrought" (18:3), and he stayed in Corinth "a year and six months, teaching the word of God" (18:11). When he sailed for Syria, they went with him, and he "came to Ephesus, and left them there" (18:19). So the couple who set Apollos straight had lived and worked beside the apostle. They gave Apollos what they had been given.

Then Apollos arrives (18:24). Luke lists his strengths before he names his gap. He was a Jew, born at Alexandria, "an eloquent man, and mighty in the scriptures". He had been instructed. He was fervent. He taught accurately. Then comes the one limit: "knowing only the baptism of John" (18:25).

What did that lack mean? Luke answers in the very next scene, and he places it on purpose: "while Apollos was at Corinth" (19:1). Paul comes to Ephesus and finds disciples who know only John's baptism. Paul tells them that John "baptized with the baptism of repentance, saying unto the people, that they should believe on him which should come after him, that is, on Christ Jesus" (19:4). They are baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus, and the Holy Ghost comes on them. Luke does not say whether Apollos himself was baptized again, and this study does not guess. But the two scenes are side by side. John's baptism pointed forward. Aquila and Priscilla showed Apollos where it pointed and what had already happened.

Look at what follows the correction (18:27-28). The brethren write, "exhorting the disciples to receive him" (18:27). In Achaia he "helped them much which had believed through grace". And "he mightily convinced the Jews, and that publickly, shewing by the scriptures that Jesus was Christ." The man who was taken aside in private comes out stronger in public. Paul later describes the result in Corinth: "I have planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase" (1 Cor 3:6, KJV).

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Scripture is the first witness, and on this verse it says three plain things.

First, the correction built on what was true. Luke says Apollos taught akribōs, accurately. He does not take that back in verse 26. He raises it to akribesteron, more accurately. The couple did not tear down a false teacher. They completed a true one. A careful reader should not turn this into a story about heresy. It is a story about something incomplete being made full.

Second, zeal was not the defect. Apollos was "fervent in the spirit", zeo, boiling. Paul commands the same thing of every believer: "Not slothful in business; fervent in spirit; serving the Lord" (Rom 12:11, KJV). Aquila and Priscilla did not cool him down. After the correction Luke says he convinced the Jews mightily. Calvin, on verse 25, warns in both directions: doctrine without zeal lies cold and useless, and zeal without knowledge runs headlong. That is why, he says, Luke puts Apollos's knowledge of the Scriptures first, to govern his zeal. Heat without light is dangerous, and light without heat is useless. The Lord meant Apollos to have both, and the correction gave him both.

Third, the man did not become a party. Apollos was so useful that Corinth tried to make a faction of him: "I am of Apollos". Paul answers: "Who then is Paul, and who is Apollos, but ministers by whom ye believed, even as the Lord gave to every man?" (1 Cor 3:4-5, KJV). The teachable man stays a servant. A man who would not take correction would be a poor servant. The two go together.

Calvin, on verse 26, saw humility on both sides of the exchange. First the couple:

"By this it appeareth how far Priscilla and Aquila were from the love of themselves, and from envying another man's virtue, in that they deliver those things familiarly and privately to an eloquent man, which he may afterward utter publicly."

Calvin, Commentary on Acts, on 18:26 (public domain)

Then the preacher. Calvin calls it no small modesty in Apollos that he let himself be taught by a craftsman and by a woman, though he surpassed them both in eloquence and in the Scriptures. The couple gave away what they knew so another man could use it in public. Apollos took it without guarding his reputation. Neither one was thinking about himself.

4. Confessional Anchor

Apollos was "mighty in the scriptures" and still needed someone to open them further. The 1689 London Baptist Confession expects both things to be true of God's people. Chapter 1, paragraph 7 (usmcmin.org/lbcf/chapter-01.html):

All things in Scripture are not alike plain in themselves, nor alike clear to all; yet those things which are necessary to be known, believed, and observed for salvation, are so clearly set forth and opened in some place of Scripture or other, that not only the learned, but the unlearned, in a due use of ordinary means, may attain to a sufficient understanding of them.

1689 LBCF, ch. 1

"In a due use of ordinary means." Aquila and Priscilla were ordinary means. They were tentmakers. That is how the Lord usually works.

The same chapter tells us how they would have done it. Paragraph 9:

The infallible rule of the interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself; and therefore when there is a question about the true and full sense of any Scripture (which is not many but one), it must be searched out by other places that speak more clearly.

1689 LBCF, ch. 1

Apollos already knew John. They showed him what the rest of Scripture says John was pointing to. In verse 28 he does the same thing in public: he goes "shewing by the scriptures that Jesus was Christ."

The Baptist Catechism, Q5 (usmcmin.org/catechism.html):

All men are not only permitted, but commanded and exhorted to read, hear, and understand the holy Scriptures.

The Baptist Catechism, Q5

Understand. Hearing is not enough, and eloquence is not enough. The command is to understand, and a man who means to obey it will need other believers to help him.

5. For the Man Reading This

Every man in this passage gives you something to do.

Be teachable. Apollos had every reason to bristle. He was eloquent, educated, mighty in the Scriptures, and already preaching in public. The two people who corrected him made tents for a living. Luke records no argument from him. He received it, and he went on to do more good than before. Ask yourself who is allowed to correct you. If the honest answer is "only men with more credentials than I have", pride has a foothold. A gifted man who cannot be taught stays exactly as incomplete as he is now.

Correct a brother in private. When you hear a brother say something true but incomplete, from a pulpit, in a group, or across a table, do not answer him from the floor. Do not post a correction for others to see. Take him aside. Start with what he has right, and add what he is missing. The goal is a stronger brother, not a win for you.

Face outward together. Aquila and Priscilla did this together, as husband and wife, and they did it privately. The verse does not say where, and it does not say who did the talking. This study will not invent either. What it does show is a marriage that faced outward. The same couple had already taken Paul in to live and work beside them (18:3). Husband, ask what your marriage is for. If it serves only your family's comfort, it is too small. A husband and wife who will take a younger believer aside, hear out his half-formed theology, and help him to the whole of it are a weapon in the Lord's hand.

Keep the heat. Do not let the lesson become "be less zealous". Apollos was corrected and stayed fervent. Lukewarm men like to call their coldness caution. It is not. Be fervent and be accurate, both at once.

Teach your sons to take correction as a gift. Apollos needed "more accurately". So will they, and so do you.
