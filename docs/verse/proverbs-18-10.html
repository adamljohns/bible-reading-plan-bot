Proverbs 18:10

The next verse will tell you the counterfeit. Wealth as a strong city, a high wall in a man’s own conceit. This verse is the real tower. The name. The run. Set aloft.

the LORD’s name is a strong tower: the righteous run to him, and are safe.— World English Bible (public domain)

The name of the LORD is a strong tower: the righteous runneth into it, and is safe. safe: Heb. set aloft— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The name of the LORD is a strong tower: the righteous runneth into it, and is safe. The KJV margin on safe is the Hebrew: set aloft. Not merely unhurt. Raised. Put out of reach. A tower does that. You do not stroll into a tower. You run, and you go up.

The name is not a sound you make to get a result. Shem is reputation, character, the Person as He has made Himself known. The LORD — YHWH — is the covenant name. The tower is not a technique. It is who He is, available as shelter to the man who will actually move toward Him.

Then the next line of the chapter, which this verse is built to answer: the rich man’s wealth is his strong city, and as an high wall in his own conceit. Two towers. One is a Name. One is an imagination. Proverbs will not let you quote 18:10 as if 18:11 were not on the page.

  • Shem · “The name” · Strong’s H8034

    The Hebrew word shem (שֵׁם) means name, but extends to reputation, fame, character, and memorial. It appears over 800 times in the OT and is one of the most theologically loaded words in all of Scripture.

    Shem means name, and then fame, memorial, the public truth of who someone is. To run into the name is to run into the God who has spoken His name, not into a slogan. Acts 4:12 will say there is no other name under heaven given among men whereby we must be saved. The proverb already knew that safety is located in a name, not in a pile.

  • Migdal · “tower” · Strong’s H4026

    The Hebrew noun migdal means a tower, a high platform, or a fortified structure. Towers served defensive (city walls), agricultural (watchtowers in vineyards), and symbolic (pride, strength) functions in the ancient world.

    Migdal is a tower, a high platform, a fortified structure — city walls, watchtowers in vineyards, the thing you climb when the field is not safe. Strength (oz) is attached to it here. The image is military and agricultural at once. A watchtower is useless if you will not enter it. A name you admire from the plain is not this verse.

  • צַדִּיק tsaddiq · “the righteous” · Strong’s H6662

    Describes a person who is in right standing — conforming to an ethical or legal standard. Appearing over 200 times, tsaddiq is the primary Old Testament word for moral uprightness.

    Tsaddiq is the one in right standing — conforming to the standard, the man the book of Proverbs keeps contrasting with the fool and the wicked. He is not the man who has no trouble. He is the man who runs the right direction when trouble comes. The verse does not say the righteous never needs a tower. It says he uses one.

  • רוּץ ruts · “runneth” · Strong’s H7323

    To run with urgency and purpose. Used of messengers (those who 'run' with news), of eager service, and of the godly running toward God's commandments.

    Ruts is to run with urgency and purpose — messengers, eager service, the godly running toward commandments. The righteous runs. Safety in this verse is not a mood that settles on you while you stand in the open. It is the result of motion toward the name. A man who only agrees that the LORD is a tower, and does not run, is still on the plain.

  • שָׂגַב Sagab · “is safe” · Strong’s H7682

    The Hebrew verb sagab (שָׂגַב) means to be high, lofty, inaccessible, or safely set on high. It describes both the literal elevation of fortresses on mountain peaks and the metaphorical exaltation of the righteous through God's protection.

    Sagab is to be high, lofty, inaccessible, safely set on high. Fortresses on ridges. The margin’s set aloft is better English for it than a vague “safe.” You can be unhurt and still exposed. This verb is elevation. The run ends above the thing that was chasing you.

2. Where It Sits

Proverbs 18 is in the Solomonic sentences, not the long father-speeches of chapters 1–9. The sayings around this one are about the mouth, isolation, the fool’s lips, the name of the LORD, wealth, a broken spirit, and a friend closer than a brother. Verse 10 is not a random piety dropped in. It is the safety-claim, set immediately against the rich man’s wall.

Read them as a pair or you will misread both. The wealth-tower looks like a migdal. It has height. It has a wall. The proverb says the height is in his own conceit. Imagination. The name-tower is not imagination. The righteous run into it. The rich man sits behind a wall he invented.

Psalm 61:3 has already used the image: you have been a shelter for me, a strong tower from the enemy. The proverb is that psalm in one stroke, with the run named. Joel 2:32 and Romans 10:13 will take the name as the place you call. Calvin will join this verse to that calling and then to the name of Christ.

The righteous in Proverbs is not a man who has earned a bunker. He is the man whose way is with the LORD. Running into the name is what his righteousness looks like under pressure. The fool’s safety-plan is elsewhere in the chapter: his mouth, his isolation, his money.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin quotes this verse twice in the Institutes, and both times he will not leave it as a Jewish proverb only.

On the Trinity and the name of Christ:

And another says, “The name of the Lord (Jehovah) is a strong tower; the righteous runneth into it and is safe”

Calvin, Institutes I.13

But the name of Christ is invoked for salvation, and therefore it follows that he is Jehovah.

Calvin, Institutes I.13

That is the proverb read from the far side of the resurrection. The tower has a name you can call. Stephen called it. The church called it. If the name of Christ is the name you run into for salvation, then the shem of Proverbs 18:10 is not smaller than Jesus. It is the same LORD.

On prayer he pairs Solomon with Joel:

“The name of the Lord,” says Solomon, “is a strong tower; the righteous runneth into it, and is safe.”

Calvin, Institutes III.20

Then: whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be delivered. Running and calling are the same motion in two verbs. The Reformed reading refused to make the tower a charm you wear. It is a place you enter by invoking the name — prayer, not a necklace.

The tradition also refused to let “the righteous” mean “the man who is already safe.” The tsaddiq is the man who runs. Righteousness here is revealed as flight in the right direction.

4. Confessional Anchor

The Lord our God is but one only living and true God; whose subsistence is in and of himself, infinite in being and perfection

1689 LBCF, ch. 2 — Of God and of the Holy Trinity

A tower is only as strong as what it is made of. This verse’s tower is the name of that God. Not a title we assigned. The living God, in and of Himself. If He were a constructed deity, 18:11 would be the whole truth: a wall in conceit.

The Lord Jesus, by his perfect obedience and sacrifice of himself, which he through the eternal Spirit once offered up unto God, has fully satisfied the justice of God, procured reconciliation, and purchased an everlasting inheritance in the kingdom of heaven, for all those whom the Father has given unto him.

1689 LBCF, ch. 8 — Of Christ the Mediator

Calvin’s inference is the confession’s Christ. The name you run into for salvation is the name of the One who purchased the inheritance. The tower is not a metaphor that stays in Proverbs. It is a Person who was offered up.

The third commandment requires the holy and reverent use of God’s names, titles, attributes, ordinances, Word, and works.

The Baptist Catechism, Q. 59

The third commandment’s proof-texts on this site include Proverbs 18:10. That is not an accident. Taking the name in vain is the opposite of running into it. A vain name is a word you handle. A tower-name is a place you enter. Holy and reverent use includes the run. It is not only the refusal to swear. It is the refusal to treat the name as empty while you hide behind some other wall.

5. For the Man Reading This

Name your tower. If the thing you actually run to when the field goes bad is a balance, a reputation, a person, a plan, a bottle, or a locked door, you are in verse 11. The wall can look high. The proverb says the height is in your conceit. It will not hold.

The righteous run. That is the verb to obey this week. Prayer is the run. Refusal of the fake wall is the run. Calling on the name of the Lord is the run. You will not be set aloft by agreeing that the LORD is strong. You will be set aloft by going in.

Do not take the name as a charm. The third commandment is on the same page as this verse for a reason. A man who uses the name to decorate a life he will not flee into the name with is still on the plain, now with a slogan.

And do not wait to feel righteous enough to run. The tsaddiq in this sentence is identified by the running. The way you prove which tower is yours is which one you enter when you are afraid. The name is a strong tower. Run.
