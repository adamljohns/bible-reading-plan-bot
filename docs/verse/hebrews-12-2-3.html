Verse Study · Hebrews

Hebrews 12:2-3

The cloud of witnesses is not the thing you look at. The writer names them, then tells you to look away, at a Man who finished under worse contradiction than yours.

looking to Jesus, the author and perfecter of faith, who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising its shame, and has sat down at the right hand of the throne of God. For consider him who has endured such contradiction of sinners against himself, that you don’t grow weary, fainting in your souls.— World English Bible (public domain)

Looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith; who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is set down at the right hand of the throne of God. author: or, beginner For consider him that endured such contradiction of sinners against himself, lest ye be wearied and faint in your minds.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Verse 1 said run. Verse 2 says where the eyes go while you run. Looking unto Jesus, the author and finisher of our faith, who for the joy set before Him endured the cross, despising the shame, and sat down at the right hand of the throne of God. Then verse 3: consider Him who endured such contradiction of sinners against Himself, so that you do not grow weary and faint in your souls.

The order is the doctrine. Witnesses first, then Jesus, then your fatigue. You are not told to find more energy. You are told to do a kind of seeing, and then a kind of considering, because the thing that faints is the soul that has been staring at the wrong contest.

  • ἀφοράω Aphorao · “looking” · Strong’s G872

    The Greek verb aphorao combines apo (away from) and horao (to see), meaning to look away from all else and fix one's gaze intently on a single object. It appears twice in the New Testament: in Hebrews 12:2, exhorting believers to fix their eyes on Jesus, and in Philippians 2:23, where Paul looks to how things will go for Timothy.

    Aphorao is to look away and fix the eyes. The apo is the discarded direction. Verse 1’s cloud, verse 1’s weights, verse 1’s besetting sin — all of that is in the room, and none of it is the mark. The verb will not let you glance at Jesus as one more inspiring figure in the catalog. You turn from the catalog to the one the catalog was about.

  • ἀρχηγός archēgos · “author” · Strong’s G747

    The Greek noun archēgos means 'leader,' 'founder,' 'pioneer,' or 'author.' Composed of archē ('beginning, rule') and agō ('to lead'), it describes the one who initiates and leads others along a path. The word appears four times in the New Testament, each time in a Christological context.

    Archegos is pioneer, founder, the one who goes first and opens the way. Leader more than literary author, though both sit in the word. He is not a coach at the rail. He ran this course. Hebrews has already called Him the archegos of salvation, bringing many sons to glory. The faith you are running in is a path He cut, not a mood you invented.

  • ὑπομένω Hupomenō · “endured” · Strong’s G5278

    The Greek verb hupomenō (ὑπομένω) means to remain under — to stay behind, to endure, to bear up under a burden or trial with patience and perseverance. The related noun hupomonē is often translated 'patience' or 'steadfast endurance.' This is not passive resignation but active, determined persistence through difficulty because of confident hope.

    Hupomeno is to remain under — to stay, to bear up, to not leave the post while the thing is still on you. It is used of Him twice in two verses: He endured the cross; consider Him that endured contradiction. The writer wants that verb in your mouth when you talk about Jesus, because it is the verb you need for the race of verse 1. Patience in this book is not a temperament. It is remaining under.

  • καταφρονέω Kataphroneo · “despising” · Strong’s G2706

    The Greek verb kataphroneo means to think down upon, to despise, to treat with contempt, or to disregard entirely. The prefix kata- (down) combined with phroneo (to think) creates the image of looking down one's mind at something.

    The same verb Paul used of Timothy’s youth. To think down on, to treat as small. Here the object is the shame of the cross. He did not fail to notice it. Crucifixion was designed as public disgrace. He ranked it — put it beneath the joy set before Him — and went through it. Despising here is not swagger. It is a valuation. The shame was real and it was not the largest thing in the room.

  • ἀναλογίζομαι analogízomai · “consider” · Strong’s G357

    The Greek verb analogízomai (ἀναλογίζομαι) means to consider carefully, reckon thoroughly, or think through. It appears once in Hebrews 12:3 as an exhortation to fix one's thoughts deliberately on Christ.

    Analogizomai appears only here in the New Testament. It means to reckon through, to think a thing out, to compare. Looking is the glance that aims the run. Considering is the slower work when the soul is about to give out: do the arithmetic of what He endured, and then look at your own opposition again. Verse 4 will say you have not yet resisted to blood. The considering is what makes that sentence land as comfort instead of a taunt.

Author and finisher

The King James finisher is the pair of author, and the confession will use both words of Christ. He begins the faith and He brings it to its end. The race is not a human project He cheers from the chair. He is at both ends of it, and between them He is the one on the cross.

2. Where It Sits

Chapter 11 ended with a crowd who died in faith, not having received the promise, God having provided something better. Then 12:1: therefore, surrounded by this cloud, lay aside the weight and the sin, run with patience the race set before you. Our verses are the how of that running.

What follows is the Father’s discipline. You have forgotten the exhortation that addresses you as sons. The same Jesus who endured the cross is the reason the hardship in verses 5-11 is fatherly and not random. Looking to Him does not exempt you from the later paragraph. It keeps you from reading the later paragraph as abandonment.

These readers have been slandered and plundered. They are weary. The writer does not tell them the hostility will stop. He tells them to do math with a crucified and seated Christ so that the hostility does not get to define the size of the race.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin’s chapter on the cross of the Christian is this passage in pastoral form. The consolation is fellowship with His sufferings, not the removal of yours.

Hence it affords us great consolation in hard and difficult circumstances, which men deem evil and adverse, to think that we are holding fellowship with the sufferings of Christ; that as he passed to celestial glory through a labyrinth of many woes, so we too are conducted thither through various tribulations.

Calvin, Institutes III.8

Labyrinth of many woes then celestial glory is verse 2’s cross then throne. The writer of Hebrews and Calvin are doing the same thing to a tired man: they will not let him treat his trouble as a detour from the path. It is the path, because it was His.

Calvin also refused to make the cross a piece of theater.

It becomes us, therefore (as Ambrose truly teaches), boldly to profess the agony of Christ, if we are not ashamed of the cross.

Calvin, Institutes II.16

If He only seemed to suffer, then “consider Him” is a meditation on a costume. The shame He despised was a real shame. The contradiction of sinners was a real contradiction. A man fainting in his soul needs a real predecessor, not an illustration.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession names Him with this verse’s title when it talks about the faith that gets the victory.

This faith, although it be different in degrees, and may be weak or strong, yet it is in the least degree of it different in the kind or nature of it, as is all other saving grace, from the faith and common grace of temporary believers; and therefore, though it may be many times assailed and weakened, yet it gets the victory; growing up in many to the attainment of a full assurance through Christ, who is both the author and finisher of our faith.

1689 LBCF, ch. 14 — Of Saving Faith

Assailed and weakened, yet it gets the victory. That is a fainting soul’s confession, and the through is a Person. He is not the mascot of your faith. He is its beginner and its ender. The looking of verse 2 is how weak faith is kept from becoming another kind of thing.

Then the session, without which the looking has no landing:

This office the Lord Jesus did most willingly undertake, which that he might discharge he was made under the law, and did perfectly fulfill it, and underwent the punishment due to us, which we should have borne and suffered, being made sin and a curse for us; enduring most grievous sorrows in his soul, and most painful sufferings in his body; was crucified and died, and remained in the state of the dead, yet saw no corruption: on the third day he arose from the dead with the same body in which he suffered, with which he also ascended into heaven, and there sits at the right hand of his Father making intercession.

1689 LBCF, ch. 8 — Of Christ the Mediator

Sits. The race has a seated Man at the end of it, still working, still interceding. You run toward an occupied chair, not toward an empty idea of finishing.

5. For the Man Reading This

If you are weary, the verse does not first tell you to try harder. It tells you to change what you are looking at. Most exhaustion in a long obedience is not lack of duty. It is a soul that has been staring at the opposition, or at other runners, or at its own remaining sin, until those things have become the largest objects in the field. Look away. The cloud is real. It is not the mark.

Then do the slower work: consider Him. That is not a glance. It is the arithmetic — He endured this contradiction; you have not yet bled; the shame He treated as small is larger than yours; He is already seated. The writer is not shaming you for being tired. He is giving you a fact pattern that makes fainting unnecessary.

And take the joy set before Him as the thing that ranks your shame too. He did not endure because He enjoyed pain. He endured for a joy. The throne is that joy, and the many sons, and the finished faith of people who would have quit. Your remaining under this week is not an orphan effort. It is a man still in the race whose pioneer is already home, and who has not stopped being the finisher just because you cannot presently feel the finish.
