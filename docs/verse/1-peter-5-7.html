Verse Study · 1 Peter

1 Peter 5:7

Two different Greek words, both translated “care.” Yours is the kind that splits a man. His is the kind that stays up with the flock. Peter tells you to throw the first onto the second.

casting all your worries on him, because he cares for you.— World English Bible (public domain)

Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The sentence is a participle hanging off the one before it. Humble yourselves under the mighty hand of God. Casting all your care on Him, because He cares for you. It is not a second project. It is how the humbling gets done. A man who keeps the anxiety in his own hands has not yet come under the hand he was told to humble himself under.

English hides a distinction the Greek will not. Your care is merimna — the divided mind, the anxious split. His care is melei — it matters to Him; you are His concern. Peter is not telling you God is a worrier. He is telling you that the thing chewing you is, to God, a fact about a son.

The verse is almost Psalm 55:22 in Christian clothes: cast your burden on the Lord, and He will sustain you. Peter, who knew what it was to sink, is not inventing a new piety. He is handing a hunted church an old sentence.

  • ἐπιρρίπτω epirrhiptō · “casting” · Strong’s G1977

    A verb meaning to throw or cast something upon someone — to transfer a burden, weight, or responsibility onto another. Used once in the New Testament in 1 Peter 5:7, with profound theological significance.

    Epiripto is to throw upon, to heave a load onto someone else. It is a physical verb. Peter does not say “share your feelings” or “try to release.” He says throw. The care has weight, and it is supposed to change shoulders. A man can talk about trust all day and still be wearing the pack. This verb will not call that trust.

  • Merimna · “care” · Strong’s G3308

    Anxious care or worry — the divided mind pulled in multiple directions, contrasted with the single-minded trust Paul commands in Philippians 4:6. Usage & Theological Significance The Greek merimna (from merizō, to divide + nous, mind) literally means a divided mind — the state of being pulled in multiple directions by worry.

    Merimna is anxious care, the mind pulled in more than one direction. It is the word Jesus used when He said not to be anxious for your life, and the word Paul used when he said be anxious for nothing. It is not ordinary prudence. It is the circling that keeps you from sleep and from prayer. Peter says all of it. He does not leave you a private stash of respectable worries that you are allowed to keep as proof you are responsible.

  • μέλω melō · “careth” · Strong’s G3199

    To be of concern, to matter, to care about — an impersonal verb used to ask whether someone genuinely cares. Appears in some of Scripture's most emotionally raw moments.

    Melei is to be a concern to someone, to matter. It appears in some of the rawest questions in the Gospels — do you not care that we perish? Here it is not a question. It is the ground. For He careth for you. The reason you can throw the merimna is not that the merimna was imaginary. It is that you are already a concern of His. The humble man of verse 6 is not unnoticed under that mighty hand.

  • πᾶς pas · “all” · Strong’s G3956

    All, every, each, the whole. Pas is the most common adjective in the NT, appearing over 1,200 times.

    Pas again: all, every, nothing parked to the side. The exception you are about to make — this one is too practical, this one is mine to solve, this one would be irresponsible to release — is the one the adjective was written to kill. Casting some of your care is how a man stays in charge and calls it faith.

The grammar that keeps it from being a poster

If you lift verse 7 out of verse 6 you get a relaxation technique. Peter fastened it to humility under a mighty hand, and then fastened verse 8 to it: be sober, be vigilant, the lion is walking. The man who has thrown his anxiety is not a man who has gone off watch. He has changed who is carrying the thing that was making him useless on watch.

2. Where It Sits

The letter is to exiles. Slander, isolation, the slow heat of being the wrong kind of people in a town. Chapter 5 has just told elders how to shepherd, the younger how to submit, everyone how to put on humility, because God resists the proud and gives grace to the humble. Verse 6 is that proverb turned into an act: get under the hand, He will exalt you in due time.

Due time is doing work. The exaltation is not scheduled by your panic. Casting the care is how you stop trying to hurry the due time. Then the lion, because a church that has just been told to relax its grip is a church that can be picked off if it also goes to sleep.

Peter is the right man for this sentence. He had been told Satan asked to sift him. He had slept in the garden when he was told to watch. He had been anxious enough to swing a sword and then to lie. He is not writing as a temperamentally calm person. He is writing as a man who learned, late, where the care belongs.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin files this verse under special providence — not a general rumor that God is kind, but a particular watching over the safety of believers.

The fact that a special providence watches over the safety of believers, is attested by a vast number of the clearest promises.

Calvin, Institutes I.17

He lists 1 Peter 5:7 among those promises, with Psalm 55. The tradition refused to let “He cares” mean only a feeling in God. It is a providence that actually governs the things you were about to stay up managing. If the care you throw never lands anywhere but in the air, the verse is cruel. Calvin will not have that.

The other half is what you do instead of circling.

In particular, it must be our anxious care, whenever we are ourselves pressed, or see others pressed by any strait, instantly to have recourse to him not only with quickened pace, but with quickened minds.

Calvin, Institutes III.20

Calvin is willing to keep the word care if it is retargeted. The anxious energy does not have to be pretended away. It has to run to Him, at speed. That is epiripto in pastoral English: not the extinction of concern, the transfer of it.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession cites this verse under adoption, which is the right chapter. You do not throw cares onto a supervisor. You throw them onto a Father.

All those who are justified, God has graciously made partakers of the grace of adoption, in and for the sake of his only Son Jesus Christ. By this grace they are taken into the number of the children of God and enjoy their liberties and privileges. They have his name put upon them, receive the Spirit of adoption, have access to the throne of grace with boldness, and are enabled to cry, Abba, Father. They are pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened by him as by a Father, yet never cast off.

1689 LBCF, ch. 12 — Of Adoption

Pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened. All four are care. The verse is not a promise that the mighty hand will stop being mighty, or that chastening will be reclassified as a mix-up. It is a promise that none of it is the neglect of a man God forgot. Never cast off is the pair of casting all your care. You throw the anxiety. He does not throw the child.

The catechism says the same in a shorter line:

Adoption is an act of God’s free grace, by which we are received into the number and have a right to all the privileges of the sons of God.

The Baptist Catechism, Q. 37

He careth for you is one of those privileges. It is not a personality trait of people who worry less. It is a legal and familial fact about the adopted.

5. For the Man Reading This

The first honesty is to name the care instead of calling it planning. If it has kept you from sleep, from prayer, from the person in front of you, it is merimna, and Peter is talking to you. The responsible man is the one most likely to smuggle it back in as duty. The verse does not praise that.

Throwing it is an act, and it often has to be done more than once, because the pack has a way of climbing back on between noon and dark. That does not mean the first throw failed. It means you have a Father, and you are still the kind of son who tries to carry the house. Humble yourselves — again — under the same hand.

Then stay awake. The next verse is not a cancellation of this one. A lion is easier to panic a man who is already exhausted by a load he was told to set down. The care you keep is not making you more vigilant. It is making you slower, harsher, easier to scatter. Peter wants the care on Him and your eyes open. Those two are the same humility: you are not the one holding the flock, and you are also not allowed to nap as if no one were hunting it.
