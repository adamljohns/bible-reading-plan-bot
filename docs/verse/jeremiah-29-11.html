Verse Study · Jeremiah

Jeremiah 29:11

The most quoted verse in the letter, and the one most often cut free of the seventy years. God knows the thoughts. He also named the calendar.

For I know the thoughts that I think toward you,” says the LORD, “thoughts of peace, and not of evil, to give you hope and a future.— World English Bible (public domain)

For I know the thoughts that I think toward you, saith the LORD, thoughts of peace, and not of evil, to give you an expected end. expected: Heb. end and expectation— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

For I know. The comfort is not that you know the plan. It is that He does. The people who first heard this were not choosing a future. They were already in Babylon, told to build houses, and warned not to believe the prophets who promised a short stay. Into that, the LORD does not hand them a map. He says He knows His own thoughts toward them.

The King James keeps thoughts, not the later English habit of “plans,” and the margin on the last phrase is the other correction: expected: Heb. end and expectation. Two words sit there — a latter end, and a hope. WEB says “hope and a future.” Same pair. Neither word is a feeling. Both are a destination God intends to give, after the years He has already numbered.

  • יָדַע Yada · “know” · Strong’s H3045

    The Hebrew verb yada means to know, perceive, discern, or be acquainted with. It is one of the most frequently used verbs in the Old Testament, occurring nearly 950 times.

    Yada is knowledge that is experiential and relational, not a bare inventory of facts. When God says He knows the thoughts He thinks toward you, He is not claiming information. He is claiming acquaintance with His own counsel as it bears on this people. He is not guessing. He is not discovering. The exile has not surprised Him into a new idea.

    That is the first thing the verse will not let a man do: treat his confusion as if God were confused too. You may not know. He does. The knowing is His.

  • מַחֲשָׁבָה machashabah · “thoughts” · Strong’s H4284

    From chashab (H2803, to think, plan, devise), machashabah means the inward thought life — particularly purposeful planning and scheming, whether evil or good. It covers the full range from artistic design (Bezalel's skilled craftsmanship) to wicked plots to God's eternal purposes.

    Machashabah is the inward thought as purpose — a formed design, for good or for ill. Isaiah will say God’s thoughts are not yours, and higher. Jeremiah uses the same family of words here in a tighter circle: the thoughts I think toward you. Not thoughts in the air. Thoughts with a people as their object.

    The word will not carry the modern load we put on “I have plans for you” — career, spouse, the next town. These are the LORD’s formed purposes for a covenant people in a specific disaster. If your reading of the verse cannot survive being spoken to a whole community under judgment, it is not this word yet.

  • רַע ra · “evil” · Strong’s H7451

    A Hebrew adjective and noun meaning evil, bad, wicked, harmful, displeasing, injurious, distressing. It covers the full range of badness — moral evil (wickedness), physical evil (disaster, calamity), aesthetic badness (ugly), and relational harm (displeasure).

    Ra covers moral wickedness and also disaster, harm, calamity. “Thoughts of peace, and not of evil” is not a claim that nothing painful will occur. They are in ra already — city lost, temple burned, children growing up beside canals that are not Jordan. The contrast is about the aim of God’s counsel, not the contents of the next decade. He is not toward them as an enemy finishing them off. He is toward them as a God who intends shalom as the end, which is why verse 10 can mention seventy years in the same breath without apology.

    If you need “not of evil” to mean “not of hardship,” you will have to tear this verse out of the letter that contains it.

  • תִּקְוָה Tikvah · “expected” · Strong’s H8615

    Tikvah means hope, expectation, or cord. It appears about 35 times.

    Tikvah is hope, expectation — and also a cord. Rahab’s scarlet line is the same word-family: something you hold that holds you. Hope in this verse is not optimism. It is a tether to a future God has spoken. The exiles are told to wait seventy years. Tikvah is what keeps a man from either the false prophets' shortcut or the despair that says there is no end.

    A cord is useless if it is not fastened. This one is fastened to verse 10: I will visit you, and perform my good word, and cause you to return. Cut that, and “hope” is just a mood you were told to have.

  • אַחֲרִית acharit · “end” · Strong’s H319

    The noun acharit comes from the root achar (behind, after) and refers to the latter end, the final state, or the ultimate outcome of something or someone. The phrase acharit ha-yamim (the end of days) is one of the Bible's foundational eschatological terms, pointing to the climactic fulfillment of God's redemptive purposes in history.

    Acharit is the latter end, the outcome, the future as what comes after. The beloved use of this verse in English is “a future and a hope.” The Hebrew is more blunt: an end, and an expectation. God is giving them a latter part that is not identical with the present part. Babylon is a chapter. It is not the book.

    That is mercy, and it is also a refusal. An expected end means this life in exile is going somewhere God knows, and it also means you are not entitled to treat the middle as if it were already the end. The false prophets offered an acharit of two years. God offered seventy, and then home. Only one of those was a thought of peace.

2. Where It Sits

Verse 11 is a for. It grounds verse 10. After seventy years at Babylon I will visit you and perform my good word toward you, in causing you to return to this place. For I know the thoughts… Then verses 12-14: then you will call, pray, seek with all the heart, and I will be found, and I will turn the captivity and gather you.

The grammar is the doctrine. The thoughts of peace are the reason the seventy years will end in a visit, not the reason the seventy years were a misunderstanding. God knew the thoughts while He caused the carrying (verse 4, verse 7). Peace is the aim of the whole, including the judgment. It is not a separate, nicer track you can switch onto by quoting the verse hard enough.

The addressees are “you” as the captives named in verse 4 — all that are carried away, whom I have caused to be carried from Jerusalem to Babylon. Graduation cards have a different “you.” The letter does not. That does not make the verse useless to a later man in Christ. It means the way it becomes yours is through the same God, the same kind of counsel, and the same refusal to lie about the middle. Romans will say all things work together for good to those who love God, to those called according to His purpose. That is the apostolic cousin. It still will not mean that every desire was the purpose.

Hananiah is still in the previous chapter, dead for promising a short yoke. Verse 8-9 still sit between the gardens and this promise: do not let your prophets deceive you; I have not sent them. Any use of 29:11 that sounds like those prophets — soon, easy, I know the plans and they look like yours — has changed sides in the argument.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed reading heard I know the thoughts as decree and providence, not as a personalized itinerary. God has, from eternity, a counsel. It is wise, free, and unchangeable. It does not make Him the author of sin, and it does not make the exile an accident He is now scrambling to redeem. The comfort is that the people’s story is inside that counsel, including the part that hurts.

They also kept the corporate shape. The “you” is the remnant in Babylon, the church in embryo under the old covenant, a people God is not done with. Special care for the church is how the confession will later name it. That is a stronger comfort than “God has a wonderful plan for my life” as a private slogan, because a wonderful plan that can fail when you fail is not Jeremiah 29. The thoughts are His, known to Him, aimed at peace, and they hold a whole people through seventy years of ordinary, unspectacular obedience.

The tradition likewise refused to let “not of evil” erase the rod. Hebrews will say whom the Lord loves He chastens. Jeremiah 29 is that in historical form. Peace is the end. The means include a city you must pray for and a wait you cannot shorten. A gospel that cannot say both is not the letter, and it is not the God of the letter.

4. Confessional Anchor

God has decreed in himself, from all eternity, by the most wise and holy counsel of his own will, freely and unchangeably, all things, whatsoever comes to pass; yet so as thereby is God neither the author of sin nor has fellowship with any therein; nor is violence offered to the will of the creature, nor yet is the liberty or contingency of second causes taken away, but rather established; in which appears his wisdom in disposing all things, and power and faithfulness in accomplishing his decree.

1689 LBCF, ch. 3 — Of God's Decree

I know the thoughts that I think is this paragraph in a single line of Hebrew. The thoughts are not being composed in response to Babylon. They were the counsel. Second causes remain — Nebuchadnezzar, gardens, prayers, seventy years of weather — and they do not take the decree down. Wisdom in disposing, faithfulness in accomplishing: that is why an expected end can be promised to people who cannot see it yet.

As the providence of God does in general reach to all creatures, so after a more special manner it takes care of his church, and disposes of all things to the good thereof.

1689 LBCF, ch. 5 — Of Divine Providence

Toward you is not a promise that every human future is a thought of peace. It is special care of the people He has claimed. The church does not float above disaster. It is disposed through all things toward its good. Verse 11 is that sentence spoken to one generation of that people, with a date attached. If you belong to Christ, you inherit the God of this letter, not a guarantee that your particular five-year plan was the machashabah. You inherit a Father who knows His own mind, whose aim is shalom, and who does not lose a people in a city He sent them to.

5. For the Man Reading This

Leave the seventy years on. If you cannot say this verse and verse 10 in the same breath, you are not quoting Jeremiah. You are quoting a souvenir. The God who thinks peace toward His people is the God who thinks in numbered years, planted gardens, and prayers for a pagan city. Your “expected end” may include a long middle that does not look like peace. The verse already knew that. It was written there.

Then stop using the verse to baptize a desire God has not spoken. Thoughts of peace are His thoughts, known to Him, not a mirror. Test a claimed “plan” against the rest of the letter: does it make you build and plant where you are, pray for the place, refuse the shortcut prophet, and seek the LORD with the whole heart? Or does it mainly make you restless for a different life you have decided He owes you? The first is this chapter. The second is closer to the dreams they caused to be dreamed.

Hold tikvah as a cord, not a mood. Hope that cannot wait is not hope. It is a demand with a Bible verse taped to it. The exiles' hope was specific: He will visit, He will perform His good word, He will bring you back. Your hope, if you are in Christ, is as specific and larger: He will finish what He has started, all things working together for good to those who love Him, the latter end not cut off. That hope will carry a man through a decade that does not photograph well. It will not always carry him through a decade that matches the postcard.

And if you are in a real exile — the job gone, the place you did not choose, the life that feels like a carrying — do not hear this as a scold. Hear verse 10 first, then 11, then 12. He will visit. He knows. Then you will call, and He will listen. The thoughts of peace are not a reason to stop praying. They are the reason praying is not shouting into a hole. Seek Him. The letter says you will find Him when you search with all your heart. That is harder than framing the verse. It is also what the verse was for.
