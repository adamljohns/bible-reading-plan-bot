Verse Study · Philippians

Philippians 2:19

After the highest Christology in the letter, Paul talks about a travel plan. That is not a drop in subject. Hope located in the Lord Jesus is how the hymn becomes a man on a road.

But I hope in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy to you soon, that I also may be cheered up when I know how you are doing.— World English Bible (public domain)

But I trust in the Lord Jesus to send Timotheus shortly unto you, that I also may be of good comfort, when I know your state. But: or, Moreover— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The King James says I trust. The WEB says I hope. Both are translating elpizo, and both are right if you will not let English split them. Paul is not crossing his fingers. He is placing a real expectation in a Person, about a real sending, for a real church, so that he himself may be cheered when he knows how they are.

The sentence is logistics on the surface. Underneath it is a doctrine of hope, of means, and of the church. He does not hope in Timothy’s talent. He hopes in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy. Timothy is the gift. The Lord is the sender.

  • ἐλπίζω Elpizo · “I trust” · Strong’s G1679

    The Greek verb elpizo means to hope, to place confidence in, or to have an expectation of good. It is the verbal form of elpis (G1680, hope) and describes the active, forward-looking trust that characterizes Christian hope.

    Elpizo is to hope, to place confidence, to expect good. It is the verb of elpis. In Paul, hope is not the opposite of knowledge. It is confidence stretched toward a future the Lord still holds. Here the object is not “that things work out.” It is the Lord Jesus, and the content is a sending. A man can plan. This verse will not let the plan be the thing trusted.

  • κύριος kyrios · “the Lord” · Strong’s G2962

    Appearing over 700 times, kyrios is a paramount christological title. In everyday Greek: "sir" or "master." In the Septuagint, it translates both Adonai and the divine name YHWH (H3068).

    Kyrios is Lord — master, and in the Greek Old Testament the name of God. Paul does not say he hopes in circumstances, or in the empire’s roads, or in his own chains loosening. He hopes in the Lord, and then names Him: Jesus. The One who emptied Himself six verses earlier is the One who governs whether a coworker gets to Philippi. The hymn was not a poem you leave in the air. It is the government of Tuesday.

  • πέμπω pempo · “to send” · Strong’s G3992

    Pempo (πέμπω) means to send or dispatch — to cause someone to go, to commission and release. It is one of two primary Greek words for 'send' (alongside apostello), and the distinction between them is theologically significant in John's Gospel: pempo emphasizes the authority and act of the one sending, while apostello emphasizes the official commission and authority given to the one sent.

    Pempo is to send, to dispatch, to commission and release. Apostles send. Churches receive. The gospel moves by people who are actually told to go. Paul cannot presently go. Verse 24 says he trusts he will come later. Verse 19 is the meantime: a sent man. Christianity that will not send, or will not be sent, has already decided the Lord does not work by means.

  • εὐψυχέω eupsucheo · “may be of good comfort” · Strong’s G2174

    To be of good courage, to be cheered, to have one's spirit lifted. Literally 'to have a good soul' (eu + psyche).

    Eupsucheo is to be of good soul, to be cheered, to have the spirit lifted. Paul wants comfort, and he locates it in their state, not in his release. That is a pastor’s sentence. His own chains are not the first thermometer. The church’s health is. He will be of good comfort when he knows — which means he does not know yet, and he will not pretend.

  • γινώσκω ginōskō · “when I know” · Strong’s G1097

    To know through experience, observation, or relationship — a knowing that involves personal engagement, not just intellectual awareness. Distinguished from oida (intuitive knowledge), ginōskō implies a process of coming to know, discovering, and recognizing.

    Ginosko is to know by acquaintance, by finding out, not by a rumor. He wants Timothy’s report. Distance is not an excuse for ignorance. The apostle of this church still considers himself responsible to know how they are, and he will spend a man to find out. Knowledge here is love with a date of travel.

The man he means to send

Verse 19 is incomplete without 20-22. No one else like-minded, who will genuinely care for their state. All seek their own, not the things of Jesus Christ. Timothy has proof: as a son with a father he served in the gospel. The hope in verse 19 is not generic optimism. It is hope attached to a tested man whose mind looks like the hymn.

2. Where It Sits

Philippians 2 has just gone as high as the New Testament goes. Have this mind in you which was also in Christ Jesus — who emptied Himself, took a servant’s form, became obedient to death, even the cross; therefore God highly exalted Him. Then: work out your salvation. Then: do all things without murmuring. Then: lights in a crooked generation, holding the word of life. Then Paul as a drink offering, and mutual joy.

Then Timothy.

The drop is only in our ears. The mind of Christ, just defined, is now a personnel decision. Who can be sent? The man who does not seek his own. Who cannot? The rest, even if they are around Paul in the city of his imprisonment. The hymn sorts a staff.

Epaphroditus follows immediately, sick nearly to death, to be received with honor. The chapter that began with Christ’s descent ends with two men who went, at cost, for a church. Verse 19 is the hinge between the doctrine and the names.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition refused to treat hope as a separate faculty from faith, as if you believed God for salvation and then hoped in luck for everything else. Hope is faith with its face turned toward what is not yet in hand.

hope is nothing else than the food and strength of faith

Calvin, Institutes III.2

And, on this same letter:

in hoping patiently we suspend our wishes until God manifest his own time

Calvin, Institutes III.2

That is verse 19 without the name Timothy. Paul has a wish: send him soon. He suspends it in the Lord. Shortly is still a request, not a claim. Verse 23 will wait until he sees how it goes with himself. Hope in the Lord Jesus includes the Lord’s timing, which may not match the apostle’s soon.

The tradition also read such sendings under providence and under the communion of saints. God governs the road. He also binds the saints to spend their gifts on one another’s good. Timothy’s gifts are not his private stock. They are for Philippi, as God offers opportunity.

4. Confessional Anchor

And being united to one another in love, they have communion in each other’s gifts and graces, and are bound to the performance of such duties, public and private, as in an orderly way conduce to their mutual good, both in the inward and outward man.

1689 LBCF, ch. 27 — Of the Communion of Saints

Bound. Not inspired, if they feel like it. Paul hoping to send Timothy is that article in motion. The inward man: they need care, teaching, a like-minded soul. The outward: a man on a road, a church that will receive him. Mutual good is not a mood in the confession. It is duties, performed.

God, in his ordinary providence, makes use of means, yet is free to work without, above, and against them at his pleasure.

1689 LBCF, ch. 5 — Of Divine Providence

Hope in the Lord and the sending of Timothy are not two plans. The Lord is free to cheer Paul some other way. He ordinarily uses a means, and the means has a name. A man who will pray and not send, or send and not pray, has split what this verse holds together.

5. For the Man Reading This

Locate your next ordinary hope. Not your salvation — that is not what this verse is testing. The trip, the hire, the man you need, the news you want from your people. If that hope is in the arrangement, you will rage when the arrangement slips. Paul put the same kind of hope in the Lord Jesus and then named a means. Do both. Do not baptize passivity as trust, and do not baptize anxiety as planning.

Then ask whether you are Timothy or the men of verse 21. They all seek their own, not the things which are Jesus Christ’s. The hymn is still in the room. A like-minded man is rare enough that Paul can say he has no one else. If your church’s state would not cheer you, and you would not spend a trusted man to find out, you are not in this verse yet. You are in a private life that happens to include a congregation.

And be willing to be sent, or to receive the one who is. Communion of saints is not a theory about invisible unity. It is a knock at the door, a report about how you are, a son in the faith who came because someone hoped in the Lord Jesus to send him. Honor that. It cost somebody.
