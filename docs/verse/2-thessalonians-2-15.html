Verse Study · 2 Thessalonians

2 Thessalonians 2:15

Stand fast and hold what you were taught.

1. The Text and Its Words

Therefore, brethren, stand fast, and hold the traditions which ye have been taught, whether by word, or our epistle.

— King James Version (public domain)

So then, brothers, stand firm, and hold the traditions which you were taught by us, whether by word, or by letter.

— World English Bible (public domain)

Five words carry the verse. Every Strong's number below is the one the Strong's-tagged King James text puts on that word, and each links to its lexicon entry.

  • stand fast — stēkō, G4739. To stand firm, to hold one's ground. The lexicon traces it to a later form of the perfect of histēmi, "to stand". In this verse it is a command given to a church that has just been shaken.

  • hold — krateō, G2902. To hold firmly, seize, take hold of. It is a gripping word. The lexicon's own examples include arresting a man and holding a teaching. So this is not a loose acquaintance with the teaching. It means taking hold of it and refusing to let go.

  • traditions — paradosis, G3862. From paradidomi, "to hand over, deliver". It means what has been handed down. The lexicon says the word itself is neutral and the value depends on the content: Jesus critiques human paradosis that nullifies God's word (Mark 7:8), and Paul commands believers to hold fast to apostolic paradosis.

  • ye have been taught — didaskō, G1321. To teach, to instruct. The traditions here are not customs that collected over time. They are content that was taught on purpose.

  • epistle — epistolē, G1992. A letter. The KJV tags "word" in this verse as G3056, logos.

2. Where It Sits

The verse opens with "Therefore". Paul is drawing a conclusion, and the argument behind it starts at verse 1.

The Thessalonians had been rattled. Verse 2 (KJV): "That ye be not soon shaken in mind, or be troubled, neither by spirit, nor by word, nor by letter as from us, as that the day of Christ is at hand." Someone was telling them the Day had already come, and was claiming Paul's authority for it.

Look at the tags. In verse 2, "word" is G3056 and "letter" is G1992. In verse 15, "word" is again G3056 and "epistle" is again G1992. The same two channels appear in both verses. A word and a letter falsely claiming to be "as from us" had shaken them. The cure is the word and the letter that really are from us. Paul does not tell them to stop listening to messages and letters. He tells them to know which ones are his and to hold those. A forged letter is not answered with silence. It is answered with the true one.

The verbs line up the same way. "Shaken" in verse 2 is G4531, σαλεύω, saleúō: to waver, agitate, rock, topple. Against it, verse 15 gives G4739, stēkō: stand fast. A man who is being rocked is told to plant his feet.

Verses 13-14 come between the two, and they are the ground of the command. God "hath from the beginning chosen you to salvation through sanctification of the Spirit and belief of the truth: Whereunto he called you by our gospel." The command to stand rests on God's choosing. Paul says what God has done first, and only then tells them what to do. Choosing and calling come before standing.

Then verses 16-17 turn the command into a prayer: may the Lord Jesus Christ and God our Father "Comfort your hearts, and stablish you in every good word and work." "Stablish" is G4741, στηρίζω, stērízō: to set fast, to confirm. The lexicon traces the form to a presumed derivative of G2476 (ἵστημι). Verse 15 tells the man to stand. Verse 17 asks God to make him stand. Paul commands the duty, and then he prays for the grace to do it. That is how Scripture works: the command does not make the obedience happen; it points the man to the One who does.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Scripture is the first witness, and it says two things that seem at first to pull against each other.

Against tradition, Jesus says (Mark 7:8, KJV): "For laying aside the commandment of God, ye hold the tradition of men." The noun is paradosis, the same word as in our verse (the G3862 lexicon page sets Mark 7:8 and 2 Thess 2:15 side by side). The English verb is the same too: hold. The Greek verb is the same as well: the Strong's-tagged King James text puts G2902 on "hold" in Mark 7:8, just as it does here. The Pharisees held tightly, but they were holding the wrong thing.

For tradition, Paul writes (1 Corinthians 11:2, KJV): "Now I praise you, brethren, that ye remember me in all things, and keep the ordinances, as I delivered them to you." And in this same letter (2 Thessalonians 3:6, KJV) he commands them to withdraw "from every brother that walketh disorderly, and not after the tradition which he received of us."

So the issue was never whether to have a tradition. Every man is holding something that was handed to him. The question is whose it is. A tradition of men sets the commandment of God aside. A tradition from the apostles is the commandment of God, delivered.

This verse has been argued over for centuries. It has been used to claim that an unwritten stream of apostolic teaching runs alongside Scripture with equal authority. The text does not carry that weight. Paul is speaking to one church, about teaching they had already received from him in person and by letter, and he is defending it against a forgery. He names no second source still to come. The question for us is where that same apostolic teaching is found today, now that the apostles are dead. The confession answers it below.

Calvin reads the word the same way. Some in his day limited "traditions" here to rules of outward church order. He would not, because Paul is telling a shaken church how to keep its footing, and that takes more than order.

"Hence, in my opinion, he includes all doctrine under this term, as though he had said that they have ground on which they may stand firm, provided they persevere in sound doctrine, according as they had been instructed by him."

Calvin, Commentary on Thessalonians, on 2:15 (public domain)

He then points to the verse itself. Paul names the channels, word and epistle, and the epistles we have contain the apostle's doctrine. That is where we learn which traditions Paul meant.

4. Confessional Anchor

The 1689 London Baptist Confession, chapter 1, paragraph 6 (usmcmin.org/lbcf/chapter-01.html):

The whole counsel of God concerning all things necessary for his own glory, man's salvation, faith, and life, is either expressly set down or necessarily contained in the Holy Scripture; to which nothing at any time is to be added, whether by new revelation of the Spirit or traditions of men.

1689 LBCF, ch. 1

Notice what the confession does. It does not reject paradosis. It rejects "traditions of men", which is the very distinction Mark 7 and 2 Thessalonians 2 draw. In paragraph 1 it says where the apostles' tradition went: it pleased the Lord "to commit the same wholly to writing." What the Thessalonians received "by word, or our epistle," we receive in the written Word. The spoken channel has closed. What it carried has not been lost. It was written down.

The Baptist Catechism, Q4 (usmcmin.org/catechism.html#q4):

The holy Scriptures of the Old and New Testament are the Word of God, and the only certain rule of faith and obedience.

The Baptist Catechism, Q4

The "only certain rule" is what a shaken man holds onto. Everything else that comes "as from us" gets weighed against it.

5. For the Man Reading This

You are being shaken. Maybe not about the Day of Christ, but by something that arrives "as from" somewhere it did not come from. A voice that says it speaks for God. A sermon clip. A feed. A man you respect, saying something the Book does not say. It may sound like the real thing. Paul's answer is not to panic and not to stop listening. It is to know what you were actually taught and hold it hard.

That means three things in practice.

Know what you hold. You cannot hold what you have never been taught. Didaskō is in the verse: these traditions were taught. If you have never read a confession and never worked through a catechism, then you are holding fog. Pick up the Baptist Catechism and learn the first six questions this week.

Grip, do not browse. Krateō is the word for arresting a man. Hold the truth that firmly. A man who only skims the Word will be rocked by the next confident voice he hears.

Hand it down. Paradosis means something handed over. It came to you from someone. Your sons are owed it next. If the tradition stops with you, it was not a tradition. It was a hobby. A father who teaches his boys the catechism at the table is doing exactly what this verse describes.

And do not grip in your own strength. Verse 17 is Paul's prayer: God "stablish you." You stand fast because He set you fast, and He chose you first (verse 13).
