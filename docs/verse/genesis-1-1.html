Verse Study · Genesis

Genesis 1:1

Seven words in Hebrew. Everything else in Scripture stands on them.

In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.

— World English Bible (public domain)

In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.

— King James Version (public domain)

בְּרֵאשִׁית בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים אֵת הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ

1. The Text and Its Words

Moses does not begin with an argument for God. He begins with God already acting, and he never once stops to prove the point. That silence is deliberate. The verse assumes what the rest of the book will demonstrate, and it does so in seven Hebrew words built on three that carry the weight.

  • בְּרֵאשִׁיתbereshit — "in beginning" · Strong's H7225

    The noun is built on rosh, the word for a head — the top of a man, the chief of a tribe, the first of a series. This is not the beginning of a story already underway. It is the head of everything that is not God. Whatever else exists, it starts here; God does not.

  • בָּרָאbara — "created" · Strong's H1254

    Hebrew has ordinary words for making and forming and fashioning, and Moses uses them freely elsewhere. He does not use them here. Throughout the Hebrew Bible this verb, in this form, never takes a human subject. Men shape what they are given. Only God does this.

  • אֱלֹהִיםElohim — "God" · Strong's H430

    The form is plural; the verb attached to it is singular. Hebrew can do this — a plural of majesty is ordinary enough — but the church has never been able to read the plural noun, the singular verb, and the hovering Spirit two verses later without hearing something more than grammar.

Two smaller things repay attention. The little particle אֵת appears twice, marking a definite direct object each time: not a vague gesture at "everything," but two specific things, named and made. And "the heavens and the earth" is a merism — naming the two poles to mean the whole, the way a man says he searched high and low. There is no third category. There is God, and there is what God made.

2. Where It Sits

Every other creation account known to the ancient world begins with something already there. There is water, or chaos, or a war among the gods, or the carcass of a defeated god out of which the world gets built. The gods themselves have birthdays. They arrive partway through the story, and the raw material was waiting on them when they got there.

Genesis has none of it. No theogony, no rival, no pre-existing stuff. The absence is the argument, and it is aimed at every generation's version of the same error: the assumption that something other than God is simply, permanently there — matter, or the universe, or the self.

What the verse asserts, the church came to call creation ex nihilo — out of nothing. Not God organizing chaos into order, but God calling into existence what had no existence. Every doctrine downstream depends on it. If matter is co-eternal with God, then God is a craftsman working with what He was handed, and He is not Lord of it. If He made it from nothing, He owns it without remainder — including you.

3. What the Fathers Saw

Augustine — on the question behind the question

Someone always asks what God was doing before He made the world. Augustine takes the question seriously enough to dismantle it rather than dodge it, and his answer is that the question smuggles in its own error: it puts time outside of God, as a room He was sitting in before He got to work.

"I boldly say, That before God made heaven and earth, He made not anything."

"Before the times created by God, times were not… Or what times should those be which were not made by You?"

Augustine, Confessions XI.12–13 (public domain translation)

Time is not the stage creation happens on. Time is part of what got created. "In the beginning" is therefore the beginning of when, not a date on a calendar that was already running.

Calvin — on the discipline of the word created

Calvin is worth reading here precisely because of what he refuses to do. A preacher with his instincts could easily make "in the beginning" a reference to Christ and get a sermon out of it by lunch. He will not:

"To expound the term 'beginning,' of Christ, is altogether frivolous. For Moses simply intends to assert that the world was not perfected at its very commencement, in the manner in which it is now seen, but that it was created an empty chaos of heaven and earth."

Calvin, Commentary on Genesis (public domain)

But where the Hebrew does carry weight, he presses it hard, and he presses exactly where we did above — on the choice of verb:

"He moreover teaches by the word 'created,' that what before did not exist was now made; for he has not used the term יצר (yatsar), which signifies to frame or form, but ברא (bara), which signifies to create… Therefore his meaning is, that the world was made out of nothing."

Calvin, Commentary on Genesis (public domain)

That is the model: refuse the reading the text will not bear, and take everything it will. In the Institutes he adds why any of it matters to a man who is not a scholar — that the account exists so we can see a God we cannot otherwise look at: "that invisible God, whose wisdom, power, and justice, are incomprehensible, is set before us in the history of Moses as in a mirror, in which his living image is reflected." (Institutes I.14 →)

Matthew Henry — on the plural name and the Son

Henry states the doctrine plainly and then does what Henry always does — turns it toward worship:

"The manner in which this work was effected: God created it, that is, made it out of nothing. There was not any pre-existent matter out of which the world was produced."

"Before the beginning of time there was none but that Infinite Being that inhabits eternity."

Matthew Henry, Commentary on Genesis (public domain)

On the plural noun he is careful but unembarrassed, reading Elohim as bespeaking "the plurality of persons in the Godhead, Father, Son, and Holy Ghost," and holding it together with John's testimony that "the Son of God, the eternal Word and Wisdom of the Father, was with him when he made the world." The New Testament writers read Genesis 1:1 the same way — John 1:1–3 and Colossians 1:16 are both deliberate echoes of this verse, and both put Christ inside the act.

4. Confessional Anchor

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 4, Paragraph 1

"In the beginning it pleased God the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, for the manifestation of the glory of his eternal power, wisdom, and goodness, to create or make the world, and all things therein, whether visible or invisible, in the space of six days, and all very good."

Read Chapter 4, Of Creation →
Baptist Catechism · Question 12

Q. What is the work of creation?
A. "The work of creation is God's making all things out of nothing, by the word of his power, in the space of six days, and all very good."

Work through the Catechism →

Notice that the confession will not let the doctrine stay abstract. It gives a purpose — "for the manifestation of the glory of his eternal power, wisdom, and goodness" — and a verdict: all very good. Creation is not neutral raw material. It was made on purpose, by a Person, and He was pleased with it.

5. For the Man Reading This

Three things follow, and none of them are academic.

You are not self-made. Every man wants to believe he assembled himself — that his discipline, his work, his name are his own product. Genesis 1:1 puts a hard floor under that. You did not make yourself, you did not make the world you operate in, and the air you are using to argue the point was issued to you. Humility is not a mood to work up. It is simply an accurate reading of the situation.

Your authority is derived, and therefore accountable. A man leads a home, a shop, a squad. Genesis says every bit of that authority is on loan from the One who owns the whole estate. That cuts two ways at once: it forbids you from being a tyrant with what is not yours, and it forbids you from being passive with what you were handed. A steward who abuses the property and a steward who abandons it are both fired in the same parable.

Your work has dignity because God worked first. The Bible's opening scene is God at labor — six days of it, and He calls the result good. Whatever you do Monday, if it is honest work, it participates in something God did before there was anyone to impress. That is worth remembering on the days it feels like nothing.

And under all three, the comfort: if He made it from nothing, then nothing in your life is outside His competence. The God who began with an empty page is not short of options for the mess on yours.
