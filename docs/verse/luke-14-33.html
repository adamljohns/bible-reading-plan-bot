Verse Study · Luke

Luke 14:33

Jesus is not recruiting a crowd. He turns to the crowd He already has and tells them who cannot come. The verse is a gate, not a slogan.

So therefore whoever of you who doesn’t renounce all that he has, he can’t be my disciple.— World English Bible (public domain)

So likewise, whosoever he be of you that forsaketh not all that he hath , he cannot be my disciple.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

So therefore. The verse is a conclusion. It does not stand by itself. Two pictures have just been set down: a man who starts a tower and cannot finish it, and a king who counts troops before he rides out. Then this: whoever of you does not forsake all that he has cannot be my disciple.

The logic is blunt. Discipleship has a cost. If you will not put every owned thing on the table, you cannot pay it. Jesus is not being poetic. He is doing arithmetic in public, in front of people who were already walking with Him and had not asked what the walk would take.

  • οὖν oun · “likewise” · Strong’s G3767

    Oun is an inferential conjunction meaning "therefore," "then," "so," or "consequently." It draws logical conclusions from what has been stated. It appears about 499 times in the NT and is especially common in John and Paul, where careful argument is being built.

    Oun is therefore, then, consequently. Luke’s so likewise in the King James is this word doing its job: what follows is the inference from the tower and the war. Skip the parables and the verse becomes a floating severity. Keep them and it becomes a warning against unfinished buildings and unwinnable wars — starting with Jesus without finishing, marching under His name without the troops such a King requires.

  • ἀποτάσσομαι Apotassomai · “forsaketh” · Strong’s G657

    The Greek verb apotassomai means to say farewell, to take leave of, or to renounce and give up. It combines apo- (away from) and tasso (to arrange/order), with the reflexive form meaning to arrange oneself away from — to detach oneself.

    Apotassomai is to take leave, to bid farewell, to renounce. It is the word for arranging yourself away from something that used to be yours to arrange. Forsaking here is not a mood of dislike. It is a change of title. The things are still in the house. They are no longer the master of the man.

    That matters, because the verse is routinely read as a command to liquidate. Some men in this Gospel are told to sell. This sentence is broader and, in a way, harder. You can sell a field and still belong to the proceeds. Renouncing all that he has is the end of the claim, not necessarily the end of the inventory.

  • πᾶς pas · “whosoever” · Strong’s G3956

    All, every, each, the whole. Pas is the most common adjective in the NT, appearing over 1,200 times.

    Pas lands twice: whoever of you, and all that he has. No subclass of disciple gets a lighter version. No category of possession is fenced off — money, yes, but also the family named two verses earlier, and a man’s own life. All is the word that kills the private exception. The thing you were hoping He did not mean is inside the sentence.

  • μαθητής mathētēs · “disciple” · Strong’s G3101

    Mathētēs appears 261 times in the NT (all in the Gospels and Acts) and is the primary term for Jesus's followers. The word comes from manthanō (G3129), 'to learn.' In the Greco-Roman world, a mathētēs was not merely a student who attended lectures — they were a committed follower who lived with a teacher, imitated their practices, and devoted themselves to embodying the teacher's wisdom.

    Mathetes is a learner attached to a teacher, from manthano, to learn. In the Gospels it is the ordinary name for the people who follow Jesus, and Luke will not let the word stay casual. Three times in four verses he says cannot be my disciple. The crowd is large. The category is narrow. A disciple is not a man who admires Jesus from among the multitude. He is a man who has taken leave of his former ownership and come after Him.

What “cannot” is doing

The King James cannot is not “will find it inconvenient.” It is inability. There is a kind of following that is not discipleship, and Jesus is willing to say so while the numbers are still high. He would rather lose a crowd than train a lie.

2. Where It Sits

Luke 14 opened at a Pharisee’s table. Sabbath healing. A lecture on the best seats. A parable about a banquet whose invited guests made excuses — a field, oxen, a wife — and were replaced by the poor, the maimed, the blind, the lame. Then this: great multitudes were going with Him. He turned and said to them.

He turns. That is the posture of the whole paragraph. The movement is toward Him and He stops it with terms.

Verse 26: if you come to me and do not hate father, mother, wife, children, brothers, sisters, and your own life, you cannot be my disciple. Verse 27: whoever does not bear his own cross and come after me cannot be my disciple. Then the tower. Then the king. Then our verse. Then salt that has lost its taste, fit for neither soil nor dunghill, thrown out.

So 14:33 is the third cannot, and it is the summary of the first two. Family, cross, possessions — the same gate named three ways. The banquet’s excuses are still in the air. The things that kept men from the feast are the things that will keep men from the road.

And the salt: a disciple who will not renounce is not a milder disciple. He is salt that does not salt. Jesus is not bargaining. He is describing a thing that either is or is not.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition filed this under self-denial, not under a special monastic vocation. The demand is for disciples, which in this Gospel is the name of the people who belong to Jesus, not a second tier of professionals.

Calvin made the issue ownership.

We are not our own; therefore, neither is our own reason or will to rule our acts and counsels. We are not our own; therefore, let us not make it our end to seek what may be agreeable to our carnal nature. We are not our own; therefore, as far as possible, let us forget ourselves and the things that are ours. On the other hand, we are God’s; let us, therefore, live and die to him.

Calvin, Institutes III.7

That is Luke 14:33 in another key. Forsaking all that you have is what a man does when he has finally believed he is not his own. The tower gets finished because the builder is no longer building for himself. The king can fight because the life at stake was already handed over.

The tradition also refused two cheap readings. One: that Jesus is against families and goods as such. He is against them as rivals. The other: that a dramatic once-for-all giveaway can substitute for the daily taking-leave. A man can empty a bank account and still be unrenounced. The word is a posture, kept.

4. Confessional Anchor

Those who under pretense of Christian liberty practice any sin or cherish any sinful lust pervert the main design of the grace of the gospel to their own destruction, and wholly destroy the end of Christian liberty itself — which is that, being delivered out of the hands of all our enemies, we should serve the Lord without fear, in holiness and righteousness before him all the days of our life.

1689 LBCF, ch. 21 — Of Christian Liberty and Liberty of Conscience

Liberty in this confession is not the right to keep your life. It is being delivered so that you can serve. The end of Christian liberty is not a larger private stash. It is a man who belongs to a Lord. Luke 14:33 is what that looks like from the road: all that he has, left behind in the claim, because the new claim is my disciple.

Christ executes the office of a king in subduing us to himself, in ruling and defending us, and in restraining and conquering all his and our enemies.

The Baptist Catechism, Q. 29

Subduing us to himself. A disciple is a subdued man. The king in Jesus' second parable sits down first and counts. The King in the catechism does not negotiate joint rule. He subdues, He rules, He defends. You cannot be His disciple and remain the other king in the story. One of the two sends for conditions of peace. That is this verse, on the far side of the math.

5. For the Man Reading This

Count before you talk. That is the tower, and it is a mercy. Jesus would rather you sit down now than mock yourself later. If there is a thing you already know you will not put down — a relationship, a sum, a reputation, a private sin you have named “necessary” — you have your answer, and it is the one He gave the crowd.

Then distinguish sale from surrender. Some callings will empty your hands. Most will not. All of them require the title change. Ask, of the thing you grip hardest: if He asked, would it still be mine to refuse? If the honest answer is yes, you are not in a grey area. You are in verse 33.

Do not use family as the loophole. Verse 26 is still in the paragraph. Loving them more than Him is one of the ways you keep all that you have. The man who will not disappoint a parent, a wife, or a child in order to obey has already chosen which king he can afford to fight.

And start with the small possession, because that is where the large one is hiding. The hour. The hidden account. The plan you have not prayed about because you do not intend to hear no. Renouncing all is not a feeling you wait for. It is a series of farewells, beginning with the thing in your hand this week, because a disciple is a man who has taken leave and is still walking.
