Verse Study · Colossians

Colossians 3:23

A line that gets painted on workshop walls, usually without the sentence before it. Paul is talking to servants, and he is telling them who the work is actually for.

And whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord, and not for men,— World English Bible (public domain)

And whatsoever ye do, do it heartily , as to the Lord, and not unto men;— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The Greek is shorter than the posters. Whatever you do, work from the soul, as to the Lord and not to men. Two directions, one task. The work does not change. The audience does.

Heartily in the King James is not a mood. The phrase is ek psyches — from the soul, out of the inner life, not from the eyeballs of the man standing over you. Verse 22 has already named the counterfeit: eyeservice, as men-pleasers. This verse is that counterfeit’s replacement.

And it is still a command to work. Paul does not spiritualize the job into a feeling. He sends the man back to the task with a different Master named over it.

  • πᾶς pas · “whatsoever” · Strong’s G3956

    All, every, each, the whole. Pas is the most common adjective in the NT, appearing over 1,200 times.

    Pas is all, every, nothing excluded. In this sentence it is the ordinary everything — the unimpressive stretch of a servant’s day. Paul does not restrict the command to sacred tasks. If he had, the men he is addressing would have had almost nothing to obey it with. The whatsoever is how a gospel reaches the floor.

  • ἐργάζομαι ergazomai · “work” · Strong’s G2038

    Ergazomai is the verb for working or laboring — performing tasks with sustained effort. It can describe physical labor (Matt 21:28 — working in a vineyard), trade and commerce (Matt 25:16 — putting money to work), and spiritual activity (Jn 6:28 — doing the works God requires; Jn 9:4 — working while it is day).

    Ergazomai is to labor, to put in a day’s effort, to work a trade. The King James renders it do, and that is fair, but the verb is the working verb, not the general poieo of the first clause. Whatever you are about, work it. The Christian life in this verse is not a layer of meaning laid over inactivity. It is labor, aimed.

  • ἐκ / ἐξ ek / ex · “heartily” · Strong’s G1537

    Ek (ἐκ, used before consonants) / ex (ἐξ, used before vowels) is one of the most common Greek prepositions, occurring over 900 times in the NT. It always takes the genitive case and fundamentally expresses origin, source, or separation: "out of," "from," or "by means of." It answers the question: where did this come from?

    Ek means out of, from, sourced in. It is only half the phrase. The other half is psyche, the soul, the inner life. Work from there. Not from surveillance, not from the hope of being noticed, not from the fear of being caught idle. The source of the effort is supposed to be inside the man, because the Lord he is working for sees there.

  • κύριος kyrios · “Lord” · Strong’s G2962

    Appearing over 700 times, kyrios is a paramount christological title. In everyday Greek: "sir" or "master." In the Septuagint, it translates both Adonai and the divine name YHWH (H3068).

    Kyrios is master, and in this paragraph it is doing double duty. Verse 22: masters according to the flesh. Verse 23: the Lord. Verse 24: you serve the Lord Christ. Paul does not deny the earthly master. He names a higher one, so that the work done under a hard man is not wasted and the work done under a decent one is not an act of worship toward him.

  • ἄνθρωπος anthrōpos · “men” · Strong’s G444

    A human being, mankind generically — not specifically male (that is anēr). Anthrōpos is the general term for humanity: 'the Son of Man' (ho huios tou anthrōpou) means 'the Human One.' It appears over 550 times in the NT, making it one of the most common nouns.

    Anthropos is a human being, mankind, the generic word — here the earthly audience a servant is tempted to live for. Not unto men is not a license to ignore them. The earthly master still gives orders. What he does not get to be is the one in whose eyes the work either counts or does not. That seat is taken.

What verse 24 does to this one

The next verse is the reason: knowing that of the Lord you shall receive the reward of the inheritance; for you serve the Lord Christ. Inheritance is a strange word to hand a slave. Slaves did not inherit. Paul hands them the family share anyway, and then says the service is already going to Christ. Verse 23 is not a productivity hack. It is an inheritance text spoken in a kitchen.

2. Where It Sits

Colossians 3 has been putting a new man on: seek the things above, put to death what is earthly, put on compassion, let the peace of Christ rule. Then the household: wives, husbands, children, fathers. Then servants. Our verse is not a free-floating proverb about hustle. It is the word to the lowest person in that house.

Verse 22 is the setup: obey in all things your masters according to the flesh, not with eyeservice, as men-pleasers, but in singleness of heart, fearing God. Verse 25 will say the wrongdoer will be paid back, and there is no partiality. Then 4:1: masters, give what is just and equal, knowing you also have a Master in heaven.

Read all four verses or you will turn 3:23 into a tool for extracting more labor. Paul binds the servant’s effort to Christ and, in the next breath, binds the master’s justice to the same Christ. A church that quotes 3:23 at employees and never 4:1 at owners has not read the paragraph.

This is also not a defense of the institution. Paul addresses people inside it, as he found them, and he tells them their labor is already aimed at a Lord who will pay in inheritance, not in the master’s currency. That is a more explosive sentence than it looks.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin’s doctrine of calling was built for this verse, whether or not he is citing the number. A man’s post is assigned. The work there, however mean, is not beneath God’s notice.

This, too, will afford admirable consolation, that in following your proper calling, no work will be so mean and sordid as not to have a splendour and value in the eye of God.

Calvin, Institutes III.10

Mean and sordid is the honest vocabulary. Calvin is not talking about careers a man would put on a card. He is talking about the work a man would hide. Colossians 3:23 is written for that work. The splendour is not in the task. It is in the eye that receives it.

Every one in his particular mode of life will, without repining, suffer its inconveniences, cares, uneasiness, and anxiety, persuaded that God has laid on the burden.

Calvin, Institutes III.10

That is the other half. The verse is consolation, not a demand that you enjoy the post. Inconveniences, cares, uneasiness — Calvin names them and then says they are under superintendence. As to the Lord includes the parts of the job you would not have chosen. You do not have to pretend they are pleasant. You do have to stop living them as if they were unseen.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession will not let good works become a private invention, and it will not let them become a wage God owes you. Both matter here. The servant’s labor is obedience, not a technique for self-salvation.

These good works, done in obedience to God’s commandments, are the fruits and evidences of a true and lively faith; and by them believers manifest their thankfulness, strengthen their assurance, edify their brethren, adorn the profession of the gospel, stop the mouths of the adversaries, and glorify God; whose workmanship they are, created in Christ Jesus thereunto, that having their fruit unto holiness they may have the end eternal life.

1689 LBCF, ch. 16 — Of Good Works

Adorn the profession of the gospel is what a well-worked day can do in a house that does not believe. Paul thinks a servant’s whatsoever can do that. The confession is willing to file ordinary labor under thankfulness and under glory, which is exactly as to the Lord.

Then the guardrail:

We cannot by our best works merit pardon of sin or eternal life at the hand of God, by reason of the great disproportion that is between them and the glory to come, and the infinite distance that is between us and God, whom by them we can neither profit nor satisfy for the debt of our former sins; but when we have done all we can, we have done but our duty, and are unprofitable servants.

1689 LBCF, ch. 16 — Of Good Works

Verse 24’s inheritance is a gift, not a paycheck you earned by working heartily. You work as to the Lord because you already serve the Lord Christ, not in order to get Him to hire you. Duty, then inheritance — that order keeps the verse from becoming another law you use on yourself.

5. For the Man Reading This

If you are not a slave, do not flatter yourself that this verse is primarily about unlocking your potential. It was spoken to men who did not choose their work and could not leave it. The way it reaches a free man is not by making his job more inspiring. It is by stripping him of the audience he has been performing for.

Most of us work unto men. The review, the client, the father who would have been satisfied, the feed. That is eyeservice even when no one is in the room, because the eye we are aiming at is still human. Paul says that eye does not get to grade the day.

So do the next thing from the soul — which means: do it honestly when nobody will see, and do not inflate it when somebody will. The Lord named in the verse already sees both. A sloppy job dressed up as “I’m not a men-pleaser” is not this text. Neither is a polished job done to be admired.

And if the work is mean — really mean, the kind you would not put in a biography — Calvin’s sentence is for you, not the man with the interesting career. No work so sordid that it has no value in the eye of God, if it is the post you were given and you work it as to Christ. That is either true or this verse is a lie. Paul thinks it is true. The inheritance is already assigned. The day’s labor is how a servant says he believes that, with his hands, before anyone thanks him.
