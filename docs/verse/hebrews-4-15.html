Verse Study · Hebrews

Hebrews 4:15

A Priest who was tempted in every point and never fell.

1. The Text and Its Words

For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities; but was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin.

— King James Version (public domain)

For we don't have a high priest who can't be touched with the feeling of our infirmities, but one who has been in all points tempted like we are, yet without sin.

— World English Bible (public domain)

The Strong's numbers below are the ones the Strong's-tagged King James text puts on each word, and each links to its lexicon entry.

  • high priest — archiereus, G749. The lexicon gives it as a compound of archē, "first, chief", and hiereus, "priest". It was the title of the Jewish high priest who presided over the temple worship. Hebrews takes the title and gives it to Jesus. He is the one who offers the sacrifice, and He is also the sacrifice.

  • infirmities — astheneia, G769. From a-, "without", and sthenos, "strength". It means lack of strength: physical illness, human frailty, spiritual limitation. Notice what the verse does not say. It does not say "sins". It says "infirmities". The weakness of a creature and the guilt of a rebel are two different things, and the verse keeps them apart.

  • tempted — πειράζω, peirazō, G3985. To test, try, or tempt. To put someone to the proof. The lexicon says context decides whether it means God proving a man or Satan enticing him. Here the word covers the whole range of what our Lord went through, from the wilderness to the garden.

  • without — χωρίς, chōris, G5565. The lexicon defines it as "at a space, i.e. separately or apart from". He was tempted, and He stood apart from sin. The word marks a gap that was never crossed.

  • sin — hamartia, G266. The lexicon calls it missing God's mark, and the guilt that follows. It can mean a single act or the power and condition of sin itself.

The phrase "be touched with the feeling of" stands for a single Greek word: the tagged KJV puts one Strong's code on the whole phrase. Both the KJV and the WEB render it the same way. He is touched by what we feel. He does not simply know about it.

2. Where It Sits

The verse opens with "For". It is giving a reason, and the thing it supports comes just before.

Verse 14 (KJV): "Seeing then that we have a great high priest, that is passed into the heavens, Jesus the Son of God, let us hold fast our profession." The command is to hold fast. Verse 15 tells you why you can.

But first look further back. Verses 12-13 are the sword. The word of God is "quick, and powerful, and sharper than any twoedged sword", and "all things are naked and opened unto the eyes of him with whom we have to do." A man reading that has just been stripped. Every thought and intent of his heart lies open. The obvious question is: open before whom? Will the One who sees it all be cold to what He sees?

Verse 15 answers him. The double negative is deliberate: we have not an high priest which cannot. The writer is answering an objection the reader already has. Jesus has just been placed "passed into the heavens". A man could take that to mean He is too high to remember what dust feels like. Verse 15 closes that door. The exaltation does not create distance.

Then verse 16 draws the conclusion: "Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need." The order is sword, then priest, then throne. First the Word shows you who you are. Then the Priest receives you as you are. Then the throne gives you what you need.

The writer did not make this up here. He laid it down in chapter 2 (KJV, 2:17-18): "Wherefore in all things it behoved him to be made like unto his brethren, that he might be a merciful and faithful high priest in things pertaining to God, to make reconciliation for the sins of the people. For in that he himself hath suffered being tempted, he is able to succour them that are tempted." Chapter 5 goes straight on to the old priesthood. Every high priest taken from among men "can have compassion on the ignorant, and on them that are out of the way; for that he himself also is compassed with infirmity" (5:2, KJV). The Aaronic priest had compassion because he was weak himself, and he had to offer sacrifice for his own sins as well (5:3). Christ has the compassion of the office without the sin that went with it. That is the whole argument of 4:15 in one contrast.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Scripture is the first witness, and it sets two facts side by side that a lazy reader will try to pull apart.

The first fact is that the temptation was real. "In all points tempted like as we are." This is not a costume. The lexicon page for peirazō sends the reader to Matthew 4:1: Jesus "led up of the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted of the devil". A temptation that cannot pull at all is not a temptation. If He felt nothing, the verse is empty and the comfort in it is a lie.

The second fact is that the outcome was total. "Yet without sin." Chōris, apart from. This does not shrink the temptation. It describes how the fight ended. Follow the text's own logic. A man who gives way at the first push never finds out how hard the push could get. A man who never gives way feels its full weight to the end. Being sinless did not make Christ's temptation lighter. It means He alone carried it all the way through.

So the verse rules out two errors at once. One says He was tempted, so He must have had a sinful nature like ours. The text says without sin. The other says He was sinless, so the temptation could not have reached Him. The text says in all points … like as we are. Hold both, or you lose the Priest.

Calvin, commenting on this verse, refuses to waste time on curious questions about what Christ feels now. He keeps to what the Apostle meant the verse to do for a struggling believer: send him to the Priest instead of away from Him.

"Whenever, then, we labor under the infirmities of our flesh, let us remember that the Son of God experienced the same, in order that he might by his power raise us up, so that we may not be overwhelmed by them."

Calvin, Commentary on Hebrews, on 4:15 (public domain)

He also reads "infirmities" the way this study does. He includes the body's wants, but also the feelings of the soul: fear, sorrow, the dread of death. He notes that the words "without sin" would not have been needed unless the Apostle meant those inward feelings. In us they are always mixed with sin. In Christ they were pure.

4. Confessional Anchor

The 1689 London Baptist Confession puts the very words of this verse into its doctrine of the incarnation. Chapter 8, paragraph 2 (usmcmin.org/lbcf/chapter-08.html):

The Son of God, the second Person in the holy Trinity, being very and eternal God, the brightness of the Father's glory, of one substance and equal with him who made the world, who upholds and governs all things he has made, did, when the fullness of time was come, take upon him man's nature, with all the essential properties and common infirmities of it, yet without sin

1689 LBCF, ch. 8

"Common infirmities … yet without sin." That is Hebrews 4:15 in confessional form. The infirmities were really His. The sin never was.

The Baptist Catechism, Q25 (usmcmin.org/catechism.html):

Christ the Son of God became man by taking to himself a true body and a reasonable soul; being conceived by the power of the Holy Spirit in the womb of the Virgin Mary, and born of her, yet without sin.

The Baptist Catechism, Q25

A true body can be tired. A reasonable soul can grieve and be afraid. That is how He can be touched. And Q28 says what He does with what He feels:

Christ executes the office of a priest in his once offering up himself a sacrifice to satisfy divine justice and reconcile us to God, and in making continual intercession for us.

The Baptist Catechism, Q28

His sympathy is not a mood. It is a work of His office, and He is still carrying it on: continual intercession.

5. For the Man Reading This

The sword of verses 12-13 has already found you. Whatever you hid last night, it is "naked and opened" before Him. You cannot change that. What you can decide is which way you run.

Three things follow from this verse.

Tell weakness from sin. The verse keeps astheneia and hamartia apart, so you must too. You are tired, lonely, grieving, pulled hard by a desire that will not let go. That is infirmity, and He has felt it. Bring it to Him without shame. But when the pull becomes a yes, when you click it or say it or reach for it, that is hamartia. Repent of it. Do not call it weakness. A man who calls his sin "weakness" never repents of it. A man who calls every weakness "sin" never stops feeling guilty and never rests. The text gives you both words. Use the right one.

Kill the lie that nobody understands. The enemy's oldest play is isolation: no one has been where you are. Hebrews 4:15 says One has. In all points. If you are a man fighting sexual sin, He was tempted and did not fall. He is not disgusted that you are tempted. He is the only one who came through it clean, and He is the one praying for you. Then turn to your battle buddy, because the Priest who receives you does not mean to leave you without brothers.

Move. The verse lands on a verb. Verse 16 says come boldly. To know that He sympathizes and still keep away from Him is passivity dressed up as reverence. Do not wait until you feel clean before you pray. The throne is called the throne of grace, and the help is for the time of need. That time is now.

Teach your sons the difference. Temptation is not proof that a boy has failed. Christ was tempted. A father who shames the struggle teaches his son to hide it. A father who excuses the sin teaches him to keep it. Point him to the Priest.
