Teach Them How to Say Goodbye

LtCol Martinez’s Hogaboom essay is a leadership charge: we train arrivals hard and departures poorly. Here’s why leave-well belongs in every mentor’s kit — and what we’re doing about it.

By Adam “MOOP” Johns  ·  U.S.M.C. Ministries  ·  Published July 27, 2026
Listen to the article AI narration generated with Adam Johns’s authorized voice clone.

Read the original essay (PDF): LtCol Nicolas L. Martinez — “Teach Them How to Say Goodbye,” Marine Corps Gazette (July 2026)

I usually surprise people when I tell them retirement was not the hard part.

The hard part was watching good Marines finish twenty years wrecked — bitter about a board that never selected them, estranged from a spouse who waited too long for a husband who was always “almost home,” bodies neglected for the next fitrep cycle, souls still trying to find out what the service meant after the last salute. We train arrivals hard. We treat departures like a checkout sheet and a cake.

LtCol Nicolas L. Martinez just put that failure in print where it belongs — in the Marine Corps Gazette. His Hogaboom second-place essay, “Teach Them How to Say Goodbye,” is not nostalgia and it is not self-help. It is a leadership charge: if you lead Marines, you own how they leave.

Read the piece. Then come back. I will wait.

Read Martinez’s essay (PDF)

What Martinez saw at the Pentagon

Martinez loved his three years in the five-sided puzzle palace — “the happiest major at the Pentagon,” if the going-away plaque is allowed to testify. What he loved most was not the building. It was the lesson:

You must know how to end one’s service well.

He watched senior leaders arrive at the end of long careers frankly wrecked — promotions and commands that never came, marriages that did not survive the calendar, health that never got a fair turn in the priority stack. Pride and gratitude on one side of the ledger. Bitterness and frustration on the other. The difference was not luck. It was whether anyone had mentored them before the end how to serve — and therefore how to leave.

That is not soft talk. That is unfinished command.

Three rails he gives every leader

Martinez does not hand you a TED talk. He hands you three practices leaders can actually run in counseling, in the ready room, and at the dinner table.

1. Start with the end in mind — the real end

Covey gets a nod, but the best line in the essay is not from a business book. A mentor asked Martinez how much time he had left. Martinez answered with years to twenty. The mentor laughed:

“No… How much more time do you have until your daughter is eighteen and out of the house… That’s your real countdown, and don’t you forget it.”

Family is not a distraction from the career. Family is the bridge out of the career — if you still have one when you get there. Leaders who never force that conversation are not “mission-focused.” They are absentee mentors.

At U.S.M.C. Ministries we say it plainer: the Corps is a billet. The household is a covenant. Confuse the two and you will leave one of them poorly.

2. Set goals you own — not trophies someone else must hand you

Martinez watched sharp Marines retire bitter because they defined success as command, school, assignment, or promotion — goods that sit outside their circle of control. When the board said no, the whole story collapsed.

He contrasts that with a boss whose goals were simpler and harder: do impactful work, be the best teammate, provide stability for his family. No work beneath him. People sought him out. He left room to leave with grace because his identity was never mortgaged to a selection message.

If your goal requires another human being to promote you, fall in love with you, or hire you, you have already outsourced your peace.

Martinez’s counseling questions are worth stealing wholesale:

  • Why did you join or re-enlist?
  • Did you accomplish the reason you started or stayed? If yes — leave with a happy heart.
  • If not, are you on the path, and is it still important?
  • What does a successful career look like — on your terms?

That is not careerism. That is stewardship of a life.

3. Urgent vs. important — choose before the inbox chooses for you

He leans on COL Mark Blum’s old test when roles collide:

  1. Is the event important to someone important to you?
  2. Would your physical presence make a difference?
  3. Will this opportunity come around again?

PowerPoint will always reappear. Your kid’s game might not. The Marine in crisis might not. Your own body will eventually stop accepting IOUs.

Leaders who never teach that distinction produce operators who can run a staff and still lose the war at home.

Hamilton, Washington, and the Birthday cake

Martinez closes with Hamilton — ambitious, useful, often blind to the legacy he was actually building — set against Washington choosing to leave with grace and gratitude rather than a scorched-earth speech. The musical line lands hard for a reason:

“And if we get this right, we’re gonna teach ’em how to say Goodbye.”

Then he brings it home to the Birthday Ball: oldest Marine passes cake to youngest. History transfers. Wisdom is supposed to transfer the same way.

That is the whole essay in one image. We are failing the pass if we only teach them how to fight and never how to finish.

Why this hits U.S.M.C. Ministries in the chest

I did not hang up the uniform because the fight was over. I hung it up because God had a different mission. “MOOP” — Marine On Own Program — started as a jab and became a declaration: I am on His program now. Not the institution’s. Not the culture’s. Not my ego’s.

But here is the honest confession Martinez forces: leaving well is not automatic just because you found a ministry lane. You can exit active duty and still carry bitterness like a seabag you refuse to drop. You can plant a nonprofit and still define success as the next platform, the next credential, the next room that finally “gets you.” Cheap continuity is not discipleship. Motion is not obedience.

Bonhoeffer would call some of our transition energy cheap grace — same intensity, thinner cross. Scripture is not unclear about the real countdown either:

“So teach us to number our days, that we may gain a heart of wisdom.” — Psalm 90:12 (NKJV)

“And whatever you do, do it heartily, as to the Lord and not to men, knowing that from the Lord you will receive the reward of the inheritance; for you serve the Lord Christ.” — Colossians 3:23–24 (NKJV)

You serve Christ in the billet and in the goodbye. Rank does not grade that paper. Faithfulness does.

What we are going to do with this

If you lead Marines — active, reserve, or veteran brothers still in the fight of second careers — do not file Martinez under “nice read.” Run it:

  1. Share the essay with one Marine who is inside five years of a decision.

https://www.mca-marines.org/wp-content/uploads/Martinez-0726-2nd-Place.pdf

  1. Ask the four questions above in your next real conversation — not as a checklist, as a mirror.
  2. Name your real countdown out loud to your spouse or a trusted brother. If you cannot say it, you are still lying to yourself about the end.
  3. Audit last week’s urgent/important scoreboard. Where did the inbox win and the covenant lose?

At U.S.M.C. Ministries our lane is Uniting, Serving, Mentoring, Counseling. Mentoring that never talks about goodbye is incomplete mentoring. Counseling that only patches the crash after retirement is late to the fight. We want men who finish the race without torching the people who ran it with them.

Old breed? New breed? Puller was right — not a damn bit of difference so long as it is the Marine breed. The difference that does matter is whether we teach them how to say goodbye.

Semper Fidelis. Peace.
