Rooted Where God Planted Me: Applying Christian Localism in Fredericksburg, VA

March 14, 2026 · General

By Adam "MOOP" Johns | March 2026

While many believers have been drawn to the conversation around Christian nationalism, debating what it means to influence culture at scale, there's a quieter, more grounded movement that I think matters more, and that's Christian localism: the idea that faithfulness starts where your feet are planted.

Jon Harris recently made a compelling case for it. His thesis is simple but devastating: without rootedness in a real place, conservatism is just ideology, and ideology doesn't survive. People will fight for their home, he says, and they won't fight for abstractions.

Jarrin Jackson takes it further in his Live Local Field Manual, a practical guide I've been working through. Jackson, an Oklahoma combat veteran who ran for state office, argues that real change happens at the county and city level and not in D.C., and he writes that Christians need to stop outsourcing their civic duty to politicians and start showing up at school boards, city councils, and planning commissions in their own backyards.

As I read both of these men, I kept thinking, this is what I'm attempting to do here, and Harris and Jackson are giving legs to the name: Christian Localism.

"The man who plants a garden is worth more than the man who debates national policy - because the garden actually feeds someone."

- Wendell Berry

Why Fredericksburg

I didn't grow up in Fredericksburg, Virginia; I grew up outside Houston, Texas. But after 20+ years in the Marine Corps, after deployments, duty stations, and enough moves to lose count, I chose this place. I planted my family here and bought property here, and I'm raising three kids on these streets while I run with three organizations, serve a church here and mentor men here.

Harris and Jackson would ascribe the term localism to these endeavors, but to me, I believe it's simple obedience, maybe even a variation of Eugene Peterson's "A Long Obedience in the Same Direction."

God placed Adam in a specific garden, a garden and not the whole earth, and He gave specific land to specific tribes. Paul taught that God "determined their appointed times and the boundaries of their habitation" (Acts 17:26). There's a theology of place built into Scripture that we've almost completely forgotten.

The average American moves twelve times in a lifetime. That is rootlessness and not freedom, and it produces the same spiritual desolation we see all around us: men with no identity, no belonging, and no direction.

"God determined their appointed times and the boundaries of their habitation"

- Acts 17:26

The Five-Front War Is Local

Everything I do through USMC Ministries is built on what I call the Five-Front War, the five arenas every man is fighting in at the same time: Heart (your marriage and family culture), Spirit (your discipleship and ministry), Mind (your strategy and growth), Body (your physical discipline and health), and Provision (your business and stewardship). What I've realized is that every one of those fronts is local. You can't lead your family from the cloud, you can't disciple men through abstractions, and you can't steward a community you've never walked through.

My marriage plays out on Caroline Street, and my boys are growing up in this community. My Airbnb business (Bow & Arrow Studios) depends on the character of downtown, my training at fit20 Cosner's Corner serves local clients face to face, and my stewardship work through C5iSR is about managing resources for this family, in this city. None of this works in the abstract, because all of it requires place.

Against the Digital Gnostics

Harris's piece connects to something else I've been chewing on, which is C.R. Wiley's argument that AI represents a competing eschatology. Transhumanism promises transcendence through technology: upload your consciousness, live forever in the machine, escape the limitations of the body. That's digital Gnosticism, and the Bible says no.

God made us embodied. He placed us in gardens and told us to cultivate specific land, and the Incarnation itself is the ultimate refutation of disembodied existence, because God didn't email us salvation. He put on flesh and walked among us, in a specific town, under a specific Roman governor, at a specific time in history (Luke 2:1-2). If God Himself chose localism, what makes us think we can transcend it?

What This Looks Like in Practice

I'm writing this from a 200-year-old building in downtown Fredericksburg where I run short-term rentals, and not from a seminary tower, so let me tell you what Christian localism looks like when it hits the pavement.

The first thing is to invest in local property. Own something and steward it, and commit to a city instead of just consuming it. When you own property, you care about zoning meetings and tax policy and whether the sidewalks are safe for your kids, and that's political engagement rooted in something real.

The second is to build local institutions. U.S.M.C. Ministries is men gathering in the middle of the week, in person and virtually, confessing their failures and holding each other accountable. It has nothing to do with a podcast brand trying to go viral, and you can only fake it for so long before someone confronts you and convicts you to your core.

The third is to serve local churches. Don't church-hop and don't stream church; show up, serve, and submit to local elders. Spotswood Baptist Church isn't the biggest or flashiest congregation in town, but it's mine, and I'm committed to it.

The fourth is to mentor local men. The brotherhood I'm building is made of neighbors and not followers: men I eat with, men whose wives know my wife, men whose kids play with my kids. That's how virtue gets passed on, through proximity and not through content.

The fifth is to engage local politics. My work restoring historic Fredericksburg is just me showing up at city council, caring about demographics, and engaging churches about the future of our city, and there's nothing national about it. Edmund Burke was right: "man acts from adequate motives relative to his interest." My interest is right here.

The Marine Connection

Harris quotes J.D. Vance: "People will not fight for abstractions, but they will fight for their home." Every Marine knows this instinctively. We didn't fight for geopolitical theory; we fought for the man next to us, for our families back home, and for a flag that represented a place, which is more than an idea: a land with soil and rivers and street addresses where our people live.

The same principle applies to spiritual warfare. You don't fight the principalities and powers by tweeting about culture war. You fight them by leading your family in prayer on Caroline Street, by teaching your son to respect women at the dinner table, and by opening your home to broken men who need a meal and a conversation and a Bible.

That's localism, and that's ministry, and that's the Kingdom of God advancing one household, one block, one city at a time.

"What I stand for is what I stand on."

- Wendell Berry
