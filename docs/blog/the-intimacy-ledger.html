The Intimacy Ledger: Frequency and Freedom in Marriage

By U.S.M.C. Ministries · drafted with AI assistance, reviewed by Adam Johns
Listen to this post · 6 min · the words light up as they are read

So I had a little bit of an unlock this past Saturday morning, and I sent it to the brothers in my men’s group before my coffee was gone. It’s about sex in marriage, and how often, which is a subject most of us husbands think about plenty and talk about almost never. I recorded a voice memo to explain it and sent it to our chaplain for a pastoral read, and his notes were good enough that I want every married man I know to have them, so his one-page brief is at the bottom of this post.

Two expectations

I’ve come to believe that most husbands carry an expectation to: a number in our heads, some count of times a week that we think a normal, healthy marriage ought to hit. Most wives carry an expectation not to, meaning she expects not to be pressured past a certain point. Those two expectations run straight into each other, and the work of a marriage is easing that tension, not winning the count.

The number is a trap

Couples try to settle it by agreeing on a target, and I’ve watched the target trap both of them. Say the number is three, it’s Saturday, and you’re sitting at two. Now you feel like you have to make it happen, and she can feel you counting from across the house. Turn it around: if three is her ceiling and you already hit it by Thursday, Friday’s date night stops feeling like a date and starts feeling like a fourth demand. Either way the number manufactures pressure, and pressure kills desire. One of our brothers learned that in a big fight on what was supposed to be a relaxing vacation, when pushing for intimacy got him the opposite of what he wanted. So skip the internet charts that tell you what’s average for your age, too, because you didn’t marry an average.

Our chaplain named the enemy

When our chaplain read my memo, he put a name on the thing I’d been circling, and he called it the ledger. Once intimacy becomes a count it becomes a debt, with one column for what’s owed and one for what’s been paid, and to a wife a debt feels like pressure. Paul doesn’t let either spouse off the hook, but he doesn’t hand either one a scorecard, either:

“Let the husband render to his wife the affection due her, and likewise also the wife to her husband. The wife does not have authority over her own body, but the husband does. And likewise the husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife does. Do not deprive one another except with consent for a time, that you may give yourselves to fasting and prayer; and come together again so that Satan does not tempt you because of your lack of self-control.” (1 Corinthians 7:3–5, NKJV)

The obligation in that passage runs both ways, it’s paid in affection, and the only pause Paul allows is one the two of them agree on together, for prayer, before they come back together. If I quote verse 5 at my wife to win an argument, I’ve turned God’s Word into the ledger and treated our covenant like a contract.

An invitation has to survive a no

Our chaplain’s second point hit me hardest. The goal is frequency and freedom: I’m free to initiate any day of the week, and she’s free to decline. That freedom only means something if her no costs her nothing, though, with no sulking, no cold shoulder the next morning, and no mental note filed away for later. How I take a no proves whether I was inviting or demanding. Paul says love “does not seek its own, is not provoked, does not take into account a wrong suffered” (1 Corinthians 13:5, NASB), and a love that takes no account doesn’t keep a ledger at all.

Ask her, and then listen

When I ask my wife how I can serve her better, she almost never names something for me to start. She names something to stop: put the phone down, clear the piles of paperwork off the table. We men tend to answer that question with a new project, and our wives tend to answer it by asking for some relief. That’s why our chaplain put understanding before frequency, straight from Peter:

“Husbands, likewise, dwell with them with understanding, giving honor to the wife, as to the weaker vessel, and as being heirs together of the grace of life, that your prayers may not be hindered.” (1 Peter 3:7, NKJV)

Dwelling with her according to knowledge means learning your wife, not the average woman. The Re:Engage homework we were given wasn’t “have more sex.” It was to talk about our expectations, out loud, at a calm time, and outside the bedroom. That conversation isn’t a negotiation for your number, so ask her what makes her feel pressured, and then really listen to the answer.

Make deposits, and go first

None of this takes the load off the husband. As the family captain I still carry the load, and the load is deposits: guarding her time and her margin, carrying the things that keep the water warm between us, and doing it with no invoice attached for later that night, because a deposit with a hidden invoice is just a loan. Christ is the pattern: “Husbands, love your wives, just as Christ also loved the church and gave Himself for her” (Ephesians 5:25, NKJV). He went first, at the cost of His own sacrifice, and He didn’t keep a tally of what His bride owed Him. Our chaplain put it plainly in his notes: stop counting and keep giving, and you’ll usually find your wife moving towards you.

Burn the ledger

So, brothers, burn the ledger. Initiate freely, take a no graciously, lead with deposits, and have the expectations talk with your wife this week, at a calm time and outside the bedroom. Do you carry a number in your head, and does she feel it? Would she say it’s safe to tell you no? Those two questions are on our chaplain’s brief along with two more, and I hope you’ll work through them with your wife or with the men in your group!
