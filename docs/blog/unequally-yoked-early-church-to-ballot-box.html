Unequally Yoked: 2 Corinthians 6 from the Early Church to the Ballot Box

By U.S.M.C. Ministries · drafted with AI assistance, reviewed by Adam Johns

Paul’s command to the Corinthians is short and it leaves no exits: “Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers” (2 Corinthians 6:14, KJV). We’ll say where we land up front so that no one has to dig for it. A Christian may work beside anyone to stop an injustice, and he may never put Christ’s name, his own name or his obedience under a common yoke with those who deny Christ’s gospel. That rule binds the stages we like as much as the stages we don’t, and this year the charge of an “unequal yoke” was thrown hard at one conference in Texas, so we went looking for it on three other platforms where it fits as well or better.

The argument among Christian men right now has two sides. One says a Christian may work with anyone on a just cause so long as the alliance never asks him to sin, since Abram went to war alongside Amorites and Daniel served Nebuchadnezzar. The other side answers that Paul didn’t say “don’t sin alongside unbelievers,” he said don’t be yoked with them. Both are quoting true things, and the text tells us how they fit together. The word-by-word treatment is in our deep study of 2 Corinthians 6:14.

What Paul wrote, and to whom

“Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers: for what fellowship hath righteousness with unrighteousness? and what communion hath light with darkness?” (2 Corinthians 6:14, KJV)

The four English words “unequally yoked together with” are one verb in Greek, heterozygeo, built from heteros (different) and zygos (a yoke), and Paul borrowed the picture from the law of Moses: “Thou shalt not plow with an ox and an ass together” (Deuteronomy 22:10, KJV). A yoke joins two animals to one load, points them in one direction, and puts them under one driver, which is why neither animal has to do anything wrong for the team to be a bad one.

The people who first heard this lived in a city full of temples, and the temples had dining rooms where guilds and families feasted on meat that had been offered to the god. Paul had already told them, “Ye cannot drink the cup of the Lord, and the cup of devils” (1 Corinthians 10:21, KJV). Yet in the same letter he said he didn’t mean for them to avoid the immoral people of this world, “for then must ye needs go out of the world” (1 Corinthians 5:10, KJV), and he told them to eat dinner at an unbeliever’s house if they were invited (1 Corinthians 10:27). So Paul himself marked two edges, the table of devils forbidden and the neighbor’s table permitted, and the yoke lies between them. His reason was that “ye are the temple of the living God” (2 Corinthians 6:16, KJV), and a temple isn’t shared.

How the church has read it

The earliest writers we can check, Tertullian and Cyprian, went straight to marriage, because a Christian marries “only in the Lord” (1 Corinthians 7:39, KJV). John Calvin widened the frame back to Corinth, since the fault Paul was correcting was Christians sitting down at idol banquets, and he was careful to say what the verse doesn’t forbid:

“For one sun shines upon us, we eat of the same bread, we breathe the same air, and we cannot altogether refrain from intercourse with them; but Paul speaks of the yoke of impiety, that is, of participation in works, in which Christians cannot lawfully have fellowship.” (John Calvin, Commentary on 2 Corinthians, on 6:14)

Calvin added that even those who don’t approve of superstition in their hearts are polluted by pretending if they don’t openly stand apart from it, so the inward reservation of the Corinthian who reclined in the temple didn’t cancel what his presence said to everyone watching. Matthew Henry applied the verse to close friendships and most of all to religious communion, and he saw harm in the pull itself before any crime: the two “will draw different ways, and that will be galling and grievous.”

The Westminster Confession of 1646 said that those who profess the true reformed religion shouldn’t marry infidels, papists, or other idolaters, and our own confession, the Second London Baptist Confession of 1689, kept the paragraph almost word for word:

“It is lawful for all sorts of people to marry who are able with judgment to give their consent; yet it is the duty of Christians to marry in the Lord. Therefore those who profess the true religion should not marry with unbelievers or idolaters; neither should the godly be unequally yoked by marrying those who are wicked in their lives, or who maintain damnable heresy.” (1689 LBCF, chapter 25)

The 1689 also cites our verse to guard the Lord’s table (chapter 30), speaks as sharply about the papacy in chapter 26 as any document of its century, and says in chapter 24 that it’s lawful for Christians to hold civil office. In the modern fights that drove Spurgeon out of the Baptist Union and Machen out of his denomination, the ground was pulpits and the Lord’s table. So the church’s long use of this verse tells us where it bites hardest: the closer a bond comes to worship and to the name of the gospel, the more surely Paul’s command governs it.

Francis Schaeffer gave us the tool for the civil question in The Church at the End of the Twentieth Century (1970). As he used the words, a co-belligerent stands on the same side as you in one fight for justice, for reasons of his own, while an ally is joined to you at the foundation. A Christian can be the first with anyone, and the second only with those who share the faith.

What makes a yoke

Scripture gives co-belligerence solid ground. When Abram went after the kings who had taken Lot, three Amorite brothers went with him, and Genesis says they “were confederate with Abram” (Genesis 14:13, KJV). If the verse forbade every joint effort with an unbeliever, no Christian could serve on a jury, in a legislature, or in a rifle platoon.

The same chapter gives us the line Abram wouldn’t cross. When the king of Sodom offered him the spoils, he refused to take “from a thread even to a shoelatchet,” and he said why: “lest thou shouldest say, I have made Abram rich” (Genesis 14:23, KJV). No one had asked him to sin, yet he wouldn’t let Sodom put its name on what God had given him. Jehoshaphat is the warning from the other direction, a godly king who “joined affinity with Ahab” (2 Chronicles 18:1, KJV) and later went into a shipping venture with Ahab’s son; the ships were wrecked, and the prophet’s whole explanation was “Because thou hast joined thyself with Ahaziah” (2 Chronicles 20:37, KJV). So a bond can be wrong before it has asked for one wrong act, because a public partnership says something whether or not you mean it to.

If the picture is a yoke, then the picture gives us four questions to ask of any bond, whether it’s a marriage, a ministry partnership, a business, or a political coalition:

  1. Direction. Does this commit me to go where the other party goes, past the one task we agreed on?
  2. Name. Do we stand under a common name, so that what my partner says is heard as said by me?
  3. Command. Can someone who doesn’t share my Lord bind what I do, or spend my name where I wouldn’t?
  4. Banner. Does the bond itself claim to speak for Christ or for the Christian faith?

Buying milk answers no to all four, and so does a vote, and so does a coalition formed to pass one bill, since each party gives his own reasons and no one speaks for another’s faith. Marriage answers yes to all four for life, which is why the church started there. Joint worship, a shared pulpit and a common statement of the gospel answer yes to the fourth by their nature, which is why Zerubbabel turned down the neighbors who said, “Let us build with you: for we seek your God, as ye do” (Ezra 4:2, KJV).

Four platforms, one test

A test is only worth having if we’ll use it on our own side, so here are four public platforms, each described from its own page or a named report.

A rabbi’s prayer at CPAC

On Friday, March 27, 2026, the second day of CPAC in the Dallas area opened with a prayer from the main stage, and CPAC’s own agenda lists who gave it: “Rabbi Daniel Lapin — Prayer.” The rabbi’s organization, the American Alliance of Jews and Christians, wrote afterward that he “concluded with a heartfelt prayer that America would use this milestone to reaffirm its Judeo-Christian heritage.” We have no quarrel with Rabbi Lapin as a neighbor or a co-belligerent, but prayer is worship, and rabbinic Judaism doesn’t confess that Jesus is the Christ. The apostle John leaves no room on that: “Whosoever denieth the Son, the same hath not the Father” (1 John 2:23, KJV). Of the four cases this one stands closest to the dining rooms of Corinth, since a hall with many professing Christians in it was led in prayer by a teacher of a religion that doesn’t come to the Father through the Son. We looked for the Christian voices who called it an unequal yoke and couldn’t find them.

The Make Heaven Crowded Tour

TPUSA Faith’s Make Heaven Crowded Tour has crossed the country all year, and its own site describes the tour as “a gospel centered gathering calling people to repentance, faith, and bold obedience to Jesus.” That’s a banner by the tour’s own words. On November 13, 2026, the tour stopped at Charis Bible College in Woodland Park, Colorado, and the tour’s page for that night lists Andrew Wommack among the speakers. Charis is the school of Wommack’s ministry, and the Colorado Springs Gazette reports that his teaching centers on healing and prosperity and that the Christian Research Institute calls it a “doctrinally aberrant” form of Word of Faith theology. A night that carries the gospel’s name and puts a Word of Faith teacher on its list of preachers meets the fourth mark more plainly than any political conference could, because the thing being shared is the preaching itself.

“Christ Is King” in Dallas

“Christ Is King: America After Trump” met November 12–14, 2026, at the Irving Convention Center near Dallas, hosted by Pastor Joel Webbon’s New Christian Right. This is the stage that drew the yoke charge. When critics objected that non-Protestants were on the program, the organizers answered in writing that the event “is explicitly a political conference,” that the critics were “shouting ‘ecumenism,’” and that “grown men have to be expected to understand the difference between a platform and a pulpit.” They cited Schaeffer’s co-belligerence by name, and on the principle they’re right.

The trouble is the name on the banner. A conference called “America After Trump” could seat anyone, but a conference called “Christ Is King” has claimed to speak for Christ before the first speaker walks out, and that is mark four. The closing keynote on Saturday night went to Nick Fuentes, whom Religion News Service describes as a Catholic, and RNS reports that when he said, “God has a name,” and, “His name is Jesus Christ,” the hall rose and chanted “Christ is King.” We say amen to the sentence. But the church of Rome has bound itself to this, and has never taken it back:

“If any one saith, that by faith alone the impious is justified; in such wise as to mean, that nothing else is required to co-operate in order to the obtaining the grace of Justification, and that it is not in any way necessary, that he be prepared and disposed by the movement of his own will; let him be anathema.” (Council of Trent, Session 6, Canon 9, Waterworth translation)

Rome’s present catechism still teaches, under the heading “Outside the Church there is no salvation,” that “they could not be saved who, knowing that the Catholic Church was founded as necessary by God through Christ, would refuse either to enter it or to remain in it” (Catechism of the Catholic Church, 846). Paul’s word for a gospel that adds to faith is just as plain: “But though we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel unto you than that which we have preached unto you, let him be accursed” (Galatians 1:8, KJV). We aren’t charging Mr. Fuentes with preaching Trent from that stage, and we’ll gladly stand beside a Roman Catholic neighbor at the statehouse for the life of an unborn child. But a Reformed pastor who believes Galatians 1:8 can’t hang the name of Christ over a stage, hand the last word to a son of the church that anathematizes his gospel, let the hall confess Christ’s kingship behind him, and then tell the critics it was only politics. Those who called that a yoke were right.

Most of the press coverage was about something else, the things said from that stage about race, which ChurchLeaders and RNS quote at length. That deserves its own post, and Scripture is clear there too, since God “hath made of one blood all nations of men” (Acts 17:26, KJV).

A party platform

The fourth platform is a literal one. The Democratic Party’s national platform, adopted at its convention in August 2024 and still the party’s current one, says, “With a Democratic Congress, we will pass national legislation to make Roe the law of the land again,” and, “We will repeal the Hyde Amendment,” which is the rule that keeps federal money from paying for most abortions. On marriage it says, “Democrats passed and President Biden signed the historic Respect for Marriage Act, enshrining marriage equality in federal law.” We call abortion child sacrifice because the Bible has already described a people who killed their children for the sake of what they wanted, and it said they “shed innocent blood, even the blood of their sons and of their daughters, whom they sacrificed unto the idols of Canaan: and the land was polluted with blood” (Psalm 106:38, KJV). God defined marriage in Genesis 2:24, Christ confirmed it in Matthew 19:4–6, and no convention can amend it.

A voter doesn’t wear this yoke, because a ballot is one choice among the names in front of him. Someone who runs under the party’s name, raises money on it and votes with its caucus has answered yes to direction, name and command, and a Christian profession doesn’t change what the platform says. President Biden told donors on June 27, 2023, “I happen to be a practicing Catholic. I’m not big on abortion. But guess what? Roe v. Wade got it right,” and on December 13, 2022, he said, “Today I sign the Respect for Marriage Act into the law.” Senator Raphael Warnock, a Baptist pastor, described himself during his first campaign as a “pro-choice pastor.” Those are their own words and acts.

The Republican platform earns no pass from us. Its 2024 text hands abortion “to the States and to a vote of the People” and pledges to support “access to Birth Control, and IVF (fertility treatments),” a practice that routinely freezes or throws away human embryos. The test doesn’t care which letter follows a candidate’s name.

Why the silence matters

No one needs to be quieter about Dallas, and what we’re asking for is the same voice in March that we heard in November, because a standard that only comes out against the people we already dislike has become a weapon, and God has told us what He thinks of that: “Divers weights, and divers measures, both of them are alike abomination to the LORD” (Proverbs 20:10, KJV). When the world watches Christians call a yoke unequal only when the other tribe is wearing it, the world concludes that the verse is a club, and it stops listening when we say that Christ alone saves.

So we’ll say it the same way in every direction. Work beside the rabbi, the Roman Catholic and the unbeliever on any just cause, and tell each of them plainly, and in love, that there is salvation in Christ alone through faith alone. Don’t pray under their lead, don’t lend the gospel’s name to a pulpit that preaches another gospel, don’t put “Christ Is King” over a stage and share it with Rome, and don’t take a party’s name when its platform promises what God calls bloodshed.

One yoke

The reason a Christian refuses a second yoke is that he already wears one. “Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls” (Matthew 11:29, KJV). No party, movement or coalition has ever said that to anyone and kept the promise, which is why the psalm tells us, “Put not your trust in princes” (Psalm 146:3, KJV). So work for justice with anyone who’ll work for it, and keep your name and your gospel clear while you do. Whose yoke would people say you’re wearing, if all they had to go on was who you stand with?
