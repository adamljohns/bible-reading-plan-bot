Unequally Yoked: 2 Corinthians 6 from the Early Church to the Ballot Box

By U.S.M.C. Ministries · drafted with AI assistance, reviewed by Adam Johns

There’s an argument going around among Christian men right now about who we may stand beside in public life, and it keeps landing on one verse. One side says a Christian may work with unbelievers, or with people from communions we have deep differences with, on any just cause, so long as the alliance never asks him to sin. Abram went to war alongside Amorites to rescue Lot, Daniel served Nebuchadnezzar, and all of us buy groceries and cheer for ball teams next to people who don’t know Christ. The other side answers that buying milk isn’t a coalition and a ball team doesn’t speak for your faith, and that Paul didn’t say “don’t sin alongside unbelievers,” he said don’t be yoked with them.

We think each side is holding part of the truth, and the church has been over this ground before. So before saying anything about a ballot box, we wanted to walk through what Paul wrote and how Christians have read it for eighteen hundred years. The word-by-word treatment is in our deep study of 2 Corinthians 6:14.

What Paul wrote, and to whom

“Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers: for what fellowship hath righteousness with unrighteousness? and what communion hath light with darkness?” (2 Corinthians 6:14, KJV)

The four English words “unequally yoked together with” are one verb in Greek, heterozygeo, built from heteros (different) and zygos (a yoke), and Paul borrowed the picture from the law of Moses: “Thou shalt not plow with an ox and an ass together” (Deuteronomy 22:10, KJV). A yoke joins two animals to one load, points them in one direction, and puts them under one driver, which is why neither animal has to do anything wrong for the team to be a bad one.

The people who first heard this lived in a city full of temples, and the temples had dining rooms where guilds and families feasted on meat that had been offered to the god. Paul had already written to them about it in 1 Corinthians 8 through 10, and he didn’t soften it: “Ye cannot drink the cup of the Lord, and the cup of devils” (1 Corinthians 10:21, KJV). Yet in the same letter he told them that he didn’t mean for them to avoid the immoral people of this world, “for then must ye needs go out of the world” (1 Corinthians 5:10, KJV), and he told them to go ahead and eat dinner at an unbeliever’s house if they were invited (1 Corinthians 10:27). So Paul himself marked two edges, with the table of devils forbidden on one side and the neighbor’s table permitted on the other, and the yoke lies somewhere between them. His reason wasn’t disgust at the neighbors; it was that “ye are the temple of the living God” (2 Corinthians 6:16, KJV), and a temple isn’t shared.

How the church has read it

The earliest writers we can check went straight to marriage. Tertullian, writing to his own wife around the year 200, argued that a Christian widow who marries again must marry a believer, because Paul said “only in the Lord” (1 Corinthians 7:39, KJV). He noticed that when Paul tells a believer to stay with an unbelieving spouse he speaks of the one who already has such a wife and not of one who takes her. Cyprian of Carthage, about fifty years later, gathered proof-texts under plain headings, and under the heading that marriage isn’t to be contracted with Gentiles he listed our verse beside the “only in the Lord” text and the history of Solomon’s wives.

The Reformers kept the marriage application and widened the frame back to what Paul was dealing with in Corinth. John Calvin said in his commentary that those who limit the verse to marriage have missed the context, since the fault Paul was correcting was Christians sitting down at idol banquets, and he was careful to say what the verse doesn’t forbid:

“For one sun shines upon us, we eat of the same bread, we breathe the same air, and we cannot altogether refrain from intercourse with them; but Paul speaks of the yoke of impiety, that is, of participation in works, in which Christians cannot lawfully have fellowship.” (John Calvin, Commentary on 2 Corinthians, on 6:14)

Calvin then added that even those who don’t approve of superstition in their hearts are polluted by pretending if they don’t openly stand apart from it, so the inward reservation of the Corinthian who reclined in the temple didn’t cancel what his presence said to everyone watching. Matthew Henry, a century and a half later, applied the verse to the settled relations we choose, to close friendships, and most of all to religious communion, and he saw harm in the pull itself before any crime: the two “will draw different ways, and that will be galling and grievous.”

The confessions wrote the marriage rule down. The Westminster Confession of 1646 said that those who profess the true reformed religion shouldn’t marry infidels, papists, or other idolaters. Our own confession, the Second London Baptist Confession of 1689, kept the paragraph almost word for word, and here it is as we host it:

“It is lawful for all sorts of people to marry who are able with judgment to give their consent; yet it is the duty of Christians to marry in the Lord. Therefore those who profess the true religion should not marry with unbelievers or idolaters; neither should the godly be unequally yoked by marrying those who are wicked in their lives, or who maintain damnable heresy.” (1689 LBCF, chapter 25)

The Baptists left out the word that named Roman Catholics, though we wouldn’t build much on that, since chapter 26 of the same confession speaks as sharply about the papacy as any document of its century. Both confessions agree that for the closest bond a Christian name on the other party doesn’t settle it, and what a person believes and how he lives both have to be weighed. The 1689 also cites our verse to guard the Lord’s table (chapter 30), and in chapter 24 it says plainly that it’s lawful for Christians to hold civil office.

In the last hundred and fifty years the verse has been preached most often in fights over church fellowship. Charles Spurgeon left the Baptist Union in 1887 over its tolerance of teachers who had given up the gospel, J. Gresham Machen and others were forced out of a Presbyterian denomination in the 1930s over the same disease, and American evangelicals divided in the 1950s over running evangelistic campaigns in partnership with ministers who denied the faith. Every one of those was about pulpits, mission and the Lord’s table. The church’s long use of this verse has been about marriage, worship and church fellowship, and its use on civil coalitions is recent and thin, which ought to make all of us a little slower to swing it.

The one modern writer who gave us a tool for the civil question was Francis Schaeffer. In The Church at the End of the Twentieth Century (1970) he distinguished a co-belligerent from an ally. As he used the words, a co-belligerent stands on the same side as you in one fight for justice, for reasons of his own, while an ally is joined to you at the foundation. Schaeffer thought Christians could be the first with anyone on a just cause, and could be the second only with those who share the faith.

The best case for standing together

The case for co-belligerence deserves to be stated at full strength, because Scripture gives it real support. When Abram went after the kings who had taken Lot, three Amorite brothers went with him, and Genesis says they “were confederate with Abram” (Genesis 14:13, KJV). Joseph ran Egypt for Pharaoh and Daniel ran a province for Nebuchadnezzar. The exiles were told to “seek the peace of the city” where God had sent them (Jeremiah 29:7, KJV). Paul stood up in a divided council and took the Pharisees’ side against the Sadducees on the resurrection (Acts 23:6). If the verse forbade every joint effort with an unbeliever, no Christian could serve on a jury, in a legislature, or in a rifle platoon.

So the brother who says he may work beside an unbeliever to stop an injustice is saying something true, and those of us who feel the danger of these alliances should say so before we say anything else.

Where the text draws the line

We don’t think that rule goes far enough, though, and the reasons come from the text and not from our nerves. Paul’s command is “be ye not,” which forbids getting into the yoke at all and doesn’t wait for the first wicked order to be given. He then asks five questions using five different words for partnership (fellowship, communion, concord, part, agreement), and none of them is a word for standing near someone.

The same chapter that gives us Abram’s Amorite confederates gives us the line he wouldn’t cross. When the king of Sodom offered him the spoils, Abram refused to take “from a thread even to a shoelatchet,” and he said why: “lest thou shouldest say, I have made Abram rich” (Genesis 14:23, KJV). No one had asked him to sin, yet he wouldn’t let Sodom put its name on what God had given him. Jehoshaphat is the warning from the other direction. He was a godly king who “joined affinity with Ahab” (2 Chronicles 18:1, KJV), and later went into a shipping venture with Ahab’s son. Scripture doesn’t record that the venture required any idolatry of him. It records that the ships were wrecked, and the prophet’s whole explanation was “Because thou hast joined thyself with Ahaziah” (2 Chronicles 20:37, KJV).

So a bond can be wrong before it has asked for a wrong act, because a public partnership says something whether or not you mean it to.

A test you can use

If the picture is a yoke, then the picture gives us the questions to ask of any bond, whether it’s a marriage, a ministry partnership, a business, or a political coalition:

  1. Direction. Does this commit me to go where the other party goes, past the one task we agreed on?
  2. Name. Do we stand under a common name, so that what my partner says is heard as said by me?
  3. Command. Can someone who doesn’t share my Lord bind what I do, or spend my name where I wouldn’t?
  4. Banner. Does the bond itself claim to speak for Christ or for the Christian faith?

Buying milk answers no to all four, and so does your job in most cases, and so does a vote. A petition or a coalition formed to pass one bill usually answers no as well, since each party gives his own reasons and no one speaks for another’s faith. Marriage answers yes to all four for life, which is why the church started there. Joint worship, a shared pulpit and a common statement of the gospel answer yes to the fourth by their nature, which is why Zerubbabel turned down the neighbors who said, “Let us build with you: for we seek your God, as ye do” (Ezra 4:2, KJV).

Political alliances move along that scale. The single cause grows into a standing movement with a religious name, the leaders begin to speak for Christ on your behalf, and staying in good standing starts to require that you keep quiet about the gospel or about a partner’s error. At some point on that road common cause has become a yoke.

A word on Roman Catholic neighbors, since much of the heat is there. The verse says “unbelievers,” and whether someone who professes Christ in another communion is one isn’t decided by this verse. That has to be decided by the gospel he confesses, argued from Scripture on its own ground. What the verse does decide is that you can’t sign a common confession with someone while believing his gospel is a different one. You can stand next to him at the statehouse for the life of an unborn child, and tell him plainly where you differ, without ever saying the two of you are one in Christ if you don’t believe it.

We hold this test with an open hand, because godly men will place particular coalitions at different points on that road. Calvin warned in the Institutes that the appetite for separation comes more often from pride than from holiness. Let’s be slow to call a brother compromised for standing where we wouldn’t, and just as slow to call a brother a schismatic for declining a platform we’d take.

One yoke

The reason a Christian refuses a second yoke has nothing to do with being too clean for his neighbors; it’s that he already wears one. “Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls” (Matthew 11:29, KJV). No party, movement or coalition has ever said that to anyone and kept the promise, which is why the psalm tells us, “Put not your trust in princes” (Psalm 146:3, KJV). The promises Paul quoted to the Corinthians hold whether our side wins the next election or loses the next ten. So work for justice with anyone who’ll work for it, and keep your name and your gospel clear while you do. Whose yoke would people say you’re wearing, if all they had to go on was who you stand with?
