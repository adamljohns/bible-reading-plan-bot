Unequally Yoked: 2 Corinthians 6 from the Early Church to the Ballot Box

By U.S.M.C. Ministries · drafted with AI assistance, reviewed by Adam Johns
Listen to this post · 16 min · the words light up as they are read

Paul’s command is short, and it leaves no exits: “Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers” (2 Corinthians 6:14, KJV). We’ll give the verdict first. A Christian may work beside anyone to stop an injustice, and he may never put Christ’s name, his own name or his obedience under a common yoke with those who deny Christ’s gospel. That rule binds the stages we like as hard as the stages we don’t. This year the charge of an “unequal yoke” was thrown at one conference in Texas, so we laid the same test on that stage and on three others. It fits the three that drew no charge, and it doesn’t fit the one that did.

What Paul wrote, and to whom

“Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers: for what fellowship hath righteousness with unrighteousness? and what communion hath light with darkness?” (2 Corinthians 6:14, KJV)

“Unequally yoked together with” is one verb in Greek, heterozygeo, and Paul took the picture from the law of Moses: “Thou shalt not plow with an ox and an ass together” (Deuteronomy 22:10). A yoke puts two animals under one load, one direction and one driver, so neither animal has to do anything wrong for the team to be a bad one.

Corinth’s temples had dining rooms where guilds and families feasted on meat offered to the god, and Paul had told this church, “Ye cannot drink the cup of the Lord, and the cup of devils” (1 Corinthians 10:21). In the same letter he refused to pull them out of ordinary life, “for then must ye needs go out of the world” (1 Corinthians 5:10), and he told them to eat at an unbeliever’s house if invited (1 Corinthians 10:27). So the table of devils is forbidden, the neighbor’s table is open, and the yoke lies between them. His reason was that “ye are the temple of the living God” (2 Corinthians 6:16), and a temple isn’t shared.

How the church has read it

Tertullian and Cyprian went straight to marriage, because a Christian marries “only in the Lord” (1 Corinthians 7:39). John Calvin took the verse back to the idol banquets of Corinth, and he was careful about what it doesn’t forbid:

“For one sun shines upon us, we eat of the same bread, we breathe the same air, and we cannot altogether refrain from intercourse with them; but Paul speaks of the yoke of impiety, that is, of participation in works, in which Christians cannot lawfully have fellowship.” (John Calvin, Commentary on 2 Corinthians, on 6:14)

Our own confession, the Second London Baptist Confession of 1689, took its paragraph on marriage almost word for word from the Westminster Confession of 1646:

“It is lawful for all sorts of people to marry who are able with judgment to give their consent; yet it is the duty of Christians to marry in the Lord. Therefore those who profess the true religion should not marry with unbelievers or idolaters; neither should the godly be unequally yoked by marrying those who are wicked in their lives, or who maintain damnable heresy.” (1689 LBCF, chapter 25)

The closer a bond comes to worship and to the name of the gospel, the harder Paul’s command bites.

Francis Schaeffer gave us the words for the civil question in The Church at the End of the Twentieth Century (1970). A co-belligerent stands beside you in one fight for justice, and an ally is joined to you at the foundation. A Christian can be the first with anyone, and the second only with those who share the faith.

What makes a yoke

When Abram went after the kings who had taken Lot, three Amorite brothers went with him, and Genesis says they “were confederate with Abram” (Genesis 14:13). The same chapter shows the line he wouldn’t cross. The king of Sodom offered him the spoils, and Abram refused to take “from a thread even to a shoelatchet,” “lest thou shouldest say, I have made Abram rich” (Genesis 14:23). Nobody had asked him to sin, and he still wouldn’t let Sodom put its name on what God had given him. A public partnership says something whether you mean it to or not.

The picture gives us four questions for any bond:

  1. Direction. Am I bound to go where my partner goes, past the one task?
  2. Name. Do we stand under one name, so his words are heard as mine?
  3. Command. Can someone who doesn’t own my Lord bind what I do?
  4. Banner. Does the bond claim to speak for Christ or His gospel?

Buying milk answers no to all four, and so does a vote, and so does a coalition formed to pass one bill. Marriage answers yes to all four for life, which is why the church started there. Joint worship and a shared pulpit answer yes to the fourth by their nature.

Four platforms, one test

A test is only worth having if we’ll use it on our own side. Here are four platforms, each taken from its own page or a named report.

A rabbi’s prayer at CPAC

On Friday, March 27, 2026, the second day of CPAC in the Dallas area opened with a prayer from the main stage, and CPAC’s own agenda lists who gave it: “Rabbi Daniel Lapin — Prayer.” His organization, the American Alliance of Jews and Christians, wrote it up afterward. We have no quarrel with Rabbi Lapin as a neighbor, but prayer is worship, and rabbinic Judaism doesn’t confess that Jesus is the Christ: “Whosoever denieth the Son, the same hath not the Father” (1 John 2:23). This case stands closest to the dining rooms of Corinth. We looked for the Christian voices who called it an unequal yoke, and we couldn’t find them.

The Make Heaven Crowded Tour

TPUSA Faith’s Make Heaven Crowded Tour calls itself, on its own site, “a gospel centered gathering calling people to repentance, faith, and bold obedience to Jesus.” On November 13, 2026, it stopped at Charis Bible College in Woodland Park, Colorado, the school of Andrew Wommack’s ministry, and the tour’s page for that night lists Wommack among the speakers. The Colorado Springs Gazette reports that his teaching centers on healing and prosperity and that the Christian Research Institute calls it a “doctrinally aberrant” form of Word of Faith theology. Here the thing being shared is the preaching itself, so this night meets the fourth mark more plainly than any political conference could.

“Christ Is King” in Dallas

“Christ Is King: America After Trump” met November 12–14, 2026, at the Irving Convention Center near Dallas, hosted by Pastor Joel Webbon’s New Christian Right, and this is the stage that drew the yoke charge. When critics objected to the non-Protestants on the program, the organizers answered in writing that the event “is explicitly a political conference,” and that “grown men have to be expected to understand the difference between a platform and a pulpit.” They cited Schaeffer by name, and on the principle they’re right.

The banner isn’t the fault, either. “Christ Is King” is a claim about the nations, and Scripture makes it to rulers directly: “Be wise now therefore, O ye kings: be instructed, ye judges of the earth. Serve the LORD with fear, and rejoice with trembling. Kiss the Son, lest he be angry” (Psalm 2:10–12). Our confession calls God “the supreme Lord and King of all the world” and sets every magistrate “under him.” A Christian may say that sentence from any stage, and Rome says it too, so the banner alone makes co-belligerents and no yoke. Still, the King of that psalm is the Son, and His crown can’t be cut loose from His gospel, which raises the stakes for whoever stands under the banner.

On Saturday night the closing keynote went to Nick Fuentes, whom Religion News Service describes as a Catholic, and RNS reports that when he said, “God has a name,” and, “His name is Jesus Christ,” the hall rose and chanted “Christ is King.” We were in the hall that night, and it sounded like a political rally, not a worship service. We say amen to the sentence, too. The gap between Rome’s gospel and ours is still real, because the church of Rome has bound itself to this and has never taken it back:

“If any one saith, that by faith alone the impious is justified; in such wise as to mean, that nothing else is required to co-operate in order to the obtaining the grace of Justification, and that it is not in any way necessary, that he be prepared and disposed by the movement of his own will; let him be anathema.” (Council of Trent, Session 6, Canon 9, Waterworth translation)

Rome’s present catechism still carries the heading “Outside the Church there is no salvation” (Catechism of the Catholic Church, 846). Paul’s word for a gospel that adds to faith is just as plain: “But though we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel unto you than that which we have preached unto you, let him be accursed” (Galatians 1:8).

So we understand the brothers who winced. A man whose church anathematizes justification by faith alone gave the closing keynote at a conference that carries Christ’s name, and a different choice for that slot would have spared the host the question. That’s a fair concern to bring to him. It settles only half of Paul’s phrase, though. Trent shows the two are unequal, and it still takes a yoke to break the command, so we put the night to the same four questions.

On direction, nobody who spoke or sat in Irving was bound to go anywhere with Fuentes once the weekend ended. On name, the organizers had said in writing that a platform isn’t a pulpit, so no speaker’s words were offered as the host’s or as the hall’s. On command, Fuentes had no authority over the program or over anyone in the room. He spoke as a guest, and when he finished, Webbon came back out, interviewed him and closed the night himself. On banner, nobody prayed under his lead or called his speech preaching, and the sentence the hall chanted is one Rome confesses too. The night answers no to all four.

The complaint we heard most was that a Roman Catholic got the last word. The host had it, as it happens, and the last word was never one of the marks. An earlier version of this post made that same charge, and when we ran our own test again it didn’t hold, so we’ve corrected it. Paul’s care for the weak still belongs in a host’s planning: “But take heed lest by any means this liberty of yours become a stumblingblock to them that are weak” (1 Corinthians 8:9). That’s counsel about wisdom, and it’s a different charge from an unequal yoke. Of the four platforms here, Dallas is the one that was accused and the one the test clears.

Most of the press covered what was said from that stage about race, which ChurchLeaders and RNS quote at length. That deserves its own post, since God “hath made of one blood all nations of men” (Acts 17:26).

A party platform

The Democratic Party’s national platform, adopted at its convention in August 2024 and still current, says, “With a Democratic Congress, we will pass national legislation to make Roe the law of the land again.” On marriage it says, “Democrats passed and President Biden signed the historic Respect for Marriage Act, enshrining marriage equality in federal law.” We call abortion child sacrifice because Scripture has already described a people who “shed innocent blood, even the blood of their sons and of their daughters, whom they sacrificed unto the idols of Canaan: and the land was polluted with blood” (Psalm 106:38). God defined marriage in Genesis 2:24, Christ confirmed it in Matthew 19:4–6, and no convention can amend it.

A voter doesn’t wear this yoke, because a ballot is one choice among the names in front of him. A man who runs under the party’s name, raises money on it and votes with its caucus has answered yes to direction, name and command, whatever he professes. President Biden told donors on June 27, 2023, “I happen to be a practicing Catholic. I’m not big on abortion. But guess what? Roe v. Wade got it right.” Senator Raphael Warnock, a Baptist pastor, described himself during his first campaign as a “pro-choice pastor.”

The Republican platform earns no pass from us. Its 2024 text hands abortion “to the States and to a vote of the People” and pledges to support “access to Birth Control, and IVF (fertility treatments),” a practice that routinely freezes or throws away human embryos. The test doesn’t care which letter follows a candidate’s name.

Why the silence matters

Bring your concerns about Dallas to the men who planned it, because some of them are fair. But the word “yoke” was spent in November on the one stage where the marks don’t show, and nobody spoke it in March, when a rabbi led a Christian crowd in prayer. A standard that only comes out against people we already dislike has become a weapon, and God hates it: “Divers weights, and divers measures, both of them are alike abomination to the LORD” (Proverbs 20:10). When the world watches Christians call a yoke unequal only when the other tribe wears it, the world decides the verse is a club and stops listening when we say that Christ alone saves.

Work beside the rabbi, the Roman Catholic and the unbeliever on any just cause, and tell each of them plainly, and in love, that salvation is in Christ alone through faith alone. Don’t pray under their lead, don’t lend them the gospel’s name, and don’t take a party’s name when its platform promises what God calls bloodshed.

One yoke

A Christian refuses a second yoke because he already wears one. “Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls” (Matthew 11:29). No party, movement or coalition has ever said that to anyone and kept the promise, so “Put not your trust in princes” (Psalm 146:3). Christ is King over every stage and every ballot, and His is the only yoke that gives a man rest.
