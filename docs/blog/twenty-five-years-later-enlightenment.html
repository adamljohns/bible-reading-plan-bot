Twenty-Five Years Later: What a Midshipman Got Right (and Wrong) About the Enlightenment

By Adam “MOOP” Johns · U.S.M.C. Ministries · January 2001 paper + 2026 reflection
Listen
Narration in the voice of Professor X (OpenClaw agent Xavier Archimedes) — a companion reading of this reflection, not a read-aloud of the 2001 paper.
This is not a dump of the academy paper into the website. It is me, twenty-five years later, rereading my own homework. Baseline: MIDN Adam L. Johns, 1/c, Kings Point (H-253, January 2001). The original scanned PDF is linked at the bottom of this article.

Why dig this up?

In January 2001 I turned in a twelve-page argument with a stubborn close: God is real, the Bible is true, and we are frail and fallible—and I hoped the reader had done his homework too. I was a midshipman at Kings Point, writing for H-253 under Professor Szaluta, and I meant every line even when the footnotes were still learning how to walk.

Twenty-five years later I am a husband, a father, a Marine veteran, and a ministry builder. The Enlightenment did not get quieter. It got into our phones, our schools, the church’s embarrassment about miracles, and the way men talk about sin as if it were only a branding problem. So the old paper is not nostalgia for me. It is a baseline. What did that twenty-one-year-old see that still maps the battlefield? Where was I generous to a fault? Where do I tighten the bolts now?

What I argued in 2001

I gave the Enlightenment its due. I named the scientific method, critical thinking, and a real hunger for human betterment. I saw the break from mere traditionalism—creativity in learning, art, and government. I traced Locke’s natural-rights stream into Jefferson and the Declaration. I respected Kant’s insistence that public reason be scholarly, not a free-for-all of unformed opinion. And I admired the monumental tools of the republic of letters, Diderot’s Encyclopedia among them.

I also refused to soft-pedal the damage. “Nothing by faith” is spiritual acid. Bible credibility took hits when the real culprit was often bad interpretation—geocentrism taught as dogma even though Scripture never required it. Science and theology talked past each other for lack of humility. Progress fattened the pocketbook while the soul thinned; Rousseau’s warning was already in my ear. Locke’s blank slate and the denial of inherent evil looked naive the moment you had met a prodigal raised in love. And I closed where I still stand: Newton, Kepler, and Galileo at their best pointed glory past themselves to the Creator; Scripture read carefully is not the enemy of honest observation; only Christ is infallible. Pick a side with your eyes open.

That was a midshipman’s paper. In outline it is still a ministry problem set.

What still holds—and why it matters more now

Everyone has faith. The question is where it lies. Secular modernity pretends neutrality, but it trusts method, market, self, or the next expert consensus. Romans 1 does not describe empty worship; it describes worship pointed at the creature. When a son tells me he “only believes science,” he has not escaped faith. He has relocated it. I still refuse to let “faith” be used as a slur. What I add now is sharper: faith’s object must be the God who speaks in Scripture and who raised Jesus bodily—not a vague spiritual mood that baptizes whatever feels empowering.

Young me saw that “earth at the center” was often a church interpretation problem, not a verse problem. That instinct was gold. Galileo’s conflict was tangled with power, Aristotelian physics, and institutional pride as much as with telescopes. I still want humility about tradition and courage to reopen the text. What I tighten now is the frame. The answer is not “Bible versus science” as two equal magisteria fighting for scraps. The answer is that the God who made heaven and earth is not threatened by careful measurement of what He made. Where Scripture speaks, it speaks truly. Where the church strapped Aristotle to the pulpit, the church needed repentance—not a new pope of the lab coat. My old catalogs of “Bible consistent with astronomy” were earnest and often suggestive, but a mature reading distinguishes what the text asserts from what a clever modern can reverse-engineer. The impulse was right. The method can grow up without surrendering inerrancy.

I asked then whether we were fattening the pocketbook while thinning the soul. Twenty-five years of Western “progress” have given us medicine, bandwidth, and loneliness; rights language and fatherlessness; data and despair. Rousseau no longer sounds like a museum quote. He sounds like a youth-group testimony. My postmillennial hope does not deny that. It says the leaven of the kingdom works through history by the Word and Spirit—not by autonomous reason congratulating itself. Optimistic eschatology without repentance is just Enlightenment with better branding.

Locke’s “no innate corruption, only experience” flatters control until a well-loved child chooses folly, or until I watch my own heart invent new justifications. Scripture is harder and more hopeful: we are image-bearers and fallen; environment matters and the heart must be made new. You do not raise sons by optimizing only inputs. You preach, correct, love, and plead with God for new hearts while still building an ordered home. Character has to hold under real load.

The Hamlet close was pure Kings Point: better decisive obedience than infinite internal debate. That still preaches to men who research their lives instead of living them. What I under-weighted then is the guardrail. Action is not self-justifying. Enlightenment “agency” without the fear of the Lord becomes the restless empire of the self. Biblical action is faith working through love under Christ’s lordship—motion with the right freight, not motion for its own sake. That is also why elder vocation matters to me now. The gray head’s job is not only to win 2001’s term-paper argument again. It is to hand the argument down with humility, scars, and a living example—without rotting into leisure or breaking under a grind that never shifts.

What I could not yet see

Honesty honors the paper and the man who wrote it. Broad survey energy needed tighter primary-source discipline. Instinct that church error is not biblical error needed confessional guardrails and better hermeneutics. Defense of theistic scientists needed a clearer Creator/creature distinction so Newton does not become a mascot for every modern fight. Critique of secular progress needed a constructive alternative: church, household, and lawful liberty under God—not only “they’re wrong.” The individual “I’ve picked my side” close needed covenant community and multiplying disciples, because conviction that stays private never rebuilds a town. My historical sketches needed truer Reformation history, including Protestant sins. And the creationist mix in the bibliography can keep the right to dissent from materialist orthodoxy while upgrading quality so the whole case is not outsourced to one thin web page.

None of that scolds the A/B+ paper. It is what twenty-five years of scars, marriage, command, and study are for.

The Enlightenment as a discipleship problem

The Enlightenment did not only change labs. It trained a type of man—suspicious of inherited authority, confident in technique, thin on original sin, hungry for progress metrics, and unsure what to do with worship. That man still walks into Spotswood, Freedom Group, Proven Men, and my living room. If I market hard-edged discipleship as if everyone is already an early adopter of repentance, I dilute the edge and lose the ready. Preach broadly. Disciple selectively. If I only curse “the Enlightenment” without offering a thicker Christendom of household and church, I hand the practical field back to the very rationality I critique.

So the work looks like this for me: preach a universal gospel in which reason is a gift and reason is not God; invest intensively in men who will admit sin nature, receive Scripture as final, and take a load; put the household first, because if ordered love does not cross the front door no essay about Locke will save the republic; keep civic memory that liberty needs virtue, not only procedures; and spend the second half of life handing strength down instead of polishing old arguments for their own sake.

A letter across twenty-five years

To MIDN Johns, 1/c —

You were right that intellect can be a battlefield and that faith is unavoidable. You were right to refuse a forced choice between telescopes and the God of heaven. You were right that progress can polish a culture while hollowing its soul. You were right that only Christ is infallible.

You did not yet know how expensive those truths become when you lead a wife, raise sons, bury illusions about your own virtue, and try to restore a town instead of only a paragraph. That is fine. The Lord was not grading you on 2026 in 2001.

Keep the stubborn close. Lose the need to win every side-skirmish of science rhetoric. Trade some survey breadth for confessional depth. And remember: the point was never to be the smartest man in H-253. The point was—and is—to fear God, love the truth, and act.

From the man you became.
