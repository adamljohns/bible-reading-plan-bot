How to Disagree Without Lying About the Other Man

By U.S.M.C. Ministries · drafted with AI assistance, reviewed by Adam Johns

This post is Adam Johns’s USMC Ministries synthesis with Scripture and men’s-group application — not a reprint of Carlen’s handout. The short list of fallacies is in our own words.
We do not lie about another man’s position in order to feel brave.

The fight starts one step too early

Most hard conversations fail before the first real argument, and they fail in the same three ways: we skip the man and attack the cartoon, we debate what we think he believes, or we sort him into a tribe and then argue with the tribe. It can feel like courage while we’re doing it, but it’s laziness with a loudspeaker.

Scripture doesn’t call men to silence. It calls us to truth in love (Eph 4:15), to gentleness and respect even when we give a defense (1 Pet 3:15), to slow speech and slow anger (Jas 1:19–20), and to speech that is gracious, seasoned, answer-ready (Col 4:6), and Proverbs treats reckless words as violence and fitting words as life (Prov 12:18; 15:1; 18:13). So what follows isn’t a “be nicer online” lecture but a procedure for men who still believe words matter: husbands, fathers, team leaders, deacons, Marines in the group text, brothers at the table after church. It has two tools, and you need both of them. The Three NOs are what you keep out of the conversation altogether, and the Four Parts are the order of battle before and during the exchange. After those comes a short field kit of five fallacies that wreck the room.

The Three NOs

The first is no personal attacks, which means no name-calling, no demeaning shots, and no moral buzzword grenades meant to end the person instead of testing the claim. You attack the claim and you honor the image-bearer, and if you need a heat check you can ask yourself, Would I say this to his face with his son standing next to him?

The second is no characterizations. Speak to what he said, and not to your fan-fiction about what it “must mean.” The weak move is “If you believe that, then you basically support X,” and the clean move is “When you said ___, do you mean ___?” Most “debates” are two people arguing with ghosts, so kill the ghosts and ask the man.

The third is no grouping the man into a blob. He isn’t “the Democrats,” “the Republicans,” “those people,” “men,” “women,” or your least favorite timeline account. He is one soul with one history and one set of reasons, and while categories can be discussed, he is not a mascot for a category. If you must compare patterns or groups, do it cleanly and invite correction: “Here’s a pattern I’ve seen — does that fit you, or am I missing something?”

The Four Parts

Part 1 is to humanize the other man, because respect has to come before pressure, and you’re less likely to torch somebody you have really seen. Ask him human questions that aren’t ammunition, about his childhood, his family load, his greatest hard season and what he’s carrying now, remembering as you ask that you’re not collecting dirt and he’s not an avatar. Where did you grow up, and what was home like? What has been one of the heaviest seasons of your life? What do you spend your strength on when no one is watching? Common ground found this way is no compromise, only accuracy about the human in front of you.

Part 2 is to get permission, which is to ask whether the two of you can even have this talk. Some topics are live wires because of past suffering, timing, authority, or sheer heat, and it’s better to find that out before you charge. Permission here is about timing and mutual readiness for an exchange between peers, and it gives no one a veto over truth, over a parent’s or an elder’s duty, or over a needed rebuke when Scripture already binds the matter. So ask permission, name the rules, test objectivity, and gauge how much voltage the subject has for him personally. Can we talk about ___ right now? Are you willing to run the three NOs with me? On a scale of 1–10, how hot is this for you personally today? Do you have enough bandwidth for a careful conversation, or do we table it? A fight postponed under wisdom beats a righteous dumpster fire on group night.

Part 3 is to clarify viewpoints, with no debate yet. Both men put their cards on the table and map where they agree and where they don’t, and no one litigates yet. Each states his view and his main reasons, and the listener’s job is to understand, not to win. Yes-or-no checkpoints are allowed: “Did I understand you?” “Do you see why that premise matters to me?” Here is a small worked example, taken from household and church load and not from cable news:

“Here’s my view, then I want yours. I think our men’s group should spend the next six weeks on confession and brotherhood because several homes are quiet-bleeding and we keep skipping the wound to study another outline. I care about doctrine — I’m not anti-study — but I think presence and honesty are the bottleneck right now. Do you understand why I’m prioritizing that? Where do you already agree? Where do you think I’m wrong? I’ll shut up and take your full view next.”

Notice what did not happen in that: there was no “you’re shallow,” no “you just want therapy hour,” no “your camp always does this.” He gave his viewpoint and stopped.

Part 4 is to discuss, and then to land the plane. Now you engage the true points of friction, with the NOs still in force. Ask for his reasons and offer yours, pressure-test both, and look for the steel-man version of the other side. Then end with a landing, which will be agreement, or partial agreement with a next step, or disagreeing agreeably with the relationship intact and the terms clear. Winning a point while losing a brother is a dumb trade, and so is a “unity” that was only silence. One more thing came from the margin of the handout, and it mattered: make every effort to avoid logical fallacies here, because fallacies are how Part 4 turns back into a street fight wearing a suit.

Five fallacies that wreck the room

What follows is a pocket card for men under pressure, not a textbook list. Ad hominem is attacking the man to avoid the claim: “Of course you’d say that — you’re just bitter / soft / partisan.” The fix is to answer the point as if a respected friend had said it. A straw man refutes a weaker version that no one owns: “So you want us to do nothing / ban all questions / burn the group down.” The fix is to quote his own sentence, and to ask him to correct your restatement before you argue. A false dilemma offers only two boxes when God and reality left a hallway: “Either we crush this or we’re cowards.” The fix is to ask, “What is a third faithful option we haven’t named?”

A hasty generalization turns one story into a universe: “Men these days never lead.” The fix is to shrink the claim to the evidence you have. And tu quoque (“you too”) points at hypocrisy to escape the issue: “You didn’t handle this well last year, so your standard is void.” The fix is to remember that hypocrisy may need its own confession later, but it doesn’t make a true claim false. There are longer academic lists as well, and they’re worth studying on your own.

In the house, at the table, at work, online

You can run this anywhere the load is real. In a marriage, humanizing means asking what the week cost her (or him); permission is “Is now the time for the money talk?”; clarifying means each of you states the fear and the ask; and the discussion ends when you decide one next obedience. At the men’s table you hear the man’s story before his take, you ask “Can this room handle politics tonight?”, you get the views on the text or the issue out first, and then you argue the text, not the jersey. At work or on a team you respect the constraint he’s under, you ask “Are we deciding or just venting?”, you define the success metrics, and you disagree on method while you lock the mission. Online you remember there is a face, the answer to the permission question is often don’t start, and if you must, you restate the steel-man and prefer a direct message and an off-ramp over arena combat. And there’s a one-sentence rule for sons: we do not lie about another man’s position in order to feel brave.

What this is not

None of this is conflict avoidance, and it doesn’t mean “both sides are always equal.” It puts no ban on hard truth, prophetic rebuke, church discipline, or clear moral judgment, and it doesn’t require you to get a sinner’s permission before obeying Matthew 18 or naming what God has already named. It also doesn’t demand that every topic be discussable with every person at every hour. Some things are false and some lines are bright. Procedure doesn’t relativize truth; what it does is keep you from bearing false witness on your way to saying true things (Ex 20:16; Prov 18:17).

The goal of constructive dialogue isn’t endless process but clarity with charity. You guard the man with the Three NOs, you see the man in Part 1, you honor timing and readiness for a conversation between peers in Part 2, you hear his true view in Part 3, and you press the true issue in Part 4. Then you end in agreement, or you disagree agreeably without rewriting the other man into a villain. In a culture trained to perform contempt this will feel slow, and that’s good, because slow is how you stop lying.
