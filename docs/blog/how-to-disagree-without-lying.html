How to Disagree Without Lying About the Other Man

By Adam “MOOP” Johns · U.S.M.C. Ministries · Pastoral voice: Chaplain Chuck Whitfield · Research spine: Prof X · Exegesis: Preacher John
Listen · BG Gus Hartwell
~10 min · original USMC Ministries formation post
Original formation synthesis for Family Captain / men’s-group readers. Sparked by a printed handout, Rules for Effective and Constructive Dialogue (3 NOs + 4 Parts) — not a reprint. Fallacies field kit in our own words; third-party fallacy catalogs stay private study only.
We do not lie about another man’s position in order to feel brave.

The fight starts one step too early

Most hard conversations fail before the first real argument.

They fail when we skip the man and attack the cartoon. They fail when we debate what we think he believes. They fail when we sort him into a tribe and then argue with the tribe.

That is not courage. That is laziness with a loudspeaker.

Scripture does not call men to silence. It calls us to truth in love (Eph 4:15), gentleness and respect even when we give a defense (1 Pet 3:15), slow speech and slow anger (Jas 1:19–20), and speech that is gracious, seasoned, answer-ready (Col 4:6). Proverbs treats reckless words as violence and fitting words as life (Prov 12:18; 15:1; 18:13).

So this is not a “be nicer online” lecture. It is a procedure for men who still believe words matter — husbands, fathers, team leaders, deacons, Marines in the group text, brothers at the table after church.

Two tools. Use both.

  1. Three NOs — what never enters the room
  2. Four Parts — the order of battle before and during the exchange

Then a short field kit: five fallacies that wreck the room.

The Three NOs

1. No personal attacks

No name-calling. No demeaning shots. No moral buzzword grenades meant to end the person instead of test the claim.

Attack the claim. Honor the image-bearer.

If you need a heat check, ask: Would I say this to his face with his son in the room?

2. No characterizations

Speak to what was actually said — not your fan-fiction about what it “must mean.”

Weak move: “If you believe that, then you basically support X.” Clean move: “When you said ___, do you mean ___?”

Most “debates” are two people arguing with ghosts. Kill the ghosts. Ask the man.

3. No grouping the man into a blob

He is not “the Democrats,” “the Republicans,” “those people,” “men,” “women,” or your least favorite timeline account.

He is one soul, one history, one set of reasons. Categories can be discussed. He is not a mascot for a category.

If you must compare patterns or groups, do it cleanly and invite correction: “Here’s a pattern I’ve seen — does that fit you, or am I missing something?”

The Four Parts

Part 1 — Humanize the other

Purpose: Respect before pressure. You are less likely to torch a man you have actually seen.

How: Ask human questions that are not ammunition. Childhood, family load, greatest hard season, what he carries now. You are not collecting dirt. You are remembering he is not an avatar.

Sample questions:

  • Where did you grow up, and what was home like?
  • What has been one of the heaviest seasons of your life?
  • What do you spend your strength on when nobody is watching?

Common ground is not compromise. It is accuracy about the human in front of you.

Part 2 — Get permission (can we even have this talk?)

Purpose: Some topics are live wires because of trauma, timing, authority, or sheer heat. Find out before you charge. Permission here is about timing and mutual readiness for a peer exchange — not a veto on truth, parental or elder duty, or needed rebuke when Scripture already binds the matter.

How: Ask permission. Name the rules. Test objectivity. Gauge personal voltage.

Sample questions:

  • Can we talk about ___ right now?
  • Are you willing to run the three NOs with me?
  • On a scale of 1–10, how hot is this for you personally today?
  • Do you have enough bandwidth for a careful conversation, or do we table it?

A postponed fight under wisdom beats a righteous dumpster fire on group night.

Part 3 — Clarify viewpoints (no debate yet)

Purpose: Put both cards on the table. Map agreement and disagreement. Do not litigate yet.

How: Each person states his view and the main reasons. The listener’s job is understanding, not winning. Yes/no checkpoints are allowed: “Did I understand you?” “Do you see why that premise matters to me?”

Worked micro-example (household / church load — not cable news):

“Here’s my view, then I want yours. I think our men’s group should spend the next six weeks on confession and brotherhood because several homes are quiet-bleeding and we keep skipping the wound to study another outline. I care about doctrine — I’m not anti-study — but I think presence and honesty are the bottleneck right now. Do you understand why I’m prioritizing that? Where do you already agree? Where do you think I’m wrong? I’ll shut up and take your full view next.”

Notice what did not happen: no “you’re shallow,” no “you just want therapy hour,” no “your camp always does this.” Viewpoint only.

Part 4 — Discuss — then land the plane

Purpose: Now engage the real points of friction with the NOs still in force.

How: Ask for reasons. Offer yours. Pressure-test. Look for steel-man versions of the other side. End with a landing:

  • agreement,
  • partial agreement + next step, or
  • disagree agreeably with relationship intact and terms clear.

Winning a point while losing a brother is a dumb trade. So is “unity” that was only silence.

Operator add-on (from the handout margin that mattered): make every effort to avoid logical fallacies here. Fallacies are how Part 4 turns back into a street fight wearing a suit.

Five fallacies that wreck the room

Not a textbook list. A pocket card for men under pressure.

1. Ad hominem

Attack the man to avoid the claim. “Of course you’d say that — you’re just bitter / soft / partisan.” Fix: Answer the point as if a respected friend said it.

2. Straw man

Refute a weaker version nobody owns. “So you want us to do nothing / ban all questions / burn the group down.” Fix: Quote his actual sentence. Ask him to correct your restatement before you argue.

3. False dilemma

Only two boxes when God and reality left a hallway. “Either we crush this or we’re cowards.” Fix: Ask, “What is a third faithful option we haven’t named?”

4. Hasty generalization

One story becomes a universe. “Men these days never lead.” Fix: Shrink the claim to the evidence you actually have.

5. Tu quoque (“you too”)

Point at hypocrisy to escape the issue. “You didn’t handle this well last year, so your standard is void.” Fix: Hypocrisy may need its own confession later. It does not make a true claim false.

One-sentence rule for sons: We do not lie about another man’s position in order to feel brave.

What this is not

  • Not conflict avoidance
  • Not “both sides are always equal”
  • Not a ban on hard truth, prophetic rebuke, church discipline, or clear moral judgment — and not a requirement to get a sinner’s permission before obeying Matthew 18 or naming what God has already named
  • Not a demand that every topic is discussable with every person at every hour

Some things are false. Some lines are bright. Procedure does not relativize truth — it keeps you from bearing false witness on your way to saying true things (Ex 20:16; Prov 18:17).

Close: land the plane

The goal of constructive dialogue is not endless process. It is clarity with charity:

  1. Guard the man (Three NOs).
  2. See the man (Part 1).
  3. Honor timing and readiness for peer dialogue (Part 2).
  4. Hear the real view (Part 3).
  5. Press the real issue (Part 4).
  6. End in agreement — or disagree agreeably without rewriting the other man into a villain.

In a culture trained to perform contempt, this will feel slow.

Good.

Slow is how you stop lying.
