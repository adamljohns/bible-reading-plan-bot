What Michael Foster Gets Right About Masculine Maturity
(And What the Church Keeps Getting Wrong)

By Adam "MOOP" Johns  ·  U.S.M.C. Ministries  ·  March 27, 2026  ·  Watch the full conversation ↗

Michael Foster, pastor and co-author of It's Good to Be a Man, sat down with Jon Harris on Conversations That Matter to talk about something the American church seems to have been allergic to for decades, which is what a mature Christian man looks like, and I'd like to share what stood out to me.

Mature masculinity begins at home. A man who leads his household shapes generations.

The Core Thesis: Leadership Is Vision-Casting

Foster makes a simple point that I think most church leadership books miss entirely:

A good leader, whether father, pastor, coach, or boss, is in the business of getting his followers to go in the direction he wants to lead them, to convince them of the vision.

Leadership isn't facilitating a group discussion about where everyone feels like going, and it's not taking a vote in order to abide by some consensus, and it's also not sitting there silent until someone asks for your opinion. A mature man has a vision, he articulates it clearly, he leads people in that direction, and he takes responsibility for the outcome, whether it goes well or not.

This is Nehemiah rebuilding the wall (Nehemiah 2:17), and it's Joshua telling Israel to choose this day whom they will serve (Joshua 24:15). It's also a father sitting his family down on a Sunday night and saying, not asking, "Here's what we're doing this week, and here's why." There's nothing toxic about that kind of masculinity; it's just a normal Tuesday for a real man.

The Church Has Feminized Male Leadership

Foster doesn't dance around this, and neither should we. The modern Western church has systematically replaced masculine initiative with three things. The first is consensus-seeking: "Let's form a committee to pray about whether we should form a committee." The second is emotional safety as the highest value. A young man in the comments shared how his friend was formally disciplined by a pastor for asking a girl out in a way that made her uncomfortable. Think about this: she wasn't harassed, she wasn't stalked, she was just asked out, and the young man got called into the pastor's office for it. Yes, this is one anecdotal story, but I'm sure it's not the only time it's happened. The third is risk-aversion disguised as wisdom, where "We just want to make sure everyone feels safe" means "We'd rather nothing happen than risk something great."

The result is that men leave, and sometimes it's even worse than that, because the men stay and become even more passive. They might show up on Sunday, sit silently, and give when asked, but they never lead anything, and the church gets what it cultivates. Foster's answer is to stop apologizing for masculine initiative and start channeling it.

What This Looks Like in Practice

Many men I have met as I've served in men's ministry face some version of the same question Foster raises: "How do I lead without getting questioned about it, or even punished for it, by the culture, or even my church?" I think there are four things they need to hear.

First, lead your home. You don't need anyone's permission to be the spiritual leader of your household. Your wife doesn't need to "feel led" before you start leading, and your kids don't need to vote on what the family devotions will be. So establish a weekly family meeting, and make sure you set an agenda, open with prayer, and review the week ahead, because the responsibility is on you to cast vision for what your family culture should look like. It is also the man's responsibility to plan each and every day in accordance with his vision, and he should document and track everything from Bible readings to primary objectives to the overall health of his family.

Second, build a band of brothers. Foster emphasizes surplus, which is having more than you need so you can deploy it for others. A man with surplus doesn't just survive; he builds, he gives, and he leads, but you can't build surplus alone. So find three men that you can meet with weekly, and think of it as your war council and not as a small group. Share your vision, your struggles, and your finances (yes, even your finances), and hold each other to standards no one else will.

Third, initiate, even when it's costly. One of the saddest comments on Foster's video came from a man describing church dating culture as "walking on eggshells." Men are afraid to approach women, afraid to lead, and afraid to speak directly, because being direct is, for some reason, now coded as some sort of aggression that people are afraid of. Initiate anyway. Ask the girl out if you're single and pursuing a wife, volunteer to lead in areas where you're gifted, and speak up in meetings, recognizing that yes, you will be misunderstood and you will make some people uncomfortable, which is a feature of leadership and not a bug.

"If everyone is comfortable with your leadership, you're not leading. You're managing."

Fourth, read It's Good to Be a Man. Foster and Tennant wrote the book I wish existed when I was a 25-year-old Marine officer trying to figure out how faith and masculinity intersected. It's theologically grounded, practically applicable, and unapologetically patriarchal in the biblical sense, as opposed to the cultural caricature. There are other books worth your time as well:

  • The Masculine Mandate by Richard Phillips
  • Disciplines of a Godly Man by R. Kent Hughes
  • No More Mr. Nice Guy by Robert Glover (secular but useful for diagnosis)

The Bottom Line

Michael Foster is saying what a lot of pastors are afraid to say: the church needs men who will lead, and the church needs to stop punishing them for doing it. Maturity isn't comfort, and it isn't "emotional intelligence" (though that helps) or sensitivity training. Maturity is vision + initiative + responsibility + endurance. It looks like a father who sets the spiritual temperature of his home, a husband who pursues his wife strategically as well as romantically, and a citizen who engages his community with solutions instead of complaints. It looks like a man who knows where he's going, knows why he's going there, and invites others to come along.

That's what I'm aiming to build with U.S.M.C. Ministries, it's what the Watchman Bible Plan instills, and it's what Michael Foster is fighting for. I'm glad he's out there, and we need more men like him.

"Watch, stand fast in the faith, be brave, be strong. Let all that you do be done with love." (1 Corinthians 16:13–14, NKJV)
