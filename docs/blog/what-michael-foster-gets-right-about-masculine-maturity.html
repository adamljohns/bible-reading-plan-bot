What Michael Foster Gets Right About Masculine Maturity (And What the Church Keeps Getting Wrong)

March 21, 2026 · Ministry & Discipleship

By Adam "MOOP" Johns | U.S.M.C. Ministries | March 2026

Notes from "Michael Foster on Masculine Maturity" — Conversations That Matter podcast

Watch the full conversation

Michael Foster — pastor, co-author of It's Good to Be a Man — sat down with Jon Harris on Conversations That Matter to talk about something the American church has been allergic to for decades: what a mature Christian man actually looks like.

I watched this while fasting on a Friday morning, and I found myself nodding so hard my coffee almost spilled. (It's black. I'm fasting. Don't worry about it.)

Here's what stood out.

The Core Thesis: Leadership Is Vision-Casting

Foster makes a deceptively simple point that most church leadership books miss entirely:

A good leader — whether father, pastor, coach, or boss — is in the business of getting his charges to go in the direction he wants to lead them. To convince them of the vision.

Read that again.

Not to facilitate a group discussion about where everyone feels like going.
Not to take a vote and follow the consensus.
Not to sit quietly until someone asks for his opinion.

A mature man has a vision. He articulates it clearly. He leads people toward it. And he takes responsibility for the outcome — whether it goes well or not.

This is Nehemiah rebuilding the wall. This is Joshua telling Israel to choose whom they will serve. This is a father sitting his family down on a Sunday night and saying, "Here's what we're doing this week, and here's why."

This is not toxic masculinity. This is Tuesday.

The Church Has Feminized Male Leadership

Foster doesn't dance around this, and neither will I.

The modern Western church has systematically replaced masculine initiative with:

  • Consensus-seeking — "Let's form a committee to pray about whether we should form a committee."
  • Emotional safety as the highest value — A young man in the comments shared how his friend was formally disciplined by a pastor for asking a girl out in a way that made her uncomfortable. Not harassed. Not stalked. Asked out. And got called into the pastor's office.
  • Risk-aversion disguised as wisdom — "We just want to make sure everyone feels safe" = "We'd rather nothing happen than risk something great."

The result? Men leave. Or worse — they stay and become passive. They show up on Sundays, sit quietly, give when asked, and never lead anything. The church gets what it cultivates.

Foster's answer: stop apologizing for masculine initiative and start channeling it.

What This Looks Like in Practice

I run a ministry called U.S.M.C. Ministries — Uniting, Serving, Mentoring, and Counseling. Every man I work with faces some version of the same question Foster raises: "How do I lead without the culture — or even my church — punishing me for it?"

Here's what I tell them:

1. Lead Your Home First

You don't need anyone's permission to be the spiritual leader of your household. Your wife doesn't need to "feel led" before you start leading. Your kids don't need to vote on family devotions.

Action: Establish a weekly family meeting. Set the agenda. Open with prayer. Review the week. Cast vision for the next one. I use what I call a "Wheelhouse" — a daily command document that tracks everything from Bible readings to family temperature checks.

2. Build a Band of Brothers

Foster emphasizes surplus — having more than you need so you can deploy for others. You can't do that alone.

Action: Find three men. Meet weekly. Not a small group — a war council. Share your vision, your struggles, your finances (yes, your finances). Hold each other to standards no one else will.

I'm part of what we call the Heartbeat group — family captains who check in regularly on marriage health, parenting, spiritual disciplines, and leadership. No spectators. No passengers.

3. Initiate — Even When It's Costly

One of the most painful comments on Foster's video was from a man who described church dating culture as "walking on eggshells." Men are afraid to approach women, afraid to lead, afraid to speak directly — because directness is now coded as aggression.

Action: Initiate anyway. Ask her out. Volunteer to lead. Speak up in the meeting. You will be misunderstood. You will make some people uncomfortable. That's not a bug — it's a feature of leadership.

As I tell the men I mentor: "If everyone is comfortable with your leadership, you're not leading. You're managing."

4. Read It's Good to Be a Man

I'm not getting paid to say this. (I don't do sponsorships — see my last blog post.) Foster and Tennant wrote the book I wish existed when I was a 25-year-old Marine officer trying to figure out how faith and masculinity intersected. It's theologically grounded, practically applicable, and unapologetically patriarchal in the biblical sense — not the cultural caricature.

Other books worth your time:

  • The Masculine Mandate by Richard Phillips
  • Disciplines of a Godly Man by R. Kent Hughes
  • No More Mr. Nice Guy by Robert Glover (secular but useful for diagnosis)

On Self-Publishing and Getting Your Message Out

Foster published through Canon Press (Doug Wilson's operation). If you're a man with a message — and I believe every mature Christian man has one — here are your options for getting it into the world:

  1. Canon Press — If your theology aligns with Moscow, Idaho, this is a strong fit. They handle editing, design, distribution.
  2. Amazon KDP — Zero cost to publish. You keep 70% of royalties. Full creative control. Start here if you're testing the waters.
  3. Your own imprint — Create a brand. "Watchman Press" or whatever fits your ministry. Use KDP or IngramSpark for printing and distribution, but it's YOUR name on the spine.
  4. Start with what you have. I've got 149 blog posts spanning 15 years, a dictionary with 50+ biblically-defined English words, and a Bible reading plan with 5 daily watches. That's three books waiting to be compiled. You probably have more material than you think.

The point isn't to become a bestseller. The point is stewardship. If God gave you insight, hoarding it is disobedience. Get it out there.

The Bottom Line

Michael Foster is saying what a lot of pastors are afraid to say: the church needs men who will lead, and the church needs to stop punishing them for doing it.

Maturity isn't comfort. It's not "emotional intelligence" (though that helps). It's not sensitivity training.

Maturity is vision + initiative + responsibility + endurance.

It's a father who sets the spiritual temperature of his home.
It's a husband who pursues his wife — not just romantically, but strategically.
It's a citizen who engages his community — not with complaints, but with solutions.

It's a man who knows where he's going, knows why he's going there, and invites others to come along.

That's what we're building at U.S.M.C. Ministries. That's what the Watchman Bible Plan trains. And that's what Michael Foster is fighting for.

I'm glad he's out there. We need more men like him.
