Plus Ultra

Go beyond your limit. Do not coast. And do not, for one second, think you're the engine.

By Adam "MOOP" Johns  ·  U.S.M.C. Ministries  ·  Written for my son Gideon  ·  July 2026

So Gideon and I were back on the couch, Son, deep into another stretch of My Hero Academia, and the whole world of that show runs on two words the kids shout when they've got nothing left in the tank: Plus Ultra. It's the school motto, the battle cry, the thing they scream right at the moment their arms should be done. It means, roughly, go beyond. Beyond the limit you thought was the limit. And I sat there thinking, that's a good word, that's a true word, and it's also about two degrees off from a cliff.

Here's what the phrase gets right. There is a lie loose in your generation that says the mature move is to find your comfortable ceiling and decorate underneath it. Manage your energy. Protect your peace. Don't overextend. And into that soft, climate-controlled little room the anime kicks the door open and yells go beyond, and something in a young man's chest stands up straight when it hears that. Good. It should. God did not knit you together to idle. Boys coast. Men climb.

But here's the thing the show can't tell you, because it doesn't know it. Go beyond toward what? Beyond your limit for whose glory? A man can strain every fiber he's got in the wrong direction and all he's built is a faster way to be lost. So before I hand you the battle cry, Son, I want to hand you the compass. Because Paul was screaming Plus Ultra in a Roman jail cell centuries before anybody drew it on a school gate, and Paul told us exactly where to run.

“Brothers, I do not regard myself as having yet taken hold of it. But one thing I do — forgetting what lies behind, and reaching forward to what lies ahead — I press on toward the goal for the prize of the upward call of God in Christ Jesus.” (Philippians 3:13-14)

Read that twice. Straining forward. The Greek there is the picture of a runner at full lean, chest out over his own feet, everything behind him already given away and everything in him thrown at what's ahead. That is not a man protecting his peace. That is Plus Ultra with a destination bolted on the front of it. And notice what the goal is: not a trophy, not a ceiling he broke, not applause from the stands, but the upward call of God in Christ Jesus. A Person. The prize is a Person.

Run Like You Mean to Win It

Paul isn't shy about the athletics, either. He grew up around the games; he knew exactly how a man trains when a real prize is on the line.

“Do you not know that those who run in a race all run, but only one receives the prize? Run in such a way that you may win.” (1 Corinthians 9:24)

So run. Not jog. Not show up and see how you feel. Run like the finish is worth your lungs. This is the same Paul, a few verses down, who says he disciplines his own body and keeps it under, so that after preaching to everybody else he doesn't himself get disqualified. That's the Marine in me nodding along, Son. You don't rise to the level of your hopes; you fall to the level of your training. A man who intends to press on does the unglamorous roadwork in the dark when nobody's filming, because he's decided ahead of time that the finish line is worth more than the couch.

And here's where I need you to hear the difference between the world's grind and the Christian's endurance, because they look identical from the bleachers and they could not be more opposite under the hood.

You Are Not the Engine

The anime version of Plus Ultra is, at bottom, self-generated. Dig deep. Find it in yourself. You are the source; strain harder and the well goes deeper. And for a while, that works, right up until the day it doesn't, and the young man who was told he was the engine burns the engine down and calls the ashes failure. That's the shadow side of the battle cry. Every gospel of self-effort ends in either arrogance or burnout, and usually both, in that order.

The Christian presses on from a completely different fuel source. Watch the two verses sit next to each other. Paul says, work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, and then in the very next breath:

“For it is God who is at work in you — both to will and to work for His good pleasure.” (Philippians 2:13)

Read that again, because it is the hinge of the whole post. You strain forward, and it is God who is doing the willing and the working inside you the whole time. Your Plus Ultra is not you squeezing a dry sponge; it is you leaning hard into a strength that isn't yours, that never runs out, that was poured into you by grace before you ran a single step. This is why we press on and don't collapse into pride, and it's why we press on and don't collapse into despair. If I win, He gets the glory, because He was the one working. If I stumble, I'm not the engine anyway, so I get back up and lean in again. Son, the theme of my life is getting back up, because I've fallen so much, and I can keep getting up precisely because the strength was never mine to run out of.

So this is not a sermon about trying harder to earn God's love. Get that all the way out of your head. You don't press on to become a son; you press on because you already are one. Identity first, effort second, always in that order. The running is the joy of a boy who already has his Father's name, not the audition of an orphan hoping to get it.

Lay the Weight Down and Look Up

Now, how do you actually run a race that long? Hebrews tells you, and it's a two-part order.

“Therefore, since we have so great a cloud of witnesses surrounding us — let us also lay aside every encumbrance and the sin which so easily entangles us, and let us run with endurance the race that is set before us, fixing our eyes on Jesus — the author and perfecter of faith — who, for the joy set before Him, endured the cross, despising the shame, and has sat down at the right hand of the throne of God.” (Hebrews 12:1-2)

Part one: lay aside every weight. Not just the sin, the weight. Some of what's slowing you down isn't even evil; it's just extra. The extra scroll, the extra hour, the grudge you're hauling, the good thing you turned into an ultimate thing. A runner strips down to nothing that doesn't serve the finish. Audit your pack, Son. Half of what's making the climb feel impossible is stuff you were never supposed to be carrying up it.

Part two: looking to Jesus. A runner who watches his own feet trips. You endure by fixing your eyes on the One at the finish who already ran this whole course, who is Himself the founder and the finisher of the faith you're running in. Your endurance has a face, and it isn't yours in the mirror. It's His at the tape.

The Difference Between Stubborn and Steadfast

Let me give you the framework I keep coming back to, from my REAL MAN list. The last letter is N: Never Quit. But I have to define it carefully, because never quit in a vacuum is just stubbornness, and stubbornness is not a fruit of the Spirit. Here's the line I want you to memorize: perseverance in the wrong direction is not a virtue; it's tragedy with momentum. Never quit means endurance in the right direction. The direction is Christ. Set the compass first, then throw away the brakes.

And Paul gives us the promise that keeps the legs moving on the days the finish is nowhere in sight:

“Let us not lose heart in doing good. For in due time we will reap, if we do not grow weary.” (Galatians 6:9)

In due season. Not today, maybe. Maybe not this year. The harvest is coming on God's clock, not yours, and the whole test is whether you'll keep sowing in a field that hasn't sprouted yet. Every man eventually faces the same fork, and I've watched it a hundred times mentoring men: you are either unsustainable or unstoppable. The man running on his own steam is unsustainable; he's just a countdown. The man running on grace, laying down the weight, eyes on Jesus, is unstoppable, not because he's strong, but because the One in him doesn't tire (compare Hebrews 12:2).

Go beyond. Toward Him. On His fuel.

That's the redeemed version of the battle cry, Son. The anime says go beyond and points at your own gut. Scripture says press on and points at a Person, hands you His strength, and tells you the finish is already secured by the One you're running toward. Same lean. Different engine. Different ending.

Stand your post. Lay down the weight. And run.

Semper Fi, and press on,
— Dad

A note to Gideon

Bud, here's the honest word. There will be a season, probably more than one, where doing the right thing stops feeling like anything at all. The prayers feel flat, the reps feel pointless, the field looks bare. That's not the signal to quit; that's the exact stretch of road Galatians 6:9 was written for. In due season is a promise with your name on it.

And when your arms are done, I don't want you digging deeper into yourself, because you'll hit the bottom of that well fast and it'll scare you. I want you to look up. He works in you to will and to do. You're not the engine, and thank God, because engines run out. You're a son leaning into a Father who doesn't. Now go beyond, straight at Him. I love you. Now go.

Father & Son Talk

Three questions for the couch

  1. Name one weight in your life right now that isn't a sin, just extra, that's slowing your climb. What would it take to set it down this week (Heb 12:1)?
  2. Be honest: when you "try hard" spiritually, are you running from being a son or because you already are one? How would today look different if you truly believed the strength was God's and not yours (Phil 2:13)?
  3. Where in your life might you be persevering hard in the wrong direction, stubborn instead of steadfast? What would it look like to reset the compass to Christ and then keep going (Gal 6:9)?
⚔ Today's Quest

Run one lap you don't feel like running

Pick one good thing you've been coasting on, prayer, the workout, the hard conversation, the chapter you keep skipping, and do it today past the point you'd normally stop. One rep beyond the limit. Before you start, say it out loud: "God, You work in me to will and to do; I'm leaning on Your strength, not mine." That sentence is the whole difference between the two battle cries. Go beyond, on His fuel.

What We Confess

The 1689 London Baptist Confession — Chapter 17, Of the Perseverance of the Saints

Those whom God has accepted in the Beloved and effectually called and sanctified by His Spirit can neither totally nor finally fall from the state of grace, but shall certainly persevere to the end and be eternally saved. And this perseverance rests not on their own free will, but on the unchangeable decree of election, the free love of the Father, the merit and intercession of Christ, and the abiding of His Spirit within them. This is why we press on and do not despair: the running is ours, but the keeping is His.

1689 LBCF, Ch. 17.1–17.2

Keach's (Baptist) Catechism — Question 36

Q. What benefits do believers receive from Christ in this life?

A. The benefits which in this life flow from justification, adoption, and sanctification are assurance of God's love, peace of conscience, joy in the Holy Spirit, increase of grace, and perseverance therein to the end.

Keach's Catechism, Q. 36
