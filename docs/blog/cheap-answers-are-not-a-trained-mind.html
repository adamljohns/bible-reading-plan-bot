Cheap Answers Are Not a Trained Mind

By Adam "MOOP" Johns · U.S.M.C. Ministries · October 20, 2026

Ruben Hassid put the fear into one picture in his August essay, “Thinking Is Now Cheap”. Cheap food made us heavier because the calories got easy and the walking stopped. Cheap answers may do the same to judgment. The fear is half right. The tool is not the loss. Stopping the practice is.

Eyeglasses do not make you blind. A calculator did not end mathematics. A map on a phone makes a trip safer. Those tools preserve the skill you still practice, or they replace a skill you no longer need to carry every day. The dangerous tools remove the repetition. You stop noticing the route, the error, the interesting line. Then the tool is gone, and you are worse than before you had it.

That is the test. Not whether the tool is convenient. Whether you still need the skill, and whether you are worse when the tool is taken away.

Watch the muscle the machine stops using

The studies Hassid cites are worth reading before anyone treats the percentages as settled. One math trial found students did better while a chat tool wrote with them, and worse than the control group once it was removed. The same tool, used as a tutor that made them try first, did not leave that hole. Doctors who had been using a detection aid later found fewer polyps without it. Pilots in automated cockpits kept basic flying and lost the tasks the machine usually did. None of that is a reason to smash the tool. It is a reason to notice which muscle stopped getting used.

The danger is not a cheap answer. It is a zero-effort pass. Output can be automated: a letter that only has to arrive, a list that only has to be sorted, a search that only has to find the page. Learning cannot. If the point is to know the thing, you draft, recall, and decide before the tool speaks. Then you let it challenge the draft. You do not let it send the thought you never had.

“He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame unto him.” — Proverbs 18:13, KJV

Make the person go first

A household that wants children who can speak in a room has to keep that order. They write the sentence. A tool may mark what is unclear. A tool does not turn in the paper. The student who cannot tell what was interesting in an interview until a machine says so has not lost a feature. He has skipped the practice that makes a judgment.

This is not a reprint of Hassid’s essay, and it is not his paid program. Read his full argument and follow his links to the studies.

The rule that travels is short enough to use on Monday. If the goal is a finished product, use the tool. If the goal is a person who can still think when the tool is down, make the person go first.

Use AI as a tutor, critic, and multiplier—not as the thought you never had.
