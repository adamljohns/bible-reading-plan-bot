Bear Much Fruit: What John 15 Says About the Only Kind of Productivity That Counts

By Adam “MOOP” Johns · U.S.M.C. Ministries
Listen
Narrated walkthrough of this study.

My grandfather kept grapes along the back fence, and the thing I remember as a boy is that he cut them back so hard every winter I was sure he had killed them. Bare knuckled stubs. Nothing that looked like life anywhere on the wire. Then every August there was more fruit than the family could eat. I asked him once why he cut so much off, and he said something I did not understand for another twenty years: the vine will spend everything it has on wood if you let it.

That is the picture Jesus reaches for on the last night of His life. Not a factory, not an army, not a marketplace. A vine. And He says something about it that ought to stop every capable man in his tracks.

I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing.John 15:5, KJV

Without me ye can do nothing. Not less. Not not as much. Nothing.

I have spent a good part of my life in institutions built on the opposite claim — that a trained man with a plan and enough will can produce results regardless of conditions. The Marine Corps made me competent. Competence is a real thing and I am not going to pretend otherwise. But competence has a ceiling, and the ceiling is exactly where Jesus draws the line: a branch can be strong, well-formed, beautifully pruned, correctly positioned, and if it is not connected, it produces nothing at all. It just takes longer to die than you would expect.

Two verses that fence in everything else

The chart at the top of this page has a banner in each upper corner, and putting those two verses at the corners was the right instinct, because between them sits the whole doctrine.

On the left, John 15:5: apart from Him, nothing. That is the floor. It is the verse that kills self-made religion, and it kills it in the mouth of Christ rather than in the mouth of a theologian, which matters when a man wants to argue.

On the right, John 15:8: “Herein is my Father glorified, that ye bear much fruit; so shall ye be my disciples.” That is the ceiling, and the ceiling is higher than most of us aim. The goal is not that we survive. Not that we hold the line. Not even that we bear some fruit. Much fruit — and the point of it is the Father's glory, not our résumé.

Between those two verses there is no room for either despair or pride. You cannot produce it. He intends much of it. Both are true at once, and holding both is most of the Christian life.

Abide is not a mood

“Abide in me, and I in you” (John 15:4). The word does a lot of work and gets softened constantly, usually into something that sounds like a feeling — a warm devotional hour, a sense of nearness. Real, sometimes. But abide means to remain, to stay put, to keep your position. It is closer to a duty station than to a mood.

The branch does not generate sap. It does not manufacture sugar out of ambition. Its entire job is to stay attached to the thing that does, and to not go looking for a better vine when the season turns dry. Men fail here in two directions. Some of us detach because we are discouraged — nothing seems to be growing, so we quietly stop staying. Others detach because we are succeeding — things are growing, we start believing we are the source, and the connection becomes a formality we maintain on Sundays.

Both are the same error wearing different clothes. The chart says it plainly in the center panel: we abide, He works. That is the division of labor, and it does not move.

The pruning nobody volunteers for

Here is where the passage stops being comfortable.

Every branch in me that beareth not fruit he taketh away: and every branch that beareth fruit, he purgeth it, that it may bring forth more fruit.John 15:2, KJV

Read that again slowly, because there are two knives in it and only one of them is judgment. The fruitless branch is taken away. But the fruitful branch gets cut too — and it gets cut precisely because it is fruitful. The Father is not punishing the healthy branch. He is removing what hinders it.

Which means the hardest season of your Christian life may arrive not when you are failing but when you are finally producing something. That is not a malfunction. My grandfather did not cut the dead vines hardest; he cut the ones that were going to carry weight.

I would not have told you that in my twenties. In my twenties, loss meant I had done something wrong. It took losing things I had built, and a few I had loved, to understand that a good Father with a sharp blade is not the same as an angry one. Hebrews puts it where a man can't dodge it: chastening seems grievous rather than joyous at the time, and afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness (Hebrews 12:11). Afterward. Not during. Don't grade the harvest in the middle of the pruning.

Fruit in us: what God grows on the inside

The left column of the chart lists eight, and the order is not random — it runs from character outward toward conduct.

1. Christlike character. Love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance (Galatians 5:22–23). Notice Paul calls it the fruit of the Spirit, singular, not the fruits — one cluster, grown by One Gardener, and you do not get to pick the three you like and skip self-control.

2. Holiness and righteousness. Set apart from sin and actually growing in godliness (Romans 6:22; Philippians 1:11). Positional holiness is settled the day you are His. Practical holiness is the slow business of a lifetime, and a man who claims the first while refusing the second is fooling himself.

3. Perseverance through trials. Tribulation works patience; patience, experience; experience, hope (Romans 5:3–5). This is fruit, not the absence of fruit. The man still standing after the thing that should have taken him out is carrying a crop most people never see.

4. Wisdom and discernment. The wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, easy to be intreated, full of mercy and good fruits (James 3:17–18). Read that list against the man who is merely clever and you will see why Scripture separates the two.

5. Godly words and speech. Words that build up, teach truth, and give grace to the hearers (Ephesians 4:29). Of everything on this list, this is the one most likely to expose whether the rest is real. A man's tongue is the honest gauge on the tank.

6. Worship and thanksgiving. The sacrifice of praise, continually, the fruit of our lips giving thanks to His name (Hebrews 13:15). Scripture calls praise itself fruit. Not the warm-up before the real work — the crop.

7. Love for God and others. The new commandment, and the badge by which men know whose we are (John 13:34–35).

8. Dependence and humility. A yielded heart and a teachable spirit; trust in the Lord with all thine heart, and lean not unto thine own understanding (Proverbs 3:5–6). This one sits at the bottom of the list and holds up everything above it. God resisteth the proud, and giveth grace unto the humble (James 4:6) — and grace is the sap.

Fruit through us: what God does with a connected man

The right column moves outward. Same vine, same sap, but now it is running through you into somebody else's life.

1. Obedience and good works. Fruitful in every good work, and increasing in the knowledge of God (Colossians 1:10). Faith without works is dead (James 2:14–26) — not because works save, but because a living thing acts like it.

2. Love for other believers. Bearing one another's burdens, pursuing unity, preferring one another (Romans 12:9–13). This is the hardest one for capable, self-sufficient men, and it is not optional.

3. Generosity and hospitality. Cheerful giving, meeting real needs, backing the work (2 Corinthians 9:6–12). Paul's phrase for a gift sent to him is striking: not that he desired the gift, but fruit that may abound to your account (Philippians 4:17). The giver is the one who grows.

4. Gospel witness. Lift up your eyes, and look on the fields; for they are white already to harvest (John 4:35–36). He that winneth souls is wise (Proverbs 11:30).

5. People brought to Christ. Eternal fruit — the only kind that walks off the field on its own two feet. Paul planted, Apollos watered, God gave the increase (1 Corinthians 3:6–7). Both men worked. Neither man produced.

6. Disciples made and equipped. Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you (Matthew 28:19–20). Note the verb — observe, not merely know. The Great Commission is not a conversion count; it is an obedience curriculum.

7. Multiplying disciples. The things thou hast heard of me among many witnesses, the same commit thou to faithful men, who shall be able to teach others also (2 Timothy 2:2). Four generations in one verse: Paul, Timothy, faithful men, others also. If your discipleship stops at the man in front of you, it stops.

8. Kingdom influence. Salt and light, in the actual sphere you occupy — work, unit, family, community, nation (Matthew 5:13–16). Daniel is the pattern: an excellent spirit was in him, and it showed up in his competence at work under a pagan government (Daniel 6:3).

The progression, and why the order will not bend

Along the bottom of the chart runs a chain, and it is the most useful thing on the page, because it puts the sixteen fruits in causal order:

StepWhat happensAnchor
1Abide in ChristJohn 15:4–5
2Walk by the SpiritGalatians 5:16
3God transforms our characterGalatians 5:22–23
4Transformed character produces obedienceJohn 14:15
5Obedience serves othersMark 10:45
6Witness produces disciplesJohn 4:35–36
7Disciples produce more disciples2 Timothy 2:2
8The Father is glorifiedJohn 15:8

Almost every discouraged Christian man I have sat with is trying to start somewhere in the middle of that chain.

He wants step 5 — service that means something — without step 3, so he burns out doing good things for people while his character quietly rots underneath. Or he wants step 7, a legacy, disciples who outlive him, and he has skipped step 1 so thoroughly that there is nothing in the pipe to hand down. You cannot enter this chain at the point that flatters you. It runs one direction, it starts at abiding, and every attempt to jump ahead produces activity that looks like fruit for about eighteen months.

The chain also explains the pruning. If step 3 is God transforming character, then the cutting is not an interruption of the process — it is step 3, felt from the inside.

Not my fruit

The banner across the bottom of the chart reads: Not my fruit. His fruit. His glory.

I want to be careful here, because there is a false humility that men use to get out of work. Saying “it's all God” can be a genuine confession or it can be a way to avoid being measured. Jesus does not let it be the second. In the same chapter He assumes the branch is working — bearing, growing, being pruned for more. Abiding is not passivity. Ask any farmer whether the branch has a job.

What abiding removes is not effort. It is ownership. The fruit hangs on your branch and has your address on it, and it was never yours. That is a hard trade for a man who has been taught since boot camp to take responsibility for outcomes — and it is the only trade that makes a lifetime of service survivable, because a man who owns the fruit will eventually be crushed by a season that produces none.

Then the last line, which on this chart sits under the silhouette of a man kneeling before a cross:

Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends.John 15:13, KJV

That verse is in the same conversation. Same night, same vine, same discourse. Which means the fruit Jesus is describing is not primarily a personality upgrade — it terminates in laid-down life. Marines understand that sentence in a way that costs something. Most of us have known men who did exactly that, and we do not say their names lightly. Christ says it first and says it about Himself, and then hands the pattern to us as the shape of a fruitful life.

Faithful Marines. Fruitful disciples. Glorious God. In that order, because the order is the whole point.

What to do with this in the next seven days

Nothing on this page is worth much if it stays a chart. So, plainly:

  • Find your entry point. Read the eight-step chain and name the step you are actually trying to start at. Be honest. Then go back to step 1 and stay there a while.
  • Name the pruning. Ask what the Father has removed in the last three years that you are still resentful about, and consider — genuinely consider — that it was cut off a branch He intends to load.
  • Pick the one fruit you are avoiding. Sixteen are listed. One of them made you uncomfortable when you read it. That is the one.
  • Find your one faithful man. 2 Timothy 2:2 requires a name, not a category. If you cannot write a name down, that is the assignment for this week.
