Bear Much Fruit

By Adam “MOOP” Johns · U.S.M.C. Ministries
Listen
Narrated walkthrough of this study.
This recording is of an earlier version of this post.

My grandfather kept grapes along the back fence, and what I remember about them as a boy is that he cut them back so hard every winter I was sure he had killed them. There would be nothing left on the wire but bare stubs, nothing that looked alive at all, and then every August there was more fruit than the family could eat. I asked him once why he cut so much off, and he said something I did not understand for another twenty years:

The vine will spend everything it has on wood if you let it.

That is the picture Jesus reaches for on the last night of His life. Of all the pictures He might have used — a factory, an army, a marketplace — He chose a vine, and then He said something that ought to stop every capable man in his tracks:

“I am the vine, you are the branches. He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit; for without Me you can do nothing.” John 15:5, NKJV

Without Me you can do nothing. He did not say less, or almost as much, but nothing, and I have spent a good part of my life in institutions built on the opposite claim, which is that a trained man with a plan and enough will can produce results no matter the conditions. The Marine Corps made me competent, and competence is real; I will not pretend otherwise. But competence has a ceiling, and Jesus draws the line right there. A branch can be strong, well-formed and correctly positioned, and if it is not connected it produces nothing at all — it just takes longer to die than you would expect.

Two verses that fence the whole field

The chart puts a banner in each upper corner because the whole doctrine sits between those two verses. On the left is John 15:5, and that is the floor: apart from Him, nothing. It kills self-made religion, and it matters, on the day a man wants to argue, that the sentence comes from the mouth of Christ and not of some theologian. On the right is John 15:8 (NKJV): “By this My Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit; so you will be My disciples.” That is the ceiling, and it is higher than most of us aim. Jesus is not asking for survival, or for a man who holds the line, or even for some fruit. He says much fruit, and the point of it is the Father’s glory, which is a long way from your résumé.

Between those two verses there is no room for despair or pride, because you cannot produce the fruit and He intends a great deal of it, and both of those are true at once. Learning to hold them together is most of the Christian life.

Abide is not a mood

“Abide in Me, and I in you” (John 15:4, NKJV). We soften that word all the time into a feeling, a warm quiet time or a sense of nearness, and sometimes the feeling is real. But abide means remain: stay put and keep your position, which makes it a good deal closer to a duty station than to a mood. The branch does not generate sap, and it does not manufacture sugar out of ambition. Its job is to stay attached to the One who does, and not to go shopping for a better vine when the season turns dry.

Men fail here in two directions. Some detach because they are discouraged; nothing seems to be growing, so they stop staying. Others detach because they are succeeding; things are growing, they begin to believe they are the source, and the connection shrinks to a Sunday formality. It is the same error in a different uniform, and the center of the chart answers both of them in four words — we abide, He works — a division of labor that does not move.

The pruning nobody volunteers for

Here the passage stops being comfortable.

“Every branch in Me that does not bear fruit He takes away; and every branch that bears fruit He prunes, that it may bear more fruit.” John 15:2, NKJV

Read it slowly, because there are two knives in that verse and only one of them is judgment. The fruitless branch is taken away, but the fruitful branch gets cut too, and it gets cut because it is fruitful; the Father is removing what hinders the healthy branch, and there is no punishment in it. That means the hardest season of your Christian life may arrive just when you are finally producing something, and when it does, nothing has malfunctioned. My grandfather saved his hardest cuts for the vines that were going to carry weight, not for the dead wood.

I would not have told you that in my twenties, when loss meant I had done something wrong. It took losing things I had built, and a few I had loved, to learn that a good Father with a sharp blade is not the same as an angry one. Hebrews will not let a man dodge it, either. Chastening seems grievous at the time, but “afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness” (Hebrews 12:11, NKJV). The word to hold onto is afterward, which means you should not try to grade the harvest in the middle of the pruning.

Fruit in us — what God grows on the inside

The left column of the chart lists eight kinds of fruit, and the order is not random; it runs from character outward towards conduct. At the top is Christlike character, which is Paul’s list in Galatians 5:22–23 (NKJV): “love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control.” Paul calls that the fruit of the Spirit, singular, so it is one cluster from one Gardener and not a buffet, and you do not get to keep the joy and pass on the self-control. Right under it come holiness and righteousness, being set apart from sin and really growing in godliness (Romans 6:22; Philippians 1:11). Positional holiness is settled the day you are His and practical holiness is the slow work of a lifetime, and a man who claims the first while refusing the second is only fooling himself.

Paul also says that tribulation produces perseverance; perseverance, character; character, hope (Romans 5:3–5), and this perseverance is fruit, not what a man has instead of fruit; the man still standing after the thing that should have taken him out is carrying a crop most people never see. Wisdom and discernment are here as well, the wisdom from above that is “first pure, then peaceable, gentle, willing to yield, full of mercy and good fruits” (James 3:17–18, NKJV). Set that list beside a merely clever man and you will see why Scripture separates the two.

Godly words are on the list too, words that build up, tell the truth, and give grace to the hearers (Ephesians 4:29), and of everything here the tongue is the gauge on the tank; it will tell you whether the rest is real. So is worship and thanksgiving, the sacrifice of praise — the fruit of our lips giving thanks to His name (Hebrews 13:15, NKJV), where Scripture calls the praise itself fruit, which makes it part of the crop and no mere warm-up.

Love for God and for others is the new commandment, and the badge by which men know whose we are (John 13:34–35). Last come dependence and humility, a yielded heart and a teachable spirit: “Trust in the Lord with all your heart, and lean not on your own understanding” (Proverbs 3:5–6, NKJV). That one sits at the bottom of the list and holds up everything above it, because God resists the proud and gives grace to the humble (James 4:6), and grace is the sap.

Fruit through us — what God does with a connected man

The right column moves outward. It is the same vine and the same sap, only now it runs through you into somebody else’s life. It starts with obedience and good works: fruitful in every good work, increasing in the knowledge of God (Colossians 1:10). James says “faith without works is dead” (James 2:14–26, NKJV), and he says it because a living thing acts like it is alive, not because works save anybody. Next comes love for other believers: bearing one another’s burdens, pursuing unity, preferring one another (Romans 12:9–13). For capable, self-sufficient men that may be the hardest line on the page, and it is still not optional. Then generosity and hospitality, which means cheerful giving, real needs met, and the work backed (2 Corinthians 9:6–12). Paul said he did not seek the gift so much as fruit that would abound to your account (Philippians 4:17), so the giver is the one who grows.

Gospel witness comes next: lift up your eyes — the fields are white for harvest (John 4:35–36), and “he who wins souls is wise” (Proverbs 11:30, NKJV). What witness is after is people brought to Christ, which is eternal fruit, and the only kind that walks off the field on its own two feet. Paul planted, Apollos watered, and God gave the increase (1 Corinthians 3:6–7). Both men worked, and neither man produced.

Then disciples have to be made and equipped, teaching them to observe all things Christ commanded (Matthew 28:19–20), and the word is observe, which asks more of a man than knowing, so that the Great Commission reads less like a conversion count than like an obedience curriculum. Those disciples are meant to multiply. Paul’s order to Timothy was this: what you heard, commit to faithful men who will teach others also (2 Timothy 2:2), which puts four generations in one verse — Paul, Timothy, faithful men, and others — and if your discipleship stops at the man in front of you, it stops. The column ends with kingdom influence, being salt and light in the actual sphere you occupy, whether that is your work, your unit, your family, your community or your nation (Matthew 5:13–16). Daniel is the pattern, a man with an excellent spirit in him (Daniel 6:3), and in his case it showed up as competence under a pagan government.

The progression, and why you cannot enter in the middle

Along the bottom of the chart runs a chain, and it may be the most useful thing on the page, because it puts the fruit in causal order. A man abides in Christ (John 15:4–5), and abiding, he walks by the Spirit (Galatians 5:16). God transforms his character (Galatians 5:22–23), transformed character produces obedience (John 14:15), and obedience serves others (Mark 10:45). Witness then produces disciples (John 4:35–36), disciples produce more disciples (2 Timothy 2:2), and at the end of it the Father is glorified (John 15:8). That makes eight links, and they run in one direction.

Almost every discouraged Christian man I have sat with is trying to start somewhere in the middle of that chain. He wants the fifth link, service that means something, without the third, and so he burns out doing good things while his character rots underneath. Or he wants the seventh, a legacy, disciples who outlive him, and he has skipped the first so thoroughly that there is nothing in the pipe to hand down. You cannot enter this chain at the point that flatters you. It starts at abiding, and every attempt to jump ahead produces activity that looks like fruit for about a season and a half. The chain also explains the pruning, because if the third link is God transforming character, then the cutting does not interrupt the process at all; it is the third link, felt from the inside.

Not my fruit

The banner across the bottom of the chart reads:

Not my fruit. His fruit. His glory.

I want to be careful here, because there is a false humility men use to get out of work. “It’s all God” can be a genuine confession, and it can also be a way to avoid being measured, and Jesus does not allow the second. In the same chapter He assumes the branch is working — bearing, growing, being pruned for more — so abiding cannot mean passivity; ask any farmer whether the branch has a job. What abiding takes away from a man is ownership, and it leaves the effort right where it was. The fruit hangs on your branch, and it may even have your address on it, but it does not belong to you. That is a hard trade for a man taught since boot camp to own outcomes. It is also the only trade that makes a lifetime of service survivable, because a man who owns the fruit will eventually be crushed by a season that produces none.

Then there is the last line on the chart, under the silhouette of a man kneeling before a cross:

“Greater love has no one than this, than to lay down one’s life for his friends.” John 15:13, NKJV

That is the same conversation on the same night, about the same vine, and the fruit Jesus has been describing turns out to be no better personality but a laid-down life. Marines understand that sentence in a way that costs something, because most of us have known men who did exactly that, and we do not say their names lightly. Christ says it first, about Himself, and then hands us the pattern for a fruitful life. The chart closes with three phrases — faithful Marines, fruitful disciples, glorious God — and they come in that order because the order is the point.

What to do with this in the next seven days

None of this is worth much if it stays a chart, so let me be plain about the week ahead. Start by finding your entry point: read the eight links again, name the one you are really trying to start at, be honest about it, and then go back to the first and stay there a while. Then name the pruning. Ask what the Father has removed in the last three years that you are still resentful about, and consider (genuinely consider) that it was cut off a branch He intends to load. After that, pick the one fruit you are avoiding; sixteen are listed, one of them made you uncomfortable when you read it, and that is the one. And find your one faithful man. 2 Timothy 2:2 requires a name and not a category, and if you cannot write a name down, then finding one is the assignment for this week.
