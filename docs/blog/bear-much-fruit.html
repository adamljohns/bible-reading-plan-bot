Bear Much Fruit

By Adam “MOOP” Johns · U.S.M.C. Ministries
Listen
Narrated walkthrough of this study (polished Hartwell cut, Prof X voice).

My grandfather kept grapes along the back fence. What I remember as a boy is that he cut them back so hard every winter I was sure he had killed them. Bare stubs on the wire. Nothing that looked alive. Then every August there was more fruit than the family could eat.

I asked him once why he cut so much off. He said something I did not understand for another twenty years:

The vine will spend everything it has on wood if you let it.

That is the picture Jesus reaches for on the last night of His life. Not a factory. Not an army. Not a marketplace. A vine. And He says something that ought to stop every capable man in his tracks:

“I am the vine, you are the branches. He who abides in Me, and I in him, bears much fruit; for without Me you can do nothing.” John 15:5, NKJV

Without Me you can do nothing. Not less. Not almost as much. Nothing.

I have spent a good part of my life in institutions built on the opposite claim — that a trained man with a plan and enough will can produce results no matter the conditions. The Marine Corps made me competent. Competence is real, and I will not pretend otherwise. But competence has a ceiling, and Jesus draws the line right there. A branch can be strong, well-formed, correctly positioned — and if it is not connected, it produces nothing at all. It just takes longer to die than you expect.

Two verses that fence the whole field

The chart puts a banner in each upper corner on purpose. Between those two verses sits the whole doctrine.

Left — John 15:5. Apart from Him, nothing. That is the floor. It kills self-made religion, and it kills it in the mouth of Christ, not a theologian. That matters when a man wants to argue.

Right — John 15:8. “By this My Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit; so you will be My disciples.” That is the ceiling, and it is higher than most of us aim. The goal is not survival. Not holding the line. Not even some fruit. Much fruit — and the point is the Father’s glory, not your résumé.

Between those two verses there is no room for despair or pride. You cannot produce it. He intends much of it. Both true at once. Holding both is most of the Christian life.

Abide is not a mood

“Abide in Me, and I in you” (John 15:4). We soften that word constantly into a feeling — a warm quiet time, a sense of nearness. Real, sometimes. But abide means remain. Stay put. Keep your position. It is closer to a duty station than a mood.

The branch does not generate sap. It does not manufacture sugar out of ambition. Its job is to stay attached to the One who does — and not go shopping for a better vine when the season turns dry.

Men fail here in two directions. Some detach because they are discouraged — nothing seems to be growing, so they quietly stop staying. Others detach because they are succeeding — things are growing, they start believing they are the source, and the connection becomes a Sunday formality.

Same error. Different uniform. The center of the chart says it plain: we abide, He works. That division of labor does not move.

The pruning nobody volunteers for

Here the passage stops being comfortable.

“Every branch in Me that does not bear fruit He takes away; and every branch that bears fruit He prunes, that it may bear more fruit.” John 15:2, NKJV

Read it slowly. There are two knives in that verse, and only one of them is judgment. The fruitless branch is taken away. But the fruitful branch gets cut too — and it gets cut because it is fruitful. The Father is not punishing the healthy branch. He is removing what hinders it.

Which means the hardest season of your Christian life may arrive not when you are failing, but when you are finally producing something. That is not a malfunction. My grandfather did not cut the dead wood hardest. He cut the vines that were going to carry weight.

I would not have told you that in my twenties. In my twenties, loss meant I had done something wrong. It took losing things I had built — and a few I had loved — to learn that a good Father with a sharp blade is not the same as an angry one.

Hebrews will not let a man dodge it: chastening seems grievous at the time, and afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness (Hebrews 12:11). Afterward. Not during. Do not grade the harvest in the middle of the pruning.

Fruit in us — what God grows on the inside

The left column of the chart lists eight. The order is not random. It runs from character outward toward conduct.

1. Christlike character. Love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control (Galatians 5:22–23). Paul calls it the fruit of the Spirit — singular — not a buffet. One cluster. One Gardener. You do not get to keep joy and skip self-control.

2. Holiness and righteousness. Set apart from sin and actually growing in godliness (Romans 6:22; Philippians 1:11). Positional holiness is settled the day you are His. Practical holiness is the slow work of a lifetime. Claim the first while refusing the second and you are only fooling yourself.

3. Perseverance through trials. Tribulation produces perseverance; perseverance, character; character, hope (Romans 5:3–5). This is fruit — not the absence of fruit. The man still standing after the thing that should have taken him out is carrying a crop most people never see.

4. Wisdom and discernment. The wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, willing to yield, full of mercy and good fruits (James 3:17–18). Set that list next to a merely clever man and you will see why Scripture separates the two.

5. Godly words. Words that build up, tell the truth, and give grace to the hearers (Ephesians 4:29). Of everything on this list, the tongue is the gauge on the tank. It will tell you whether the rest is real.

6. Worship and thanksgiving. The sacrifice of praise — the fruit of our lips giving thanks to His name (Hebrews 13:15). Scripture calls praise itself fruit. Not the warm-up. The crop.

7. Love for God and others. The new commandment — and the badge by which men know whose we are (John 13:34–35).

8. Dependence and humility. A yielded heart. A teachable spirit. Trust in the Lord with all your heart, and lean not on your own understanding (Proverbs 3:5–6). This one sits at the bottom of the list and holds up everything above it. God resists the proud and gives grace to the humble (James 4:6) — and grace is the sap.

Fruit through us — what God does with a connected man

The right column moves outward. Same vine. Same sap. Now it runs through you into somebody else’s life.

1. Obedience and good works. Fruitful in every good work, increasing in the knowledge of God (Colossians 1:10). Faith without works is dead (James 2:14–26) — not because works save, but because a living thing acts like it is alive.

2. Love for other believers. Bear one another’s burdens. Pursue unity. Prefer one another (Romans 12:9–13). Hardest line on the page for capable, self-sufficient men. Still not optional.

3. Generosity and hospitality. Cheerful giving. Real needs met. The work backed (2 Corinthians 9:6–12). Paul said he did not seek the gift so much as fruit that would abound to your account (Philippians 4:17). The giver is the one who grows.

4. Gospel witness. Lift up your eyes — the fields are white for harvest (John 4:35–36). He who wins souls is wise (Proverbs 11:30).

5. People brought to Christ. Eternal fruit — the only kind that walks off the field on its own two feet. Paul planted. Apollos watered. God gave the increase (1 Corinthians 3:6–7). Both men worked. Neither man produced.

6. Disciples made and equipped. Teaching them to observe all things Christ commanded (Matthew 28:19–20). Observe — not merely know. The Great Commission is not a conversion count. It is an obedience curriculum.

7. Multiplying disciples. What you heard, commit to faithful men who will teach others also (2 Timothy 2:2). Four generations in one verse: Paul → Timothy → faithful men → others. If your discipleship stops at the man in front of you, it stops.

8. Kingdom influence. Salt and light in the actual sphere you occupy — work, unit, family, community, nation (Matthew 5:13–16). Daniel is the pattern: an excellent spirit in him, and it showed up as competence under a pagan government (Daniel 6:3).

The progression — and why you cannot enter in the middle

Along the bottom of the chart runs a chain. This may be the most useful thing on the page, because it puts the fruit in causal order:

StepWhat happensAnchor
1Abide in ChristJohn 15:4–5
2Walk by the SpiritGalatians 5:16
3God transforms characterGalatians 5:22–23
4Transformed character produces obedienceJohn 14:15
5Obedience serves othersMark 10:45
6Witness produces disciplesJohn 4:35–36
7Disciples produce more disciples2 Timothy 2:2
8The Father is glorifiedJohn 15:8

Almost every discouraged Christian man I have sat with is trying to start somewhere in the middle of that chain.

He wants step 5 — service that means something — without step 3, so he burns out doing good things while his character quietly rots underneath. Or he wants step 7, a legacy, disciples who outlive him, and he has skipped step 1 so thoroughly there is nothing in the pipe to hand down.

You cannot enter this chain at the point that flatters you. It runs one direction. It starts at abiding. Every attempt to jump ahead produces activity that looks like fruit for about a season and a half.

The chain also explains the pruning. If step 3 is God transforming character, then the cutting is not an interruption of the process. It is step 3, felt from the inside.

Not my fruit

The banner across the bottom of the chart reads:

Not my fruit. His fruit. His glory.

I want to be careful here. There is a false humility men use to get out of work. Saying “it’s all God” can be a genuine confession — or a way to avoid being measured. Jesus does not allow the second. In the same chapter He assumes the branch is working — bearing, growing, being pruned for more. Abiding is not passivity. Ask any farmer whether the branch has a job.

What abiding removes is not effort. It is ownership. The fruit hangs on your branch. It may even have your address on it. It was never yours.

That is a hard trade for a man taught since boot camp to own outcomes. It is also the only trade that makes a lifetime of service survivable — because a man who owns the fruit will eventually be crushed by a season that produces none.

Then the last line on the chart, under the silhouette of a man kneeling before a cross:

“Greater love has no one than this, than to lay down one’s life for his friends.” John 15:13, NKJV

Same conversation. Same night. Same vine. Same discourse. The fruit Jesus is describing is not a better personality. It terminates in a laid-down life.

Marines understand that sentence in a way that costs something. Most of us have known men who did exactly that, and we do not say their names lightly. Christ says it first, about Himself — then hands us the pattern as the shape of a fruitful life.

Faithful Marines. Fruitful disciples. Glorious God.
In that order — because the order is the whole point.

What to do with this in the next seven days

Nothing on this page is worth much if it stays a chart. So, plainly:

  • Find your entry point. Read the eight-step chain. Name the step you are actually trying to start at. Be honest. Then go back to step 1 and stay there a while.
  • Name the pruning. What has the Father removed in the last three years that you are still resentful about? Consider — genuinely consider — that it was cut off a branch He intends to load.
  • Pick the one fruit you are avoiding. Sixteen are listed. One of them made you uncomfortable when you read it. That is the one.
  • Find your one faithful man. 2 Timothy 2:2 requires a name, not a category. If you cannot write a name down, that is the assignment for this week.

Peace.
