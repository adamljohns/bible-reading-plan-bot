Who Holds the Scorecard? The Pentagon, Spiritual Fitness, and the 7 F’s

The Pentagon wants tools to assess spiritual fitness across the force. I built one for myself a couple of years ago. What matters is who holds the scorecard.

By Adam “MOOP” Johns · U.S.M.C. Ministries

On September 30, the Secretary of War signed a two-page memo standing up a new Office of Religious Affairs inside the Pentagon. Its Director reports straight to the Secretary “without intervening authority,” and the Armed Forces Chaplains Board moves under him. You can read the whole thing here.

I read it twice. Then I went digging through my old files for something I built a couple of years ago, because one line in that memo hit close to home.

Credit where it’s due

When Secretary Hegseth announced the office at Quantico, he said, “The people who actually practice faith and religion, our chaplains, were buried echelons deep, waiting for permission” (Military Times). He’s not wrong. An advocate at the top table, with a budget line and a deadline, is a good thing for chaplains, and I’m not going to pretend otherwise.

The memo also says this, and I’m glad it does:

“Nothing in this memorandum shall be construed to compel religious participation or to diminish the free exercise rights of any Service member, civilian employee, or family member of any faith or no faith.”

The line that stopped me

Among the Director’s core responsibilities, the memo lists this one: “Sponsor and oversee research, technology, and analytical tools to support and assess spiritual fitness, spiritual readiness, and spiritual resilience across the Total Force.”

So somebody plans to grade spiritual fitness across the whole force. If you’ve worn the uniform, you know how that goes. Once something gets measured, it gets graded, and sooner or later it shows up somewhere around a fitness report. Nobody has to order anyone to do anything. The day a lance corporal’s prayer life turns into a readiness metric, the believer starts performing for the grader, and the Marine who’s struggling learns to keep his mouth shut.

I built one of these myself

I’m not against measuring spiritual strength. I built a test for it.

A couple of years back I put together what I called MOOP’s OSSSA, the Objective Spiritual Strength Self-Assessment (yes, that’s a lot of S’s… I’m a Marine, we like our acronyms). Seven categories, the 7 F’s: Family, Faithfulness to the Word, Fellowship, Fruit (with gifts and armor), Fulfilling the create-and-cultivate mandate, Finances, and Focus on purity. Three hundred points. It asks what you did, not how you felt: did you take your wife on a date, did you lead your kids in prayer, did you actually give the tithe. Think of it as a PFT for the inner man.

When I pulled it back out this week, I found mistakes I’d be embarrassed to hand to any seminary professor.

I scored salvation. “Prayed and received salvation” was worth ten points out of three hundred. That means a man who has never been born again could score 290 and feel pretty good about himself. Salvation doesn’t belong on a points sheet. It comes before the sheet. Paul puts it plainly: “Examine yourselves as to whether you are in the faith. Test yourselves.” (2 Corinthians 13:5, NKJV) That’s the first question, and you don’t score it.

I scored results in evangelism. You got points if the man you shared with prayed to receive Christ. But “I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase.” (1 Corinthians 3:6, NKJV) My job is to be faithful and share. Whether anything grows is God’s business.

I rewarded forgetting. One line gave you points if you couldn’t remember the last time you fell into sin. John says, “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us.” (1 John 1:8, NKJV) If a man can’t remember his last sin, he probably isn’t looking very hard.

On top of that there were plain old scoring errors: one line that actually penalized a man for a year of discipleship, timeframes that didn’t match between the question sheet and the grading sheet, and categories whose points quietly added up past their cap.

What’s different in version 2

  • Salvation is the gate, asked first and not scored: have you repented of your sin and put your trust in Christ alone? If a man isn’t sure, he stops and talks to his pastor or a brother he trusts before he goes any further.
  • Evangelism scores whether you shared and leaves the results with God.
  • Honest, recent confession to God and to a brother replaces “can’t remember.”
  • Every category now adds up exactly to its cap.
  • Daily time in the Word is on the sheet. The original Word section never even asked, which is a little embarrassing for a guy with an M.Div.
  • N/A lines come off the possible points, so a single man or a man with no kids at home can still score himself fairly, as a percentage.
  • Don’t trust your total, because it’ll make you feel better than you should. Your lowest category is what you work on for the next 30 days.

And one thing I should have said the first time around: none of these points earn anything with God. A man stands on Christ’s record, not his own. The sheet is for seeing where you’re slipping, not for proving you’re good enough.

A mirror for a man and his brother

Here’s the real difference between my test and whatever the Pentagon builds: who holds the scorecard.

Scripture commands self-examination. “Let each one examine his own work.” (Galatians 6:4, NKJV) “Let us search out and examine our ways, and turn back to the LORD.” (Lamentations 3:40, NKJV) That’s a man holding up a mirror, maybe with one brother he trusts looking over his shoulder. It’s a whole different thing when an institution collects the scores.

Back in September 2018 on Okinawa, I was part of a suicide-awareness event with Phil Downer, put on in coordination with the III MEF Religious Ministry Teams. The feedback form had boxes a Marine could check if he wanted somebody to contact him about being mentored or discipled. Opt-in. His name, his choice. That’s how it ought to work.

Build every office as if the worst man who’ll ever run it is already in charge. This Secretary won’t be there forever, but the office will. A tool built to measure faith can be turned against the faithful by whoever comes next.

What I’ll be watching

The memo gives the Pentagon 90 days to draft the directive that formally charters this office. When it comes out, I’ll be asking three questions:

  1. Does it put limits on the Director’s power to decide his own authorities?
  2. Do the endorsing churches, not the Pentagon, stay the gatekeepers of who serves as a chaplain?
  3. Does any spiritual-fitness assessment stay anonymous, aggregate, and out of evaluations?

If the answers are yes, it’s a good day for chaplains, and I’ll say so. If not, we’ve handed a very sharp sword to whoever holds the office next.

Take the test

Download MOOP’s OSSSA v2 (PDF). Take it this week. Don’t turn it in to anybody. Show it to one brother you trust, then circle your lowest F and work on it for the next 30 days.

Peace.
