Denominationalism Is Dead — And Men Need to Hear It

By Adam "MOOP" Johns  ·  U.S.M.C. Ministries  ·  Published July 22, 2026  ·  Why brand-loyalty Christianity can’t disciple men in this hour

A brother dropped Joel Webbon’s episode “Denominationalism Is Dead” into The Real Ones the other night — an online discussion group for Christian men. The responses weren’t mild.

“Pull this from the archives.”

“Men need to hear this.”

“I wish people would wake up and know the times.”

“Critical.”

Then someone said what needed saying: write it down.

So here it is — a men’s reading of the broadcast: the steel under the title, and the parts men need to actually do something with.

Source episode: Joel Webbon & colleagues, NXR / Theology Applied — Denominationalism Is Dead (~Nov 14, 2025). Original X post.

The title is not a stunt

Webbon isn’t saying the Church is dead, and he isn’t saying doctrine doesn’t matter; he isn’t saying “just love Jesus and skip the hard stuff.” He’s saying denominationalism — the brand-loyalty version of Christianity where the logo does the work the Lordship of Christ is supposed to do — is finished as a reliable map.

That distinction matters.

A denomination, at its best, is a confessional family: shared doctrine, shared discipline, shared worship, shared accountability. Denominationalism is what happens when the family name becomes a costume. Men wear the jersey, quote the heroes, fight the rival tribes online… and never submit to a real local church, a real elder board, or a real standard of holiness.

One is a house; the other is a hat. This episode is about the hat era ending — whether the hat-wearers admit it or not.

Survival over particularities

The core premise of the broadcast is blunt: in a culture still soaked with residual Christianity, churches could afford to divide endlessly over secondary convictions — Baptist vs. Presbyterian vs. Anglican as the main identity game. That luxury is gone.

When orthodox Christianity is on the defensive, survival and courage outrank niche particularities. Not because secondary doctrines become false, but because a man who can’t find a courageous church within driving distance while he waits for a perfect clone of his favorite confessional niche is not “faithful.” He’s stranded.

The 50-mile radius rule

Rather than hunting for a church that matches every nuanced doctrinal checkbox, the hosts urge men to find any church within about 50 miles that shows courage and discernment. That is not theological laziness; that is wartime triage.

Protection and solidarity

A primary criterion for church selection: Will the leadership protect its people? Will they stand when cancel culture, political pressure, or soft institutional cowardice comes for members who refuse to bow — or will they throw the sheep under the bus to keep the brand clean?

If your elders will not protect the flock, your logo is a costume.

Theological triage: stop dying on Tier-3 hills

The hosts use theological triage:

TierWhat it isAction
Primary (Tier 1) Core of the faith — Trinity, salvation, the doctrine of God Non-negotiable. Denial is heresy. Hold the line.
Secondary & tertiary Mode of baptism, specific eschatology, denominational polity details Can be temporarily set lower when the urgent need is a courageous local body

Historically, men argued hard about lower-tier issues because the primary alignments were assumed across Protestant churches. That assumption no longer holds.

Today a church can fly a historic banner and deny plain Scripture on creation, sexuality, sin, repentance, headship, and the exclusivity of Christ. Another church with no famous brand can preach Christ crucified, practice real discipline, and form men who actually change.

The label is no longer the guarantee; the fruit is.

If that sentence offends you more than a soft church does, this episode is for you.

Monday–Saturday unity, Sunday division

Monday through Saturday

In culture and politics, the hosts argue for linking arms with conservative Catholics and Eastern Orthodox where the common enemies are secularism, globalism, and the dismantling of the created order. That is not “ecumenism as mush”; that is coalition against a shared assault.

Sunday

On the Lord’s Day, the distinctions still matter — and they hit Tier 1 categories. Joint worship is not the answer just because you fought the same cultural battle on Friday.

Hold both truths at once: allies in the public square where conscience allows, and clarity in worship and doctrine where the gospel and the nature of the Church are at stake. Soft men collapse one into the other — either they refuse all cooperation and lose every civic fight, or they blur Sunday until doctrine means nothing.

Why young men run to Orthodoxy

The hosts note the surge of young men into Eastern Orthodoxy and name a wound underneath it: a fatherless West. When Western churches feel compromised, rootless, or effeminate, young men go looking for something with spine, history, hierarchy, and a fatherly shape — the “Mr. Miyagi complex.”

Don’t sneer at that hunger; diagnose it. Men will not stay in churches that offer branding without fatherhood, content without courage, and community without standards.

Baptism, the “Baptist spirit,” and American individualism

Webbon — pastoring in a Baptist context — speaks with unusual honesty about his own tradition. He expresses sympathy for paedobaptist / covenantal arguments, and even suggests that if Protestants “win” toward a more Christian social order, it may land more naturally in a paedobaptist frame. You don’t have to agree with that forecast to hear the self-critique underneath it.

More important for men in the room is his critique of the “Baptist spirit” as it fused with the worst of the American spirit — extreme individualism, reflex rebellion, anti-authority posture, and “just me and my Bible” as a lifestyle, not a confession. That spirit can plant churches and still fail to form sons.

I’m a Southern Baptist man walking toward ordination with eyes open. I love the best of that heritage: the Book, the local church, the missionary impulse, the conviction that Christ saves sinners. I also know this: no convention badge disciples your heart.

If your church is soft on sin, confused on manhood and womanhood, allergic to discipline, and addicted to attendance metrics, the letters on the sign will not save your sons. If your church preaches the whole counsel of God, practices meaningful membership, and calls men to repent and lead — stay and build, whether the brand is famous or not.

COVID as a courage litmus test

The hosts treat a church’s COVID response as a courage metric. Webbon even names his own failure — closing for a stretch — and then draws the sharper line: the true test is not only whether a church never closed; it is whether leadership repented publicly if they folded under pressure and later knew it was wrong.

Courage is not perfection; courage is truth-telling after the fear passes.

Men should ask the same of themselves: not “Did I never stumble?” but “Did I repent in the open, or did I rebrand my cowardice as wisdom?”

Babel, households, and what fathers actually do

The broadcast ties cultural fragmentation to Babel: forced fusion under a godless tower always breaks. Globalism that pretends gravity doesn’t exist will learn gravity the hard way.

Then it gets practical for households:

  • Speak to your wife gently — not with the same edge you use with “the bros.” Hardline truth without hardness of heart. Root her in a solid local church and good friendships; women often take their cues from the immediate community around them.
  • Family worship now — even before a child understands the words. Start the rhythm early. The standard in the home is set by what you repeat, not what you intend.

That is not podcast Christianity; that is household Christianity.

What died — and what must not

What died: blind trust in the logo; “we’re safe because our tribe is the good tribe”; treating secondary distinctives like the gospel itself; using denominational identity as a substitute for personal holiness and local accountability; the fantasy that the machine will disciple your sons for you.

What must not die: confessional clarity on Tier-1 truth; biblical authority; local church membership that means something; elders who can say “no” and protect the flock; men who repent in the open instead of hiding behind theology talk; the courage to leave a compromised house and find a faithful one within reach.

Dead denominationalism does not equal churchless Christianity; that would just be a lonelier idol. The answer to a dead brand is not isolation. The answer is a living church under the living Christ.

Why men especially need this

Men love systems; men also hide in systems. Some hide in tradition — “my people have always been X.” Some hide in anti-tradition — “I don’t do organized religion.” Some hide in content — podcasts, clips, debate bros, Substack theology — while their marriage, lust life, and household remain untouched.

Denominationalism gave a lot of men a place to park their identity without parking their sin. If the brand no longer protects you, you’re left with the only questions that ever mattered:

  1. Who is Jesus, and do I obey Him?
  2. Where is my local church, and do I submit there?
  3. What do the men closest to me actually know about my life?
  4. Is my house ordered under Christ — or just decorated with Christian language?

You can switch logos every year and never answer those.

What to do after you listen

Don’t just nod at the clip and move on.

  1. Watch the episode. youtube.com/watch?v=PBDrMAYKK2w
  2. Run triage. Name your Tier-1 non-negotiables. Stop treating Tier-3 hills like Calvary.
  3. Apply the 50-mile test. Is there a courageous church within reach — and are you in it?
  4. Ask the protection question. Will your elders guard the flock when pressure comes?
  5. Name your church. Not the network. Not the podcast. The actual local body where you are known.
  6. Find two men. Not fans — brothers who can ask about lust, leadership, money, and anger without you performing.
  7. Lead your house this week. Family worship. Gentle strength with your wife. One hard conversation you’ve delayed.
  8. Stop outsourcing courage. Algorithms will not raise your sons; elders and fathers will.

The Real Ones already feel it

That chat title wasn’t an accident. The Real Ones is an online discussion group for Christian men — and the men in it aren’t looking for a cooler jersey. They’re looking for truth with a spine.

Denominationalism-as-brand is dead because it cannot carry the weight of this cultural moment. Soft men in hard times always reach for a logo when what they need is a Lord.

Christ is not a denomination; He is King.

Find His people. Submit to His Word. Kill your sin. Build something that will still stand when the brand names change again.

If this stirred you, send it to one man who is still sleeping through the times — then listen to the episode together and talk like brothers, not commentators.
