What the Care and Counsel Bible Gets Wrong About Mutual Submission

By Adam "MOOP" Johns · U.S.M.C. Ministries · June 28, 2026

The SOUL NOTE

I was sitting at a coffee shop this morning reading through Ephesians in the Care and Counsel Bible, the NKJV study Bible edited by Tim Clinton, Edward Hindson, and Jared Pingleton through the American Association of Christian Counselors. It is a serious resource and one I keep close, because counseling and discipleship overlap more than most pastors admit. On the spread covering Ephesians 5, there is a SOUL NOTE titled "The Feeling Is Mutual," and the headline argument is the popular evangelical reading of the passage: that husbands and wives submit to one another equally, drawn from Ephesians 5:21.

The problem is that Paul does not actually teach that. The popular reading does not survive a careful look at the structure of the passage, the Greek verb Paul uses, or the analogy he reaches for. Let me walk through why.

What people think Paul says

The argument runs like this. Ephesians 5:21 says "submitting to one another in the fear of God," and verse 22 says "wives, submit to your own husbands." The logic is that verse 21 sets the rule of mutual submission, verse 22 gives the wife's application, and the husband's symmetrical application must follow by inference. Therefore, the popular reading concludes, both spouses submit to one another in the same sense.

That sounds clean and even charitable. It is not what the text actually says.

Look at the structure

Beginning in verse 18 Paul instructs Spirit-filled believers in a series of participles: speaking, singing, giving thanks, submitting. Verse 21 introduces a household code that runs through chapter 6, and that code is structured as a series of paired commands to authority figures and those under their authority: wives and husbands, children and parents, slaves and masters. The pattern is consistent across all three pairs, and Paul never reverses the direction within a pair. He does not tell husbands to submit to wives, he does not tell parents to submit to children, and he does not tell masters to submit to slaves.

The word Paul actually uses

The Greek verb is hypotassō, meaning to arrange oneself under, to place oneself under authority, to subordinate oneself willingly. Paul uses the same family of words for wives in relation to husbands, children in relation to parents, slaves in relation to masters, citizens in relation to civil authorities, churches in relation to Christ, demons in relation to the apostles, and creation in relation to its order. In every interpersonal use, the verb describes ordered authority, not generic kindness. If Paul wanted husbands to submit to wives, he had the exact word in front of him. He simply never uses it for husbands.

What Paul tells husbands instead is dramatic: love her as Christ loved the church, give yourself for her, sanctify her, nourish her, cherish her. Those are extraordinarily costly commands that require profound humility. They are not, however, called submission, and the difference is not accidental.

The analogy that breaks the symmetry

Ephesians 5:23 says the husband is the head of the wife as Christ is the head of the church, and verse 24 says wives submit to their husbands as the church submits to Christ. Now ask the obvious question: does Christ submit to the church? He serves the church. He washes the church. He dies for the church. He loves the church. He never places Himself under the church's authority. If the marriage analogy mirrors the Christ-and-church relationship Paul says it does, then the husband cannot submit to his wife in the same direction the wife submits to him. The whole analogy depends on that asymmetry, and dissolving the asymmetry dissolves the gospel picture Paul is painting.

What "one another" really means

The Greek phrase allēlois, "one another," is more flexible in koine Greek than it is in modern English. It often means within the community, among yourselves, toward one another according to your respective roles, rather than mathematically symmetric reciprocity. The same Paul who writes "submit to one another" in Ephesians 5:21 writes in 1 Corinthians 11 that the head of every man is Christ and the head of every woman is man. Those two statements cannot mean perfectly identical reciprocity, or Paul is contradicting himself within the same body of letters. The honest reading lets each command sit in its actual context.

The pastoral correction is not redefinition

Many teachers reach for "mutual submission" because they have seen domineering husbands misuse Ephesians 5. That pastoral concern is real, and I take it seriously. The biblical correction to abusive headship, however, is not to redefine submission so that it disappears. The biblical correction is louder, harder, and aimed squarely at the husband: love her as Christ loved the church, even unto death. A husband who lives that command is not abusive, and a husband who does not live that command is not exercising biblical headship in the first place. The category of headship does not need to be erased; the category of self-sacrificial love needs to be enforced.

What husbands rightly yield without submitting

To be clear, a godly husband should defer constantly. He should listen to his wife with attention, prefer her interests, defer to her wisdom in areas where her wisdom is greater, repent when he is wrong, seek her flourishing over his convenience, and let her choose the restaurant more often than not. All of that is biblical, and all of that is acts of humility and sacrificial love. None of that is the same as placing himself under her covenant authority. The distinction matters because the covenant role does not move, but the daily acts of preference and service should saturate the marriage.

Bottom line

The New Testament teaches mutual love, mutual honor, mutual service, mutual humility, and mutual self-sacrifice between husbands and wives. It does not teach mutual submission in the identical sense, because Paul writes different commands to husbands and wives by design. The husband is never told to hypotassō his wife; he is told to love her as Christ loved the church. The wife is never told to love her husband as Christ loved the church; she is told to submit to her husband as the church submits to Christ. Those commands are complementary, not interchangeable, and together they reflect the gospel.

If you want to test the reading yourself, read Ephesians 5:18 through 6:9 in one sitting and underline every imperative Paul gives. Then make two columns: commands to those under authority, and commands to those exercising authority. The structure becomes plain when you see the whole household code at once, and a study Bible's SOUL NOTE will not have to do the interpretive work for you.
