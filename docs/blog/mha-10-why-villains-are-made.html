Why Villains Are Made

Nobody is born a monster. But plenty of boys get left long enough to become one.

By Adam "MOOP" Johns  ·  U.S.M.C. Ministries  ·  Written for my son Gideon  ·  July 2026

So Gideon and I were back on the couch, deep into the villain arc now, and the show did the thing that good stories do when you least want them to. It made me feel something for the bad guy. Not approval. Something worse and truer than approval. Recognition.

You know the villain I mean, Son. The one whose whole body is falling apart with hate, the one who wants to turn a whole society to dust. For a long time the show just lets him be the nightmare. Then, the way this kind of story always does, it pulls the curtain back on where the nightmare came from. And what you find behind the curtain isn't a monster. It's a small boy, alone on a street, with a power he never asked for and couldn't control, crying out in the worst moment of his life while a city full of heroes kept walking. The society was so busy polishing its heroes that not one of them stopped for the one kid who needed saving. Somebody else stopped for him. The wrong somebody. And that wrong somebody took a terrified, abandoned child and spent years teaching him to call his abandonment by another name: revenge.

I paused it. Of course I did. You gave me the look. And I want to write down for you what I was actually thinking, because it wasn't really about the cartoon anymore.

I was thinking: the world has a name for that grown man on the screen. Villain. Monster. Lost cause. Put him down. And Scripture, looking at the same man, sees something the world trained itself not to see:

A sheep that wandered off. And a Shepherd who goes after it.

Evil Is Real. Say That First.

Let me be a careful father here, because there's a soft lie that likes to hide inside a true story like this one. The soft lie says: if he was hurt, then he isn't guilty; explain the wound and you've excused the sin. That is not the gospel, and I won't hand it to you dressed up as compassion.

The villain in the show does real evil. Real people, in the real world, do real evil, and they are really responsible for it. A hard childhood explains a man; it does not acquit him. Scripture never once says the wounded get a pass. What Scripture says is harder and better than that: sin is real, guilt is real, judgment is real, and the God against whom all of it is committed still moves toward sinners with mercy. Hold both. Drop either one and you've stopped telling the truth.

“But God demonstrates His own love toward us — in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.” (Romans 5:8)

Read that twice, Son. Not after we cleaned up. Not once we deserved it. While we were yet sinners. The cross is not God rewarding the innocent. The cross is God rescuing the guilty. Which means the only reason you and I aren't the villain in somebody's story is grace, and grace is the one thing you can never look down your nose from. A man who understands his own rescue loses the ability to sneer at anyone still drowning.

The One That Wandered Off

You know I love a good backronym, but this next one isn't mine, it's the Lord's, and it's just arithmetic. Ninety-nine and one. Jesus tells it plain:

“"What man among you, if he has a hundred sheep and has lost one of them, does not leave the ninety-nine in the open pasture, and go after the one which is lost, until he finds it?” (Luke 15:4)

Look at what the Shepherd does with the math. A worldly manager keeps the ninety-nine and writes off the one; the one is a rounding error, an acceptable loss, the cost of doing business. The Shepherd runs the numbers backward. He leaves the ninety-nine and goes into the wilderness after the single sheep that wandered, and He does not come home until He is carrying it. He does not go out hoping. He goes out until.

That little word until is the whole thing. The villain the world wrote off as a rounding error, heaven counts as the one. The show, in its own dim way, feels for this. There's a moment where the hero looks at the enemy and, underneath all the rage, sees the crying kid nobody saved, and something in him wants to reach out and save that kid even now, even this late. That instinct is a shadow of something real. But it's only a shadow. The cartoon can pity the lost boy; it cannot raise him from the dead. Only the Shepherd who went all the way to a cross can go all the way into the wilderness and carry a sinner home. Mine the story for what's true, Son, then walk it up to the Cross where the real rescue happens.

The Eyes That See a Crowd

Here's the part that convicts me, because I am often the crowd that walked past the crying kid. It's easy to feel righteous about a fictional city of heroes who couldn't be bothered to stop. It's harder to admit how many times I've had the eyes of that city, seen a difficult man, an angry kid, a wrecked coworker, a brother spiraling, and felt annoyance where the Lord felt something else entirely.

“And seeing the multitudes, He felt compassion for them — because they were distressed and downcast like sheep without a shepherd.” (Matthew 9:36)

Same crowd, different eyes. The world's eyes see a threat, a nuisance, a lost cause; the Lord's eyes see sheep with no shepherd and are moved. That word is a gut word. His compassion wasn't a polite feeling; it landed in His body and put Him in motion. The difference between a hero of the city and a son of God isn't the crowd they're looking at. It's what the looking does to them. One keeps walking. The other gets involved.

And getting involved with the lost is not gentle work, so the Lord doesn't pretend it is. He gives you the rules of engagement:

“And on some have mercy — those who waver;” (Jude 22–23)

Look at that, it's a whole doctrine of rescue in two verses. Compassion for the one who's wavering. Urgency for the one already in the flames, snatch him, don't discuss him. And caution for yourself, hating even the garment the fire touched, because the man who runs into the fire to pull somebody out had better not fall in love with the smoke. That's mercy with its boots on. Not the sentimental kind that excuses the fire, the strong kind that reaches into it. Compassion, urgency, caution. Three edges on one blade.

Two Names for the Same Boy

So here is what I want burned into you, Son. The villain and the saint are not two different species. They are the same lost boy, and the only variable in the equation is whether somebody who carried the Shepherd's heart got to him in time, and whether, once reached, he turned.

That second part matters, don't skip it. Grace pursues the sinner; it doesn't pretend he never sinned. The turning has a name in the old confession, and it's a beautiful one: repentance unto life. It's what happens when a man finally sees his sin for what it is and, in the same breath, catches sight of the mercy of God in Christ, and hates the sin and turns from it and comes home. The wandering sheep still has to be carried in the Shepherd's direction. But he gets carried. That's the good news the villain in the story never got told. It's the good news you were told, and the good news you now carry to the next lost boy.

You can't reach everyone. But you can refuse to be the crowd that walks past the one.

Father & Son Talk

Three to chew on together

  1. Think of one person you've quietly written off as a "lost cause." Be honest, who is it, and what wound might be underneath the behavior you can't stand? Naming the wound doesn't excuse the sin; it's just the first step in seeing him the way the Shepherd does.
  2. Romans 5:8 says Christ died for us "while we were yet sinners." How does remembering your own rescue change the way you're allowed to look at someone still lost? Where have you been the crowd that kept walking?
  3. Jude gives three moves: compassion, urgency, and caution ("hating even the garment"). Which of the three is easiest for you, and which do you tend to skip? What goes wrong when you run into the fire but forget the caution?
⚔ Today's Quest

Go after the one

Pick the one person you were tempted to write off, and this week make a single, unowed move toward him: a text, a check-in, a "how are you actually doing," an invitation. No sermon required. Just be the somebody who stops for the kid on the street. Leave the ninety-nine of your comfortable friends for one afternoon and go after the one.

The world calls him a villain and writes him off. The Shepherd calls him the one, and goes out into the wilderness, and does not come home without him. Which name you use tells me which heart is running your chest.

Stand your post. Keep your eyes moved. Go after the one.

Peace,
Adam

What We Confess

1689 London Baptist Confession, Chapter 15 — Of Repentance Unto Life and Salvation

The Confession teaches that repentance is a gospel grace, worked in the sinner by the Spirit, so that seeing the danger and filthiness of his sin, and grasping God's mercy in Christ to the penitent, he grieves for it, hates it, and turns from it to God, purposing to walk with Him in all the ways of His commandments. It also confesses that there is no sin so great it can bring damnation on the one who truly repents, which is exactly why no lost sheep is ever past the reach of the Shepherd who goes out until He finds it.

Keach's (Baptist) Catechism — What is repentance unto life?

"Repentance unto life is a saving grace, whereby a sinner, out of a true sense of his sin, and apprehension of the mercy of God in Christ, doth, with grief and hatred of his sin, turn from it unto God, with full purpose of, and endeavour after, new obedience."

The wound explains the man; the mercy of God in Christ is what actually turns him. Both true. Hold both.

A note to Gideon

Bud, the reason I paused it isn't that I felt sorry for the bad guy. It's that I looked at that lost, furious kid on the screen and thought: that could have been almost anyone the Lord didn't reach through somebody like us. You've been reached. You know it. So you don't get to be the crowd that walks past anymore; you're on the rescue side of the equation now. Keep the two things I gave you today welded together: sin is real and grace still runs into the fire. Never soften one to make room for the other. Go be the somebody who stops. I love you. Now go.

— Dad
