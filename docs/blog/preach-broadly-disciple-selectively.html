Preach Broadly, Disciple Selectively

By Adam “MOOP” Johns  ·  U.S.M.C. Ministries  ·  Published May 29, 2026

I was talking with a man who serves with Proven Men Ministries this week, and he said something that has been working on me ever since. He was applying a marketing framework — the diffusion of innovations curve — to discipleship. His point was blunt: we should stop trying to market a hard-edged discipleship program to everybody. It dilutes the message, and it loses the very men who needed the hard call in the first place.

He is right. And what struck me is that he was not importing a corporate growth hack into the church. He was describing something Jesus already did.

The curve, briefly

In 1962, Everett Rogers mapped how any new idea moves through a population. Thirty years later, Geoffrey Moore sharpened it for the modern market. The shares are fixed and have held across thousands of studies:

SegmentSharePosture
Innovators2.5%Build it. Tolerate breakage. Driven by the thing itself.
Early Adopters13.5%Adopt for advantage. Visionaries, comfortable with rough edges.
Early Majority34%Pragmatists. Need references, standards, and proof first.
Late Majority34%Conservatives. Move only when not moving costs more.
Laggards16%Skeptics. Move only under compulsion.

Moore’s contribution was to name the deadly gap between the visionaries and the pragmatists: the chasm. Most movements die there, because the early majority will not move until the product is finished, proven, and endorsed by their peers. The fatal mistake is trying to cross that gap by softening the very thing that made the work matter.

This is the Parable of the Sower

Read the curve again and you are reading Matthew 13:1-23 as percentages. The same seed meets four soils and yields four results. Jesus named the disparity plainly — and He kept sowing. But notice where He spent His three years of depth: not on the multitudes, but on twelve men.

The pattern runs through the whole New Testament. In Luke 10:1-12, the seventy are told to find the “son of peace” in each town — a readiness filter, not a rejection of the lost. In Matthew 7:6, Jesus warns against casting pearls before swine; sacred things are allocated, not scattered. In Acts 13:46, Paul and Barnabas, once rejected, turn their effort toward receptive soil. And in 2 Timothy 2:2, Paul tells Timothy to commit the deposit “to faithful men who will be able to teach others also.”

That last verse is the whole strategy in one line. Invest in the faithful, and they multiply. The Great Commission is universal in scope — preach to every man — and selective in depth — disciple the ready. Confusing those two has wrecked more men’s ministries than any scandal.

What it means on a Tuesday night

Here is how the curve changes the way a ministry actually spends its hours.

Build for the hungry, not the hesitant. A twelve-week group designed to appeal to the late majority gets softened until the men who needed the hard call quietly disengage. Filter with your language. “For the man who knows he needs to fight” lets the wrong men self-exclude — and that is the point, not a failure.

Measure multipliers, not enrollment. In year one, success is not heads in seats. It is the men your first cohort produces who can lead the next one. Volume metrics are a trap that lure good leaders into watering down the edge.

Keep the pulpit open to every man. The curve governs where your structured hours go — never whether the gospel is preached. The man who is hostile at thirty-five may be the innovator at fifty when the crisis hits. Keep the door unlocked. Just do not pour your week into prying it open.

It starts at home

Before any of this touches a church program, it touches a kitchen table. The first discipleship cohort is the household, and every father is the family captain — the man standing watch over his home. At U.S.M.C. Ministries we frame that home as a TACC: a Temporary Abode for Character Cultivation, a station held on loan where character is forged before it is deployed. A man who will not disciple at his own table will not disciple anywhere. Start there.

No single ministry works every soil

And none should try. Across the network of ministries I serve and partner with, each one is calibrated to a different segment of the curve. Spotswood Baptist Church pairs broad proclamation from the pulpit with a structured Freedom Group for the ready. Proven Men Ministries disciples men toward sexual integrity. Noble Warriors runs retreats and rites of passage that move men across the chasm. Men of Valor International trains leaders and multiplies mentors across nations. CBMC reaches men in the marketplace where they already work. And Tun Tavern Fellowship is a brotherhood for active duty and prior service Marines, named for the 1775 Philadelphia tavern where the Corps was born. Together they cover ground no single program could.

Three things to take with you

  1. Evangelism is universal; discipleship is allocated. Keep the door open to every man, and spend your structured hours on the ready.
  2. Build for the hungry. A program softened for the reluctant loses the multipliers who needed the hard call.
  3. Measure multipliers. Faithful men who teach others are the only metric that compounds — and the only one that keeps a ministry from burning out its leaders.

The man from Proven Men was, in the end, giving me permission to stop apologizing for a strategy Scripture already endorsed. Preach broadly. Disciple selectively. Multiply relentlessly.

The diffusion framework

1. Everett M. Rogers, Diffusion of Innovations
Free Press, 1962 (5th ed. 2003). The foundational study that mapped how a new idea moves through a population in five adopter segments — innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards. The percentage shares cited in this article come from Rogers’ model, replicated across thousands of subsequent studies.
2. Geoffrey A. Moore, Crossing the Chasm
HarperBusiness, 1991 (3rd ed. 2014). Sharpened Rogers’ curve for the technology market and named “the chasm” — the deadly gap between the visionary early adopters and the pragmatic early majority where most movements die.
