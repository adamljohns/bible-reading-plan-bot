The 7 A's and the 6 F's
What Every Authentic Relationship Needs to Pass

By Adam "MOOP" Johns  ·  U.S.M.C. Ministries  ·  Published March 23, 2026  ·  Revised April 14, 2026 (full template upgrade, fixed BTE links, added footnotes)

Last week we mapped out the descent and the ascent of a man's interior life — the Six D's that kill a dream and the Seven L's that bring it back. That post was about what happens inside one man.

This one is about what happens between two.

Because the same Scripture that says "as iron sharpens iron" also says "so one man sharpens another" (Proverbs 27:17). It takes two. And not just any two — two men in a relationship that has earned the right to do the sharpening. Without that earned trust, accountability isn't accountability. It's surveillance. And men hate being surveilled.

So how do you build the kind of relationship that earns the right to speak hard truth into another man's life? How do you know if your relationship with the men you're trying to lead is actually authentic — or just a religious performance with a polite smile and a coffee cup?

There's a framework I learned a few years ago that answered this question better than anything else I'd seen. It came from Jordan Northrup, a mentor of mine who refined it in 2023.1 He called it the 7 A's and the 6 F's, and once you see it, you can't unsee it.

The 7 A's are what an authentic relationship needs to get a passing grade. Skip one and you're building on sand. Each A is a door — and you have to walk through all seven before the real work begins.

The 6 F's are the failure modes — the stages of decline a relationship goes through when one or more of the A's are missing. They're predictable. They're brutal. And once they start, they're hard to stop.

This is the field manual for masculine discipleship. Let me walk you through it.

There's a reason men don't stay in discipleship programs.

It's not that they don't want to grow. It's not that they don't believe in accountability. It's that somewhere between the first conversation and the third session, something breaks — and they disappear. Ghost. Drift. Make excuses. Stop showing up.

I've watched it happen too many times. For years, I thought the problem was them. Then Jordan showed me this framework, and I realized the problem was usually me — or more precisely, the relationship I'd built with them. Or hadn't.

The 7 A's: What a Man Must Experience Before He'll Let You In

These aren't nice-to-haves. They are prerequisites for real discipleship. Skip one and you're building on sand. Each one is a door. You walk through all seven before the real work begins.

1
Acceptance
He has to believe he's welcome as he is — not his cleaned-up Sunday version

Before a man can change, he has to believe he's welcome as he is. Not his best self. Not his cleaned-up Sunday version. Him. The guy who yells at his kids. The guy who hasn't opened his Bible in three weeks. The guy carrying shame he hasn't told anyone.

If he senses your love is conditional — contingent on performance — he will never let you past the surface.

Jesus didn't wait for people to get it together before He sat with them. The tax collectors (Matthew 9:10–13), the woman at the well (John 4:7–26), the man at the pool for 38 years (John 5:5–9) — He accepted them first. Change came after.

"Accept one another, just as Christ also accepted us, to the glory of God." — Romans 15:7

2
Admiration
Real respect — not flattery, not management

Men need to be genuinely respected — not flattered, not managed. Respected. A man can smell fake admiration from a mile away and it repels him. But real admiration — seeing his strength, his effort, his heart — unlocks something.

This is why great coaches know their players' strengths before they ever call out their weaknesses. You can't lead a man you secretly look down on.

Find something real to admire. If you can't find it yet, you haven't looked hard enough.

3
Affirmation
Admiration is what you see — affirmation is what you say

Admiration is what you see. Affirmation is what you say. Men are starved for it — not because they're weak, but because most grew up with fathers who withheld it, coaches who used silence as motivation, and a culture that mocks male vulnerability.

Specific, earned affirmation changes men. Not "you're doing great, bro" — but "I watched you choose your family over that opportunity last week. That was a hard call. That was a strong man's move." Say it. Mean it. Watch what happens.

4
Approachability
Can he come to you when he fails — or does he already know failure means a lecture?

Can he come to you when he fails? Or does he already know that failure means a lecture?

Approachability isn't about being soft — it's about being safe. A man won't bring you his real problems if he's already rehearsing your disappointment in his head. The most dangerous thing in discipleship is when a man starts performing for his mentor instead of being honest with him.

If he can't come to you at his worst, you'll only ever see his best — and his best doesn't need you.

"Brothers and sisters, if someone is caught in a sin, you who live by the Spirit should restore that person gently. But watch yourselves, or you also may be tempted." — Galatians 6:1

5
Affection
Love with skin on it — the relationship is more than transactional

This one makes men uncomfortable. But it's real. Men need to know the relationship is more than transactional — that you actually care about him, not just his attendance or what he can do for the ministry.

Affection in discipleship looks like: remembering the small things, checking in when there's nothing to check in about, showing up when he's struggling with no agenda. It's love with skin on it.

"We were well-pleased to impart to you not only the gospel of God but also our own lives, because you had become very dear to us." — 1 Thessalonians 2:8

Paul didn't just give men information. He gave them himself. That's the standard.

6
Accountability
Iron sharpening iron — calling him what he is, not just cataloging what he's done

Now we're in the harder territory. After the first five A's are established — after a man feels accepted, admired, affirmed, sees you as approachable, and knows you genuinely care — then accountability works.

Accountability without the first five is just surveillance. Men hate being surveilled. They'll comply on the surface and route around you. But accountability inside a real relationship with the first five A's? That's iron sharpening iron.

True accountability means calling him what he is — not just cataloging what he's done. You hold him to his own stated values. And because he trusts you, he doesn't just tolerate it. He wants it.

"As iron sharpens iron, so one person sharpens another." — Proverbs 27:17

And from Paul, the principle that distinguishes accountability from accusation:

"Instead, speaking the truth in love, we will grow to become in every respect the mature body of him who is the head, that is, Christ." — Ephesians 4:15

7
Authority
The whole game — authority isn't taken, it's given

This is the whole game.

Authority isn't taken — it's given. A man grants you the right to speak into his life after he's experienced the first six A's. It's the final door. You cannot kick it open. You can only earn the key.

This is why Jesus taught with an authority unlike the scribes and Pharisees — He wasn't quoting other people's opinions. He spoke from relationship, from truth, from demonstrated love. The people gave Him authority willingly because they experienced something real.

"And they were astonished at his teaching, for he taught them as one who had authority, and not as the scribes." — Mark 1:22

A man who grants you authority over his life is the most coachable, transformable human being on earth. He's not agreeing because he has to. He's agreeing because he trusts you — with his growth, his failure, his future.

Get to #7 and nothing is off the table.

"Iron sharpens iron, and one man sharpens another." — Proverbs 27:17

The 6 F's: What Happens When the A's Aren't There

Without the 7 A's — especially if #7 is demanded rather than earned — a man doesn't just stay the same. He actively retreats. And it follows a predictable pattern.

The 6 F's aren't a moral failure on his part. They're a protection mechanism — the body and soul defending themselves against a relationship that doesn't feel safe. If you understand them, you can spot them early. If you don't, you'll lose every man you try to lead.

1
Fight / Flight / Freeze
The amygdala fires — this is biology, not rebellion

The amygdala fires. Something in the relationship feels like a threat — judgment, comparison, exposure. He either attacks (deflects, argues, pushes back), disappears (misses sessions, ghosts), or freezes (shows up but shuts completely down, giving you nothing real).

This is biology. Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory shows that when the nervous system perceives a threat — even a social one — it activates the same defensive cascades that protect us from physical danger.2 A man being "spiritually accountable" while his nervous system is in fight-flight-freeze isn't being discipled. He's being interrogated.

Don't take it personally. Diagnose it. The threat isn't you specifically — it's the absence of one or more of the A's. Find which one is missing and rebuild it before you push for more growth.

2
Frustration
The gap between expectation and experience

The relationship starts to feel like work with no return. He came expecting growth and got guilt. He came for brotherhood and got homework. Frustration is the gap between expectation and experience — and it signals that one or more of the A's is missing.

A frustrated man isn't a rebellious man. He's a man whose hopes have been disappointed, and he doesn't know how to say it without sounding ungrateful. Watch for the early signs: shorter answers, fewer questions, less initiative.

3
Fatigue
Sustained frustration becomes exhaustion — this is what kills slow

Sustained frustration becomes exhaustion. He's tired of performing. Tired of the gap between who he actually is and who the relationship seems to demand he become. Fatigue is what kills slow.

Larry Crabb described this in Connecting: relationships either restore the soul or drain it, and there is no neutral middle ground.3 A discipleship relationship that drains a man — that adds weight without adding life — is a relationship in failure mode, even if all the right words are being said.

4
Faking / Facade
You're discipling a performance — not a man

A fatigued man stops trying to actually grow and starts trying to look like he's growing. He knows what to say. He knows how to answer the questions. The relationship continues — but nothing real is happening. You're discipling a performance.

This is the most dangerous stage because it can persist for years without anyone noticing. The man comes to every meeting. He prays out loud. He uses the right vocabulary. And inside he is dying, because he has learned that the relationship cannot handle who he actually is.

Patrick Morley calls this "the imposter" — the man who shows up at church for thirty years and never lets a single person see him.4 The 7 A's are the antidote. The 6 F's are the autopsy.

5
Forgetfulness of Anything Good
The negative weight has buried the positive

By this stage, the negative weight has buried the positive. He can't remember why he started. The good moments are gone. All that remains is the weight of expectations he can't meet. This is dangerous — not because he's rebellious, but because he's lost.

If you've ever talked to a man who left a church, a small group, or a discipleship relationship and asked him what was good about it — and watched him struggle to remember anything — you've watched stage 5 in action. The negativity bias of a wounded man erases the good faster than the good can be made.

6
Forced Separation
The outcome you're trying to prevent

The relationship ends — by his choice or by the structure collapsing. He sets "healthy boundaries" or simply stops showing up. He leaves more guarded than when he arrived.

Here's the tragedy: the next man who tries to disciple him will have to overcome not just the resistance every man brings, but the scar tissue from this failed relationship. Your failure doesn't just affect you. It affects everyone who tries to come after you.

This is the outcome you're trying to prevent.

The Framework at a Glance

The Seven A's — What a Relationship Needs
Acceptance → Admiration → Affirmation → Approachability → Affection → Accountability → Authority
The Six F's — What Happens When the A's Are Missing
Fight/Flight/Freeze → Frustration → Fatigue → Faking → Forgetfulness → Forced Separation
The A's are doors you walk through in order. The F's are the failure cascade when one or more of those doors is locked.

Buy-In Before Build-Up

The first 3–4 sessions of any discipleship or coaching relationship aren't about content. They're about building the A's.

Before you hand a man a reading plan, a growth chart, or a list of habits — establish:

  • Does he feel accepted as he is right now?
  • Can he tell you something real that he's ashamed of?
  • Has he seen genuine admiration from you — not flattery?
  • Does he believe he can come to you when he fails?
  • Has the relationship felt warm enough to qualify as affection and not just professionalism?

Run intake not just as data collection — but as A-building. Every question is an invitation to trust.

Only when you've walked through all 7 does real discipleship begin. And then the 6 F's lose their power — because a man who is fully known and fully loved has nothing to hide and nothing to fear.

The Authority Question

Someone once asked me: "What is your authority?"

Right question. The answer isn't your credentials. It's not your ordination. It's not your podcast following. It's not even your title.

Your authority is the sum of the A's a man has experienced with you. The degree to which he has been accepted, admired, affirmed, found you approachable, felt genuine affection, and been held accountable in love — that is your authority.

Jesus didn't say "I have authority because I went to rabbinical school." He had authority because He loved people better than anyone had ever loved them. And they gave Him everything.

Go and do likewise.

Your Next Step

Pick one man in your life and ask yourself honestly — which of the 7 A's is weak in your relationship with him?

Start there this week. Don't add another session or pile on more content — just add one A, and watch what happens.

And if you're on the other side of the table — if you're the man being discipled and one of the F's looks too familiar — you don't have to ghost. You can speak the truth. You can name which A is missing. You can ask for what you need. The same iron-sharpens-iron principle works in both directions.

This is how authentic relationships are built. This is how authority is earned. This is how men finally stop performing and start being known.

"A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another: just as I have loved you, you also are to love one another. By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another." — John 13:34–35
