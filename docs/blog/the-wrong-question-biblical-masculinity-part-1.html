The Wrong Question: Why the X Debate About Biblical Masculinity Keeps Going in Circles

By Adam "MOOP" Johns · U.S.M.C. Ministries · June 29, 2026 · Series: Biblical Masculinity, Part 1 of 5

A recent thread on X between Trevor West, Dan, Eric Conn, and me started as an argument about whether mocking your opponents is biblical, but the real fight was buried in one of my replies: "When a Christian leader prescribes virtues for men to aspire to, yet women should also embody those virtues, men are left confused and wondering: what's the diff?"

That, brothers, is the question evangelicalism has not answered in thirty years.

Ask the typical American pastor what it means to be a Christian man and you will hear the standard list: pray, read your Bible, love your wife, raise your kids, and be humble. They are good answers, and they are true answers, but they are not masculine answers. A faithful Christian woman should do every one of those things too. If "Christian man" means nothing more than "Christian who happens to be male," then the category has functionally collapsed.

Scripture refuses both ditches modern teaching falls into. Some books overshoot on being: introspection, healing, and "resting in identity," which produces passive men. Others overshoot on doing: productivity, achievement, and alpha posturing, which produces anxious men. The Bible will not let you stay in either, because the biblical order is Identity → Character → Mission, and that order matters.

Before Adam receives his commission to cultivate, guard, and name in Genesis 2, he is already something specific in Genesis 1: an image-bearer, a son under God's authority, and a covenant representative. Identity precedes responsibility, and responsibility precedes action. Paul writes the same way throughout his letters, consistently moving from indicative ("this is who you are in Christ") to imperative ("therefore live this way"). You are chosen, therefore live as chosen. You are adopted, therefore live as a son. You are redeemed, therefore walk worthy of that redemption.

But the Bible never lets identity sit alone, because the men of Scripture are relentlessly active. Adam works the garden. Abraham leaves Ur. Moses confronts Pharaoh. Joshua conquers the land. David fights. Nehemiah rebuilds. Paul plants churches. Jesus sends disciples. James says it plainly: "Faith without works is dead," not because works save, but because living faith always produces them.

So the X debate framed itself wrong. The argument was not really about mockery, and it was not really about whether someone counted as Christian. The buried question was: What makes Christian masculinity distinct from generic Christian discipleship?

If your answer is no, there is no distinction, then you have collapsed masculinity into discipleship and erased the category. If your answer is yes, you owe the next sentence: what specifically?

That is where this series goes. Four more posts walk through what Scripture commands every Christian (Part 2), what Scripture uniquely assigns to men (Part 3), the three legs of biblical manhood (Part 4), and what the conversation we are not having is costing the next generation (Part 5). "Find yourself" is the wrong answer, and so is "crush it." Scripture's answer is older, harder, and clearer than either, and most of the young men I disciple have never actually heard it.
