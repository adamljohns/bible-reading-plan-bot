Self-Care Is the Wrong Word. Stewardship Is the Right One.

By Adam "MOOP" Johns · U.S.M.C. Ministries · October 18, 2026
Listen · Pops reads the post
~8 min · word for word

The men I most respect are not the ones arguing about manhood online. They are too busy for it. They put in a full day at work, come home, and spend whatever they have left on a wife and kids who need them, and by bedtime they are tired in the right direction. A man like that does not need a softer word for discipline. He needs a true one.

Ryan Michler covered most of this ground on the Order of Man episode “What Self-Care for Men Actually Looks Like,” and he deserves the credit for it. I am not going to reprint his script. I want to keep the ten things he tells men to do, set them under Scripture, and push back on the two places where I think the conversation slips.

Ten orders, not ten tips

I call them orders on purpose. A tip is something you try when you feel like it. An order is something you carry out because someone is depending on you.

  1. Wake up on purpose. Whatever you say matters most should get your first attention of the day, before the phone and the inbox get theirs. Writing a list is not the same as walking with God, and letting the day drift is not the same as being humble. David put it this way: “My voice shalt thou hear in the morning, O Lord; in the morning will I direct my prayer unto thee, and will look up” (Psalm 5:3, KJV).
  2. Train mind, body, and soul. Read something that is capable of correcting you, lift something heavy, and then open your Bible, pray to God through Jesus Christ, and get yourself to church on Sunday. Paul gives the order of importance: “For bodily exercise profiteth little: but godliness is profitable unto all things” (1 Timothy 4:8, KJV).
  3. Read to obey. A stack of half-finished books looks good on the nightstand, but it is a badge rather than a life. I would rather you read fewer books, take notes, and actually do what they say. James 1:22 calls us to be doers of the word and not hearers only.
  4. Get brothers before the crisis. The divorce papers, the layoff, or the funeral is a bad time to start looking for men who will tell you the truth. Find them now, while things are calm. Iron sharpens iron (Proverbs 27:17), and we are told to exhort one another daily so that none of us is hardened by sin’s deceit (Hebrews 3:12–13).
  5. Track the money. Know every dollar that comes in and every dollar that goes out, and plug the leaks you find. Handled well, money buys you time with your wife and children. It was never meant to be the prize. “Be thou diligent to know the state of thy flocks, and look well to thy herds” (Proverbs 27:23, KJV).
  6. Be home when you are home. Put the phone down when you walk in the door. Your wife and kids deserve the whole man, not whatever is left of him after everyone else has had a turn. God told fathers, “And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children,” including “when thou sittest in thine house” (Deuteronomy 6:7, KJV), and you cannot do that from behind a screen.
  7. Own the miss. When you get it wrong, skip the excuse. Say what you did, fix what you can, and keep moving. “He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy” (Proverbs 28:13, KJV).
  8. Examine the heart. Anger, sorrow, and guilt are not sins in themselves, but using them to tear people down is. Sit still long enough to see who you have hurt, and then go make it right. Pray with David, “Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts” (Psalm 139:23, KJV).
  9. Write the code before the temptation. No man wrecks his marriage in a single moment. He walks there one small compromise at a time, usually telling himself each step is harmless. Job made a covenant with his eyes (Job 31:1), and Solomon tells us to keep our hearts with all diligence, because out of them flow the issues of life (Proverbs 4:23). Decide where your lines are while your head is clear.
  10. Do hard things for a reason you can name. Comfort dressed up as a calling is still comfort. And hardship that is not tied to any duty is not discipline either; it is just stubbornness with better branding. Paul trained with a target in view: “I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection” (1 Corinthians 9:27, KJV), so that he would not be disqualified from the work God gave him.

Put those ten together and what you have is stewardship. Paul tells us in Ephesians 5:15–16 to walk carefully, not as fools but as wise, redeeming the time, because the days are evil. And in 1 Timothy 5:8 he says that a man who will not provide for his own household has denied the faith. That is a long way from self-care. Self-care asks what will make you feel better. Stewardship asks what you owe, and to whom.

Where the conversation slips

The first slip is the idea that a man can train his soul with “whatever your book is” or “whoever your god is.” I understand why a broad podcast says it that way, but it does not hold up. Two claims that contradict each other cannot both be true. Jesus said He is the way, the truth, and the life, and that no one comes to the Father except through Him (John 14:6). He prayed that the Father would sanctify us by the truth, and He said plainly that God’s word is truth (John 17:17). A men’s show can be genuinely useful and still go wrong the moment it puts the living God on a shelf as one option among many.

The second slip is treating the examination of your sin like Carl Jung’s shadow work. Honest self-examination matters, and most of us avoid it. But looking at your sin does not cleanse you of it. A man who just stares into his own darkness tends to end up either proud of how deep he has gone or crushed by what he found. Paul says godly sorrow produces repentance that leads to salvation, while the sorrow of the world produces death (2 Corinthians 7:10). We are told to put off the old man and put on the new (Ephesians 4:22–24), and the power to do that comes from Christ, not from a technique.

Do one thing tomorrow

Please do not read this, collect ten new habits, and then do none of them. Pick the one order you already know you are failing, and start there.

  • If you are isolated, get one godly man on your calendar this week. Do not wait for the wreck to go find him.
  • If you are home but not really there, leave the phone in another room from supper until the kids are in bed.
  • If you have been calling your sin “just a moment,” write your code tonight. Pick one line you will not cross, and tell your wife what it is.

The culture is loud right now, and it has a lot of opinions about what a man should be. The best answer I know is a man who quietly leads his own house well. He does not need a comment section to prove it.
