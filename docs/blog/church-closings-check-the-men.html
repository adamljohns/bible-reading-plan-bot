100,000 Churches Closing? Check the Number. Then Check the Men.

CNN says a “tsunami” of church closings is coming. The counts tell a slower, harder, more hopeful story. And they skip the question that matters most: where were the men?

By Adam “MOOP” Johns · U.S.M.C. Ministries · Pastoral voice: Chaplain Chuck Whitfield · Theology review: Preacher John

CNN ran a story this week with a headline built to scare you: a “tsunami” of church closings, 100,000 American churches closed or repurposed in the next decade. It’s tied to a new PBS series, Christianity at a Crossroads. The story opens with a United Methodist pastor in Belding, Michigan, preaching a eulogy for her own congregation: about a dozen elderly members in a sanctuary built for a thousand. No choir. No children. No young families.

That scene is real. The number deserves a harder look.

Where 100,000 comes from

CNN links the figure to a 2024 piece on the Congress for the New Urbanism’s site, an urban-planning group, reprinted from a Buffalo News op-ed written after the Catholic Diocese of Buffalo announced it would close about half its churches. That piece cites one estimate from a former research official at the National Council of Churches: 100,000 churches closed “over the next several years.” That’s an estimate passed along from article to article, not a count.

Here’s what the counts say:

  • Lifeway Research estimates 4,000 Protestant churches closed in 2024 and 3,800 were started. That’s a net loss of about 200, out of roughly 293,000 Protestant congregations.
  • That’s a big improvement on 2019, when closures beat openings by 1,500. In 2014, more churches opened than closed.
  • In the Southern Baptist Convention, 715 churches disbanded or closed in 2024. That’s the smallest yearly loss since the SBC’s peak in 2017, and with new plants and new affiliations the convention ended the year only 183 congregations down.
  • The Hartford Institute for Religion Research surveyed nearly 7,500 congregations of every faith and found median in-person attendance rose to 70. That’s above pre-pandemic levels and the first increase in 25 years.

So is the church collapsing? No. Is it fine? Also no. Hartford found the typical congregation is about half the size it was in 2000. Big congregations are growing, small ones are shrinking, and nearly half are still losing people. Lifeway’s sharpest finding: among Southern Baptist churches, the only group whose membership grew over five years was churches started since 2000. They grew 12%. Every older group shrank.

Strip away the headline and the story is this. Many old, aging congregations are dying. New churches that reach new people are carrying the load.

Positive or negative?

Someone in our SBC group asked the right question. Long-term, is this good or bad?

Some of both, and the Bible has categories for each.

Some of it may be judgment. Jesus told the church at Ephesus, “Repent and do the first works, or else I will come to you quickly and remove your lampstand from its place—unless you repent.” (Revelation 2:5, NKJV) Notice who He said that to. Ephesus worked hard and tested false apostles. Their problem wasn’t bad doctrine. “You have left your first love.” (Revelation 2:4, NKJV) If Christ will take the lampstand from an orthodox church that won’t repent, a congregation that has stopped preaching Christ crucified and risen is in far worse trouble than losing its building. But we can’t read God’s verdict off a closing notice. We don’t mourn every closed door the same way, and we don’t pronounce judgment on any one of them either.

Some of it is pruning. “Every branch in Me that does not bear fruit He takes away; and every branch that bears fruit He prunes, that it may bear more fruit.” (John 15:2, NKJV) Jesus is talking about disciples abiding in Him, not property. But the Father does cut away what keeps fruit from growing. One pastor in the CNN piece, Matt Braddock in Silver Spring, Maryland, closed his church’s building, which cost about $400,000 a year to insure and maintain, and moved his people into another church’s basement. Membership stabilized, pledges got stronger, and they could afford staff. The building was never the church.

And some of it is loss we should grieve. Faithful old saints watch the church where they were baptized, married and buried their parents go dark because no one younger stayed. That’s a family failure before it’s a statistic.

What doesn’t change is the promise. “On this rock I will build My church, and the gates of Hades shall not prevail against it.” (Matthew 16:18, NKJV) That promise belongs to Christ’s church, the whole company of His people, not to any one congregation or building. He isn’t worried about 2036. His church will stand. Whether your congregation does is a different question.

The question CNN didn’t ask

Go back to that Michigan sanctuary. Twelve elderly members. No children, no young families.

I don’t know that congregation’s story. But I know the pattern, and it raises a question the article never asked: where were the men?

Most churches don’t die the Sunday they close. They die slowly, years earlier. A church lives by the Word and the Spirit, not by its men. But God ordinarily passes the faith down through faithful men: “commit these to faithful men who will be able to teach others also.” (2 Timothy 2:2, NKJV) One of the surest signs of a dying church is men who stop leading, stop bringing their sons, stop discipling the next man. When fathers drift, families drift with them. When men sit in the back row hiding their sin, the church gets quieter and older until one day it’s a eulogy.

CNN’s list of what growing churches share is fine as far as it goes: accessible parking, lively music, powerful preaching, hospitality, a ministry kids actually want to come to. But it skips the thing underneath all of it: men who are walking with God, honest about their battles, and willing to carry other men.

That’s the work. Not saving buildings. Raising men.

What to do about it

  • Get in a group of men who tell the truth. Not an attendance program. A room where no one hides, every man has a battle buddy, and sin gets named instead of managed.
  • Disciple your own house first. Your sons and daughters will decide what church is by watching what it is to you.
  • Know your church, or find a living one. Our Church Directory scores more than 28,500 congregations on the same ten-category scorecard of doctrine and practice. Look up the church you’re in. If you’re looking, find one where the Word is preached and men are being raised up, not just filling seats.
  • Back church planting. The data is plain: new churches are where the growth is. If your church has never helped start one, ask why.
  • Stop measuring your church by its building. Measure it by whether the men in it are becoming more like Christ.

The tsunami headline sells. The real story is slower, harder and more hopeful. The church isn’t drowning. Some congregations are, and many of them started going under long before the closing notice, when the men stopped standing up.

Christ will build His church. The question is whether He’ll build it with us.
