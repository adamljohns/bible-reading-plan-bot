Pride Doesn’t Think; Pride Knows

By Adam “MOOP” Johns · drafted with AI assistance, approved by Adam Johns
Listen to this post · 4½ min

Fourteen years ago, sitting in Tampa with my iPhone notepad, I wrote that the common denominator between me and the three brothers I kept arguing with was pride, and that if it was given root it would do its dividing work and eventually dominate anyone and anything it touched (The Common Denominator is the Common Dominator). I still believe every word of that, but this week I learned that pride has a second hiding place I had not named back then, and it was not in how I argue so much as in what I claim to know.

It started, as these things do now, with a two-minute clip on X and a chorus of Christians declaring that the man in it was not a Christian. Some of what he said in that clip was strange, and I would not defend it, but he has professed Christ by name for years, and I found myself writing back that the reason you cannot determine whether somebody else is a Christian is not that judging is forbidden but that you are not God. You can test what a man teaches against the Word, and you should, but the verdict on his soul belongs to the One who searches hearts: “Who are you to judge another’s servant? To his own master he stands or falls” (Romans 14:4, NKJV).

Then, I am ashamed to say, I did the very thing I had just warned against. Trying to explain why a former ally had turned critic, I offered the men around me a theory about what was going on in his heart, and it took a brother asking me plainly how I could know that before I saw that I had traded one kind of certainty for another. That is when a line I have carried around for years came back to me, a line I always thought belonged to C. S. Lewis: pride doesn’t think; pride knows.

I went looking for it, and so did my research team, through seventeen of Lewis’s books, and it is not there. As far as we can tell nobody else has written it, so I will own it as mine, and I think Lewis would at least recognize it, because the idea runs all through his work. In Mere Christianity he wrote:

As long as you are proud you cannot know God. A proud man is always looking down on things and people: and, of course, as long as you are looking down, you cannot see something that is above you.

Pride closes the very door it claims to have walked through, and Lewis shows two ways it counterfeits knowledge. In The Pilgrim’s Regress, Mr. Enlightenment explains to young John that “if you make the same guess often enough it ceases to be a guess and becomes a Scientific Fact,” which is how a two-minute clip becomes a settled verdict once enough people repeat it. In The Great Divorce, a ghost who was a bishop on earth boasts that “for me there is no such thing as a final answer,” and the Spirit answers him:

Once you were a child. Once you knew what inquiry was for. There was a time when you asked questions because you wanted answers, and were glad when you had found them… Thirst was made for water; inquiry for truth.

The first man knows what he cannot know, the second refuses to know what has been plainly given, and both of them are proud. G. K. Chesterton put the cure in one sentence in Orthodoxy, that “a man was meant to be doubtful about himself, but undoubting about the truth; this has been exactly reversed.” Humility is not the same thing as uncertainty about everything; it is being sure of what God has revealed and willing to stay unsure of what only God can see, which is why the humble man can say “I don’t know yet” without feeling smaller for it, while the proud man would rather be wrong out loud than admit he is guessing.

Paul had already said it better than any of us: “Knowledge puffs up, but love edifies. And if anyone thinks that he knows anything, he knows nothing yet as he ought to know” (1 Corinthians 8:1-2, NKJV). Solomon is harder still, “Do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him” (Proverbs 26:12, NKJV), and James tells us why it matters so much: “God resists the proud, But gives grace to the humble” (James 4:6, NKJV).

So this is where I have landed fourteen years after Tampa. When I catch myself certain about another man’s heart, his motives or his eternity, I am going to treat that certainty as a warning light rather than a conclusion, because pride doesn’t think, pride knows, and humility is content to keep thinking until God shows it what is true. And if I get it wrong, I want to be the kind of man who goes back to the brothers I said it to and takes it off the table, and this week I had to do just that.

Lord, forgive us for our pride once again, and teach us to be doubtful about ourselves and undoubting about You. In Christ’s Name, Amen.

Peace,
Adam
