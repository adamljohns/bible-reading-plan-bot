Now It's Your Turn

The handoff. A father spends himself fully, and passes the torch.

By Adam "MOOP" Johns  ·  U.S.M.C. Ministries  ·  Written for my son Gideon  ·  July 2026

So Gideon and I were on the couch again, near the end of this whole story we've been walking through together, and All Might, the strongest hero, the one they called the Symbol of Peace, finally ran his tank all the way to empty.

Here's the setup, in my own words, for the folks who haven't watched. For a long time this man carried a power that had a clock on it. Every day he could only hold his hero form for so many minutes before his body gave out and he shrank back into his true shape, a hollow, skeletal frame he hid from the world so people would keep feeling safe. And in his last great fight, against the villain who had spent a whole lifetime taking power from other people, All Might had nothing left in reserve. So he stopped rationing. He burned the last ember. He pushed past the line where a wise man stops, threw the final punch he had in him, and won. And then, in front of the whole watching world, hollowed out, done, he lifted his arm, pointed past the cameras at the boy he'd been training, and passed it on.

"Now, it's your turn." (All Might.)

I paused it. Gideon knows the pause by now. He gave me the look that says here it comes, and it did, because I wasn't thinking about a cartoon anymore. I was thinking about a bony old prophet's cousin standing in a river, watching his own crowd walk off toward a younger man, and saying the truest sentence a mentor ever says out loud.

“He must increase; I must decrease."” (John 3:30)

Read that twice, Son. That is not a defeat talking. That is a man who understood the whole point of his own life. John the Baptist had disciples, a crowd, a reputation, and the moment the real thing showed up, he did not clutch any of it. He got smaller on purpose, gladly, so the greater One could get larger. The world has never once called that success. Scripture calls it the highest thing a servant ever does.

The World Says Hold On; the Kingdom Says Hand Off

There's a lie loose among men, and it wears the mask of ambition. It says the goal is to be the strongest one in the room for as long as you possibly can, to stay the hero, to keep the spotlight warm, to be needed right up to the end. And a lot of men run their whole race that way and then panic when the strength finally goes, because they built their name on being the answer instead of raising the next one.

But watch how a real man closes out. Paul is in a Roman cell, near the end, no crowd, no muscle left, and he is not panicking. He is filing a report.

“For I am already being poured out as a drink offering, and the time of my departure has come. I have fought the good fight. I have finished the race. I have kept the faith.” (2 Timothy 4:6–7)

Notice who he's writing to. Timothy. His son in the faith, the younger man he'd been pouring into for years, the one who would carry it after him. Paul isn't writing a memoir; he's making a handoff. The whole letter is a torch changing hands. That is the aviation way too, honestly. Twenty-one years in the Marine Corps taught me a thing that took me longer to learn in the home than the cockpit: the senior man does not eat first, and the senior man does not fly the last sortie of the war just to keep his name on the flight schedule. He briefs the young stick, he sits back seat, and one day he climbs out of the aircraft for good and watches somebody he trained take the mission. A captain who hoards the playbook isn't a captain. He's a hobbyist.

Joshua is the one who marched into the land, not Moses. And Moses knew it, laid his own hands on the younger man's head, and blessed the handoff instead of resenting it. The men who finish well are the ones who measured success by what walked forward after them, not by how long they stayed the strongest man standing.

He Was Thinking About the Ones He Wouldn't See

Here's the part that gets me as a father. When Peter knew his own time was short, he didn't spend it building a monument to Peter. He spent it making sure they'd remember after he was gone.

“I think it right, as long as I am in this body, to stir you up by way of reminder — knowing that the laying aside of my body will be soon, just as our Lord Jesus Christ has made clear to me. And I will make every effort, so that after my departure you will always be able to call these things to remembrance.” (2 Peter 1:13–15)

After my decease. He's writing so the truth outlives the teacher. That is the exact posture of a man burning his last ember for the ones coming behind, and it lands on a father like a weight and a joy at the same time. Because Son, the honest math of it is this: I will not be in the room for most of your life. I get these years, the couch, the paused shows, the drives, the Tuesday mornings, and then a day comes when the strength goes and the aircraft I climb out of is my own body. And when I think about that, I do not want to have spent these years being your hero. I want to have spent them making you one.

“Now also when I am old and grayheaded, O God, do not forsake me, until I declare Your strength to this generation, Your power to everyone who is to come.” (Psalm 71:18)

Every one that is to come. That's you, and it's your kids you haven't met yet, and it's whoever the LORD hands you to disciple down the line. The old man in that psalm isn't asking to stay strong so people keep admiring him. He's asking to last just long enough to show the next generation and then get out of the way. That's the prayer. Not make me great; make me a bridge.

Where the Anime Runs Out of Road

Now I have to be honest with the show the way I'd want you to be honest, Son, because a shadow is only ever a shadow. All Might handed off a power, a thing he carried, that got a little stronger each time it changed hands. That's a beautiful picture, and it's true as far as it goes. But there's a place it can't go. All Might had to decrease because he was running out. He was mortal, spent, finished, and the torch had to move because the man was dying and could do no more.

That is not why the true Champion says the same words. When Jesus finished His fight, He did not decrease because He had nothing left. He laid His life down of His own strength and took it back up again. Our High Priest didn't burn His last ember and fade; He fully paid what was owed, rose in power, and sat down, because a finished work sits down. When He said it was finished, it was finished. And here's the wonder: the power He hands to you and me is not a candle running low. It is the very Spirit of the risen and exalted Christ, and it does not diminish in the giving. All Might handed off what was left of him. Jesus hands off the fullness of Himself, and has just as much the next morning.

He saved you to send you.

So when the LORD points at you and says your turn, Son, He is not pointing because He got tired. He is pointing because that was always the plan; that men He rescued would become men He sends. He must increase. And the strange, glad secret of the whole Christian life is that you increase too, precisely by decreasing under Him. The servant who says less of me, more of Him is not disappearing. He's being handed the torch.

⚔ Today's Quest

Hand something off, on purpose, today.

Pick one thing you're good at, a skill, a job, a piece of what you know, and instead of just doing it, teach it to somebody younger or greener than you and let them do it while you stand back. Coach, don't grab the controls. Feel how it stings a little to not be the one, and do it anyway. That sting is the beginning of becoming a man who builds other men.

Father & Son Talk
  1. John the Baptist said "He must increase, but I must decrease," and he said it gladly. Where in your life right now does it feel like staying big matters more to you than pointing people to Jesus? Name one.
  2. Paul spent his last strength writing to Timothy, not writing about himself. If your life ended tonight, who is the "Timothy" you've been pouring into, and if the honest answer is nobody yet, who could it be starting this week?
  3. All Might had to hand off his power because he was running out; Jesus hands off His power full and undiminished. How does it change the way you'd spend yourself for other people, knowing you're passing on Someone who never runs low?
What We Confess

The 1689 London Baptist Confession, Chapter 8: Of Christ the Mediator

Paragraph 5 confesses that "the Lord Jesus, by His perfect obedience and sacrifice of Himself, which He through the eternal Spirit once offered up unto God, hath fully satisfied the justice of God," and "purchased not only reconciliation, but an everlasting inheritance in the kingdom of heaven, for those whom the Father hath given unto Him." The handoff we make is real, but it rests on a work already finished. All Might spent his last ember and had nothing left; our Mediator satisfied God's justice in full and could not be held by death. A father passes a torch he did not light.

1689 LBCF, Ch. 8, Of Christ the Mediator

Baptist (Keach's) Catechism — Christ's Exaltation

Q. Wherein consisteth Christ's exaltation?
A. Christ's exaltation consisteth in His rising again from the dead on the third day, in ascending up into heaven, in sitting at the right hand of God the Father, and in coming to judge the world at the last day.

This is why the handoff has power behind it. We do not point our sons toward a fallen hero or a fading memory, but toward a living, reigning, exalted King who sits at the right hand of the Father right now. He must increase, forever.

Baptist Catechism, on the Exaltation of Christ

Stand your post. Train the next man. Then, when He points, get out of the way and let Him grow larger in you.

He must increase.

Semper Fi,
Adam

A note to Gideon

Bud, you sat next to me for the whole ride of this show, and near the end you watched a strong man empty himself out to win, then turn and point at the kid he'd been raising up. I want you to know I saw myself in that scene, and I saw you in it too.

These years with you are my ember. I'm not rationing them. I would rather spend everything I've got making you into a man who can carry it than hold anything back to keep being the strong one in the house. That's not a sad thing to me. It's the whole point. My father did it for me; his father, in his own broken way, tried to do it for him; and now it comes to you.

So here's the pass. Everything I know about God, about being a man, about getting back up after you fall, and I have fallen plenty, it's yours. Not to admire. To use. He saved you to send you. One day the LORD is going to point at you and say your turn, and I want you ready, not surprised.

You're ready sooner than you think. I love you. Now go.

— Dad
