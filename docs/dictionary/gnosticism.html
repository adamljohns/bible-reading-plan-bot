Gnosticism
/ˈnɒs.tɪ.sɪ.zəm/
noun
From Greek gnōsis (γνῶσις) — knowledge; from gignōskein (to know). The name covers a family of religious movements that grew up beside the early church in the first and second centuries and taught that salvation comes through secret knowledge (gnosis) that awakens a divine element in the soul and frees it from the material world. Its best-known schools (Simon Magus and his heirs, Basilides, Saturninus, the Sethian writings, and above all Valentinus and his followers) blended Jewish speculation, Greek Platonic dualism and Christian vocabulary, and the apostles and the early church fathers fought them hard.

Biblical Definition

Gnosticism is the religion of saving secret knowledge. Every religion claims to know something, but Gnosticism makes the knowing itself the way of salvation. The Pocket Dictionary of Theological Terms gives a sound baseline: “Gnostics believed that devotees had gained a special kind of spiritual enlightenment through which they had attained a secret or higher level of knowledge not accessible to the uninitiated, and they tended to emphasize the spiritual realm over the material which was considered evil and to be escaped” (Grenz, Guretzki and Nordling, 56). Across all its forms, two marks are always present: spirit is set above matter, and a special revealed knowledge is held by insiders that the ordinary believer does not have.

The cosmology. The classic second-century systems that Irenaeus describes (Against Heresies 1.1–1.5) and that the Nag Hammadi books preserve (for example, the Apocryphon of John) tell the same basic story. Far above this world is an unknowable highest God surrounded by a “fullness” (pleroma) of divine emanations called aeons. One of them, Sophia (Wisdom), falls out of order, and from that fall comes a lesser and ignorant creator, the demiurge (in Sethian texts, Yaldabaoth), who makes the material world and thinks he is the only god. The God of Genesis is demoted to this lesser being, matter becomes a prison or a defect, and the secret books carry the hidden story. So the usual short definition (matter is evil, a lesser god made it, and the truth is in secret writings) is accurate for the cosmology.

The anthropology and the salvation. In some human beings a spark or seed of the divine is trapped in flesh. Salvation is the awakening of that spark by gnosis, after which it returns upward to the pleroma; the body is left behind for good. The Valentinians carried this furthest by dividing mankind into three kinds by nature, with the “spiritual” saved by what they are (see Saved by Nature below). Because flesh could not be saved, most Gnostics also said Christ only seemed to have a body, which is Docetism.

Scripture’s answer. God made the material world and called it very good (Genesis 1:31). The Word was made flesh (John 1:14), and John says that every spirit that will not confess that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is not of God (1 John 4:2–3). Paul warned the Colossians against philosophy and vain deceit, because in Christ “dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily” (Colossians 2:9, KJV), and he told Timothy to avoid “what is falsely called knowledge” (1 Timothy 6:20, NKJV), where “knowledge” is the Greek gnōsis. The knowledge the Bible prizes is open, not secret: “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom, And the knowledge of the Holy One is understanding” (Proverbs 9:10, NKJV). One caution from the paper: setting your mind on things above (Colossians 3:2) is not Gnostic. The Gnostic error is to call everything physical evil and everything spiritual good, when Scripture shows us evil spirits that Jesus cast out and a physical creation that God called good.

Saved by Nature: The Valentinian Three Classes

Valentinus’s school taught that the “spiritual” are saved by nature, the “material” perish by nature, and ordinary church members sit in the middle with free will, needing faith and good works. This is the doctrine behind the modern charge that Calvinist election is Gnostic.

expand to see more

Valentinus taught in Rome in the middle of the second century (about 136–160), and his disciples (Ptolemy, Heracleon, Theodotus and others) built the most developed Gnostic theology of the age. They divided human beings into three kinds: the material or hylic (“of the earth”), the psychic (“of soul”) and the pneumatic (“of spirit”), and they read them back into Cain, Abel and Seth (Clement of Alexandria, Excerpts from Theodotus 54).

Irenaeus reports their teaching in his own words. Material things “must of necessity perish,” the psychic “passes to the side to which inclination draws it,” and then comes the claim that matters for the debate: “Wherefore also they maintain that good works are necessary to us, for that otherwise it is impossible we should be saved. But as to themselves, they hold that they shall be entirely and undoubtedly saved, not by means of conduct, but because they are spiritual by nature” (Against Heresies 1.6.1–2, ANF). The “us” there is the church. The Valentinians called ordinary Christians psychics, people “established by their works, and by a mere faith,” and called themselves “the elect seed” (1.6.2, 1.6.4). Irenaeus adds that some of them drew the obvious conclusion and lived as they pleased, since gold sunk in filth is still gold (1.6.2–3).

Theodotus, as Clement preserved him, puts it in one line: “Now the spiritual is saved by nature, but the psychic has free-will, and has the capacity for both faith and incorruptibility, as well as for unbelief and corruption according to its own choice; but the material perishes by nature” (Excerpts from Theodotus 56, trans. Casey).

Does Calvinist election repeat this? The charge is made in good faith and has history behind it. Irenaeus answered the Valentinians by insisting that God made man free from the beginning and that it is not true that some are good by nature and others bad (Against Heresies 4.37), and the early fathers stressed human free choice partly because the Gnostics denied it. In our day Kenneth Wilson has argued that Augustine, after 412, brought the determinism of his Manichaean past into the church’s doctrine of grace, and that Calvin inherited it. Both systems speak of an elect, both say the outcome is certain before anyone acts, and the Valentinians even used the word “elect.” Those overlaps are why the comparison lands with readers.

The comparison breaks at the root, though, and the differences are in the primary sources themselves:

• Nature versus grace. The Valentinian pneumatic is saved because of what he is, a seed of the divine substance. Reformed theology says that no one is saved by nature, because all of us “were by nature children of wrath, just as the others” (Ephesians 2:3, NKJV). Election is God’s choice of guilty sinners “according to the good pleasure of His will” (Ephesians 1:5, NKJV), “out of his mere free grace and love” with nothing in the creature as its cause (Westminster Confession and 1689 London Baptist Confession, 3.5). The Gnostic elect are better stuff; the Reformed elect are the same ruined stuff as everyone else, shown mercy.

• The body and the world. The Valentinian scheme saves the spirit and lets matter perish. Reformed theology confesses a good creation, a Savior who took real flesh, and the resurrection of the body.

• Conduct. Irenaeus says the Valentinian spirituals thought conduct did not matter for them. Reformed theology says the elect are “created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them” (Ephesians 2:10, NKJV), and that true believers persevere in holiness (1689 London Baptist Confession, chapters 13 and 17).

• Secret versus public. Gnostic salvation came through hidden teaching for initiates. The Reformed gospel is preached openly to every person, and faith comes by hearing it.

• Where free will sits. The Valentinians gave free will and a faith-plus-works salvation to the psychic middle class, which is to say, to the ordinary church. Their system does not line up neatly with any modern camp. Augustine, for his part, wrote against the Manichaean teaching of two kinds of souls (On Two Souls), so the line from Gnostic natures to Augustinian grace is contested from the start.

The paper gives a test that cuts both ways and is the fairest place to land: “The greatest indicator of someone drifting into Gnosticism is when the knowledge they claim to hold offers them a sort of salvation.” Any Christian, Calvinist or not, who treats his system, his insider vocabulary or his superior understanding as the thing that sets him above ordinary believers has picked up the Valentinian spirit Irenaeus described, people who “highly exalt themselves, and claim to be perfect, and the elect seed” (1.6.4). Both sides of the debate confess that we are saved by grace through faith, not by nature and not by knowledge (Ephesians 2:8–9).

Gnosticism Through the Centuries

The paper’s thesis: from the early church to modern congregations, Gnostic tendencies have crept into Christian thought, sometimes pushed by leaders who did not know it, leading people astray and dividing the body of Christ; but Christians who understand it can guard against it and help others do the same.

The first and second centuries. Organized Gnosticism shows up alongside the church in the first century, and we know it mostly through the writings of those who opposed it, because theirs were better preserved. R. M. Grant and others have argued that it grew among Jews disillusioned when apocalyptic hopes went unfulfilled, possibly before AD 70, though the developed systems appear after the fall of Jerusalem. The church fathers named Simon Magus, the sorcerer Peter rebuked for trying to buy the power of God (Acts 8:9–24), as its first teacher (Against Heresies 1.23). The apostle John opposed Cerinthus, and Irenaeus tells the story of John leaving a bathhouse instead of staying under the same roof with him (3.3.4). Ignatius warned the churches against those who said Christ only seemed to suffer (To the Smyrnaeans 2). In the second century came Saturninus and Basilides, then Marcion and Valentinus, and with them books like the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Truth, the Gospel of Philip, the Gospel of Mary and the Gospel of Judas that challenged the four Gospels. Justin Martyr and Irenaeus led the defense, and Irenaeus wrote Against Heresies around 180 mostly against the Gnostic schools. As Mark Noll points out, Irenaeus, Justin and Justin’s pupil Tatian defended the fourfold Gospel against Marcion’s cut-down canon, yet Tatian himself later fell into Gnostic teaching (Against Heresies 1.28.1), which is a standing warning that sitting under good teaching does not make anyone immune.

The Middle Ages and the Reformation. After that apologetic campaign, few writings in the medieval and Reformation eras can be called Gnostic outright, but the appetite for knowledge held by a few did not go away. The paper argues that the medieval church fostered a Gnostic-like pattern of its own: ordinary people attended a mass in a language they did not understand, could not read the Scriptures, and deferred to the pope and to tradition on doctrines like transubstantiation, the veneration of Mary and indulgences for purgatory, so that saving knowledge sat with a clergy instead of in the hands of believers. The clearest medieval heirs of Gnostic dualism were the Paulicians, the Bogomils of the Balkans (tenth century) and the Cathars or Albigensians of southern France (twelfth and thirteenth centuries), who taught two opposing principles and an evil material world. The Reformation was a recovery from that darkness. Luther’s 95 Theses (1517) and the five solas are anti-Gnostic at the core, because none of them rests on knowledge or spirit alone, and Luther knew Paul’s word that knowledge puffs up but love builds up (1 Corinthians 8:1). Luther, Tyndale, Olivétan and Lefèvre put the Bible into German, English and French, so that ordinary people could read it and know God for themselves. Even so, as Colonel Doner wrote, Gnosticism survives “by its ability to adapt, adopt, merge, or permeate,” and the antidote is sound doctrine joined to faithful practice, orthodoxy with orthopraxy.

The colonial era to the present. After the Reformation, the paper argues, Gnosticism lay largely dormant while churches in Europe and America held to orthodox teaching and the Enlightenment turned minds to science and reason, and it did not rear its head again until the twentieth century. Some of the return came through mysticism: the paper points to Vladimir Lossky’s Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church, which tied doctrine to mystical experience and the pursuit of theosis, much as Clement and Origen of Alexandria had once offered a “true gnostic” Christianity to compete with Valentinus. Movements that rest on new revelation outside Scripture, such as Mormonism, share the same appetite for hidden knowledge. In December 1945 farmers near Nag Hammadi in Egypt found thirteen leather-bound Coptic codices holding about fifty texts, many of them Gnostic; one codex was bought for the C. G. Jung Institute, and the find fed a popular revival. The paper’s point stands: an old manuscript is not a true one, any more than an ancient copy of Homer would make the Iliad history.

Modern forms. The paper names three in the church today, each treating a belief as knowledge with a saving or self-justifying edge: what Voddie Baucham calls “ethnic Gnosticism” in Fault Lines (the claim that one’s ethnicity grants special knowledge of racism, known in the academy as standpoint epistemology); a gospel of signs and wonders that makes miracles the proof of the faith; and the “soft Gnosticism” of conspiracy theories, which has divided congregations and hurt the church’s witness. Add the prosperity gospel, emotional worship that sidelines doctrine, and loose new “translations” that import a teacher’s ideas into the text. The defense is old and plain: study the Scriptures to show yourself approved (2 Timothy 2:15), be content with what God has not revealed (1 Timothy 6:6), and stay humble, because pride is what keeps Gnostic belief alive. As Noll asks in Turning Points, could the intense spiritual life of Christianity be told apart from groups that relied on secret wisdom? Only by staying with what God has openly revealed.

Key Scripture

• John 1:14 — "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth." (KJV) — The incarnation is the anti-Gnostic anchor of orthodox Christianity.

• 1 John 4:2–3 — "Hereby know ye the Spirit of God: Every spirit that confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God: And every spirit that confesseth not that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is not of God..." (KJV)

• Colossians 2:8–10 — "Beware lest any man spoil you through philosophy and vain deceit, after the tradition of men, after the rudiments of the world, and not after Christ. For in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily. And ye are complete in him, which is the head of all principality and power:" (KJV) — Written against proto-Gnostic speculation in Colossae.

• 1 Timothy 6:20–21 — "O Timothy, keep that which is committed to thy trust, avoiding profane and vain babblings, and oppositions of science falsely so called: Which some professing have erred concerning the faith. Grace be with thee. Amen." (KJV) — "Science" here is gnōsis, knowledge.

• Genesis 1:31 — "And God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good. And the evening and the morning were the sixth day." (KJV) — The creation is good, not evil, which is the foundational refutation of Gnostic dualism.

• Ephesians 2:8–9 — "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast." (KJV) — Saved by grace through faith, not by nature and not by knowledge.

• 1 Corinthians 8:1 — "...Knowledge puffeth up, but charity edifieth." (KJV)

Modern Corruption

Gnosticism never died; it adapts and changes its clothes, and wherever a claimed knowledge offers a kind of salvation, it is back.

expand to see more

Modern forms include New Age religion, with its divine self within, spiritual evolution and secret energy knowledge; progressive Christianity’s turning of the bodily resurrection into a “spiritual” victory over death; the prosperity gospel’s contempt for suffering and weakness; the “ethnic Gnosticism” that claims special knowledge by ethnicity; miracle-centered teaching that treats signs as the proof of the faith; the conspiracy-theory habit that sells hidden knowledge to the unguarded; and the long Western fascination with hidden knowledge, from Freemasonry to the Nag Hammadi texts to Dan Brown’s Da Vinci Code.

The church has to keep saying what Gnosticism cannot stand: matter matters, the creation is good, the resurrection is bodily, and salvation is the redemption of the body and not escape from it. The paper also warns against the opposite mistake of calling anyone a Gnostic simply because he claims to know something after careful study; God has revealed truth in His Word, and sharing it is not Gnosticism. The mark to watch for is pride, knowledge that sets a man above other believers or offers him a salvation of his own, and the cure is humility, open study of the Scriptures, and the example of Christ the humble servant.

Greek & Hebrew Roots

G1108 — gnōsis (γνῶσις): knowledge, understanding. Used positively in Paul (knowing God, knowing the mystery of Christ) but the Gnostics appropriated it for their secret-elite framework. Contrast: epignōsis (G1922) — full, accurate knowledge — the kind Paul prays believers would have.

G1097 — ginōskō (γινώσκω): to know, to come to know. In John’s Gospel especially, knowing God is relational and saving, not esoteric or elite.

G4152 — pneumatikos (πνευματικός) and G5591 — psychikos (ψυχικός): “spiritual” and “natural.” The Valentinians took their class names from Paul (1 Corinthians 2:14–15), but Paul’s natural man is anyone without the Spirit of God, and his spiritual man is anyone the Spirit has given life, not a separate kind of human being.
