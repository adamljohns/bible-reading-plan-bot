Expiation
/ˌek.spiˈeɪ.ʃən/
noun
From Latin expiatio — a making good, an atoning; from expiare → ex- (out, away) + piare (to appease, to purify; from pius, dutiful, holy). Where propitiation focuses on the offended party (God's wrath turned aside), expiation focuses on the offending thing (sin removed, guilt cleansed). Both are present in the cross; the distinction helps clarify the mechanics of atonement.

📖 Biblical Definition

Expiation is the removal, cleansing, or cancellation of sin and its guilt. If propitiation asks "How is God's wrath turned aside?", expiation asks "How is the sinner's defilement removed?" The OT system included both: the blood covered the sin (propitiation) and the scapegoat carried the sin away (expiation, Leviticus 16:21–22). In the NT, Christ is the fulfillment: his blood both satisfies divine wrath (propitiation) and washes away guilt and defilement (expiation). Hebrews uses the language of purification and cleansing; 1 John speaks of the blood cleansing from all sin. The two concepts are inseparable: God's wrath is turned away precisely because sin has been genuinely and fully dealt with.

EXPIA'TION, n. The act of atoning for a crime; the act of making satisfaction for an offense or crime, by which guilt is done away and the obligation to punishment cancelled. Expiation is made for sin, when the penalty due to it is endured, or a satisfaction is offered and accepted in lieu of the penalty. By Christ's expiation our guilt is washed away and peace is restored between God and man.

📖 Key Scripture

• Leviticus 16:21–22 — The scapegoat carries the sins of Israel into the wilderness — expiation enacted as a physical picture.

• Hebrews 1:3 — "After making purification for sins, he sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high."

• 1 John 1:7 — "The blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin."

• Isaiah 1:18 — "Though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow."

• Psalm 51:2 — "Wash me thoroughly from my iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin!"

G2511 — katharizō (καθαρίζω): to cleanse, to purify; the dominant expiation-language in the NT.

G2512 — katharismos (καθαρισμός): cleansing, purification (Heb 1:3).

H2403 — chattath (חַטָּאת): sin offering — the sacrifice that dealt with the defilement of sin.

H5771 — avon (עָוֹן): iniquity, guilt — the weight that expiation removes.

Note: The LXX uses both ἱλάσκεσθαι (hilaskomai) and καθαρίζειν (katharizein) for atonement-language, showing that the two concepts overlap and were understood as complementary.

Some liberal theologians use "expiation" as a replacement for "propitiation" to strip the doctrine of its God-ward dimension — making the atonement about sin-removal only, not about turning away divine wrath. This move is theologically dishonest: the cross does both. Expiation without propitiation implies a God who is not wrathful against sin — a therapeutic deity who merely wants the mess cleaned up. The full biblical picture requires both: sin is removed (expiation) because God's righteous wrath has been satisfied (propitiation). To hold only expiation is to have half a cross.
