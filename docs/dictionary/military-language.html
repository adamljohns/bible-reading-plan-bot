Navy

WordOriginSources
BamboozleDisputedOf unknown origin, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1703, in the writing of the actor and playwright Colley Cibber, and also records a noun from the early 1700s. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) gives it a nautical sense from the days of sail, deceiving another ship by flying a false ensign, but does not claim that the word began at sea.oed.com · history.navy.mil
Binnacle ListAttestedFrom binnacle, the stand or housing that holds the ship’s compass, and list. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) says the list of men unable to report for duty was given to the officer of the watch and kept at the binnacle, or placed on it each morning so the captain knew the health of his crew. NHHC dates the practice to the eighteenth century and probably earlier.history.navy.mil · history.navy.mil
Bitter EndAttestedNautical. A bitt is a wooden or iron post set through a ship’s deck to make lines fast. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) explains that a turn of line around a bitt is called a bitter, so the last part of the line, secured to the bitts, is the bitter end. Sailors now use it for the end of any line, and landsmen took it up in “to the bitter end.”history.navy.mil
BumboatDisputedPerhaps a borrowing from Dutch bunboot, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1671, in a proclamation of Charles II on “Dirt-boats & Bum-boats.” The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) derives it from “boomboats,” because the craft were allowed to tie up to a ship’s boat boom, and mentions an early Low German spelling. The OED marks its own etymology “perhaps.”oed.com · history.navy.mil
Captain's MastAttestedNamed for the place of the ceremony. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) explains that in the days of sail the captain held it under the mainmast, on a regular basis and usually on a Sunday morning just before divine services, so it came to be called “mast.”history.navy.mil
Carry OnAttestedNautical. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) explains that in the days of sail, when a good breeze came, the officer of the deck ordered the crew to “carry on,” meaning to hoist every bit of canvas the yards could carry. Today the Bluejacket’s Manual defines it as an order to resume work.history.navy.mil
Charley NobleDisputedSailors’ name for the galley smokestack. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) says it is said to come from a British merchant captain, Charles Noble, who about 1850 found that his galley stack was copper and ordered it kept polished, so the crew called it the “Charley Noble.” NHHC gives this as a reported origin, not a documented one.history.navy.mil · history.navy.mil
Crow's NestDisputedA compound of crow and nest, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1604, in a translation by Edward Grimeston, with the earliest subject listed as military, then whaling and nautical from the 1810s. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) explains the name from crows carried aboard to find land, whose cages were kept at the masthead; the OED’s earlier military use is not explained by that story.oed.com · history.navy.mil
DogwatchDisputedOrigin uncertain. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) records two explanations: that dog is a corruption of dodge, because the short watches let men dodge standing the same watch every day, or that it refers to the fitful sleep called “dog sleep.” NHHC concludes that “no one really knows the origin of this term,” which was in use at least back to 1700.history.navy.mil · history.navy.mil
GundeckingDisputedNavy slang for falsifying reports or records. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) calls the origin “somewhat obscure” and gives two explanations: the false gundeck of British sailing warships, a deck that carried no guns and so misrepresented the ship’s armament; or midshipmen who went below to the gundeck and worked their navigation problems backwards from a dead-reckoning position instead of from real sights. NHHC thinks the second more plausible.history.navy.mil
HolystoneDisputedA soft sandstone used to scrub a ship’s decks. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) gives two explanations for the name: sailors had to kneel to use it, as if in prayer, and it was full of holes. The source does not decide between them.history.navy.mil
Jacob's LadderAttestedFrom the proper name Jacob and ladder, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1733, in a dictionary by the horticulturist Philip Miller, and lists the nautical sense from the 1840s. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) says the name alludes to the patriarch Jacob, who dreamed of a ladder reaching to heaven (Gen 28:12).oed.com · history.navy.mil
KeelhaulAttestedA borrowing from Dutch kielhalen, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1666, in the London Gazette. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) says the punishment was said to have originated with the Dutch and was adopted by other navies in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.oed.com · history.navy.mil · history.navy.mil
Know the RopesAttestedNautical. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) says the statement was first printed on a seaman’s discharge to show that he knew the names and main uses of the principal ropes aboard ship; in other words, that he was a novice who knew only the basics. Its meaning has since shifted to “experienced.”history.navy.mil
MidshipmanAttestedFormed within English as a compound of midship and man, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1652, in Severall Proceedings in Parliament. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) explains that the boys training as officers on British ships carried orders from the officers aft to the crew forward, through the middle of the ship.oed.com · history.navy.mil
Pea CoatDisputedThe Oxford English Dictionary gives it as a compound of English pea and coat, with its earliest evidence in 1790, in the Pennsylvania Packet. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) explains pea from “pilot cloth,” sometimes called P-cloth, which made a “p-jacket” and later a pea coat; NHHC dates the term to 1723, earlier than the OED’s first evidence. The two accounts differ.oed.com · history.navy.mil
PortholeAttestedA compound of port (n.3, an opening or gate) and hole, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1569, in a translation by Thomas Stocker, with early senses in gunnery and the navy. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) explains that the name has nothing to do with the port side: it comes from the French porte, a door, used for the gun ports cut in a ship’s side.oed.com · history.navy.mil
Sally ShipAttestedAn order and method of freeing a grounded ship. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) explains that the crew gathered along one side and ran together from port to starboard and back until the ship began to roll, which could break the suction of the mud.history.navy.mil
ScuttlebuttAttestedNaval, from scuttled butt: a butt is a cask, and a scuttled one has a hole cut in it. The ship’s drinking-water cask, with its opening for the dipper, was the scuttlebutt (recorded from the early 1800s), and sailors gathered there to drink and trade rumors, as office workers later did at the water cooler. The sense of rumor or gossip is recorded from the early 1900s and was universal in the Navy and Marine Corps by the Second World War.history.navy.mil · history.navy.mil
Sea ShantyDisputedShanty is probably a borrowing from French chantez, “sing!”, from chanter, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1856, in the writing of C. Nordhoff. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) gives the French chanter explanation and a second one, that the name refers to the shanties on the Mobile, Alabama, waterfront where sailors learned many of the tunes.oed.com · history.navy.mil
Sick BayAttestedA compound of sick and bay. The Oxford English Dictionary records its earliest evidence in 1813, in the writing of John Thomson, physician and surgeon. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) explains that sick berths on sailing ships were in the rounded stern, whose shape suggested a bay.oed.com · history.navy.mil
SideboysAttestedFrom tending the side. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) explains that in the days of sail, visitors, including officers coming to conferences or dinner on another ship, were often hoisted aboard in a boatswain’s chair, and the crewmen who did the hoisting at the side were the sideboys. NHHC calls it a custom inherited from the British Navy.history.navy.mil · history.navy.mil
SkylarkingDisputedThe Oxford English Dictionary treats the verb skylark as formed within English by conversion from the noun skylark, the bird, with its earliest evidence in 1771, in the Proceedings of the Old Bailey. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) gives a different account: that young sailors’ play high in the masts was first “skylacing,” from an old word lac, to play, later corrupted to skylarking. The two accounts do not agree.oed.com · history.navy.mil
Smoking LampAttestedA compound of smoking and lamp. The Oxford English Dictionary records its earliest evidence in 1889, in the Century Dictionary. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) says the exact date and origin of the lamp itself “has been lost,” but that it probably came into use in the sixteenth century, when seamen began smoking aboard ship.oed.com · history.navy.mil · history.navy.mil
Spinning a YarnAttestedNautical. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) explains that strict officers in the old Navy did not let sailors gather and tell stories, except on the one weekly job that required them to sit together: unravelling the strands of old line. That period became the time for “spinning yarns,” and later anyone telling a tale was said to be spinning a yarn.history.navy.mil
Toe the LineAttestedFrom the seams of a wooden ship’s deck. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) explains that the seams between deck planks, packed with oakum and sealed with pitch and tar, made parallel lines along the deck, and that sailors falling in at quarters were directed to stand with their toes just touching a seam; boys and student officers were also made to stand on a seam as a punishment.history.navy.mil
WardroomDisputedThe Oxford English Dictionary analyses it as a compound formed within English from ward (n.2) and room, with its earliest evidence in 1759, in the Annual Register 1758, under the subject “navy.” The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) tells a different story: that it was once the wardrobe, a compartment on eighteenth-century British ships for storing booty, where the officers took to eating when it was empty. The two accounts do not agree.oed.com · history.navy.mil

Civil War

WordOriginSources
BushwhackerAttestedAn English compound of bush + whacker, per the Oxford English Dictionary. OED’s earliest evidence is from 1809, in the writing of Washington Irving, well before the war, and OED records the military sense from the 1860s. The American Battlefield Trust notes that in Missouri and the other border states guerrilla fighters of either side were commonly called bushwhackers.oed.com · battlefields.org
DeadlineAttestedAn English compound of dead + line. The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for the word is from 1814, before the war. Its grim Civil War sense was the line in a military prison that a prisoner could not cross without being shot. The National Park Service notes that such deadlines were common in the open stockade prisons of the era, Andersonville among them, and not unique to it. The OED tags the word in fishing from the 1810s, in military use from the 1860s and in printing from the 1910s; the printing sense, the last moment copy could go to press, became today’s time limit.oed.com · nps.gov · npr.org
HardtackAttestedAn English compound of hard + tack (an old word for food), so “hard food.” The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence is from 1830, in the United Service Magazine. It became the everyday ration of the Civil War soldier: a cracker of flour, water and salt that kept for a very long time.oed.com · nps.gov
JayhawkerDisputedProbably a compound of jay + hawker, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which hedges the derivation. OED’s earliest evidence is from 1860, in the Ashtabula Sentinel. The American Battlefield Trust notes that the term originated in the prewar Bleeding Kansas period and was used for pro-Union partisan fighters.oed.com · battlefields.org
SkedaddleDisputedAmerican, first recorded in 1861 among Union soldiers in the opening year of the Civil War, used of troops retreating in a hurry; origin uncertain (fanciful explanations from Greek or Scots dialect are unproven). The word outlived the war as comic, mock-grand slang for leaving fast, and it was the grandparents’ word for it all through the twentieth century: "Now skedaddle!" to children underfoot in the 1930s, 1940s, and 1950s. The Silent Generation did not coin it, but they are the generation that handed it down to the rest of us.oed.com
