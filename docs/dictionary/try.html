Try
/trī/
verb
Middle English trien “to pick out, test, prove” | from Old French trier “to sift, select, separate the good from the bad” | ultimately related to the idea of testing or refining. The older sense was proving quality under pressure — not dabbling. Modern casual use has softened the word into a polite alibi for half-effort.

📖 Biblical / MOOP Definition

Try: to make an attempt without adequate preparation. In the MOOP Dictionary, try is not neutral courage — it is the soft word men use when they want credit for effort without the cost of readiness. Scripture does not honor vague trying; it honors counting the cost, preparing the field, and then doing the work. Jesus asked who builds a tower without first sitting down and calculating (Luke 14:28). The sluggard “tries” in the sense that he still talks about the path, but he never leaves the house (Proverbs 26:13). Faithful men do not mainly try; they prepare, then they act. Where Scripture uses the language of testing, it means proving and refining — silver in the fire, a heart examined by God — not dabbling and calling the dabble obedience.

📜 Webster 1828 Definition

TRY: to endeavor; to examine; to prove by experiment or test; to refine.

TRY, v.i. To exert strength; to endeavor; to make an effort; to attempt. Try to learn; try to lift a weight.

TRY, v.t. 1. To examine; to make experiment on; to prove by experiment. 2. To experience; to have knowledge by experience of. 3. To prove by a test; as, to try weights and measures by a standard; to try one’s opinions by the divine oracles. 4. To act upon as a test. 5. To examine judicially by witnesses and the principles of law. 6. To essay; to attempt. 7. To purify; to refine; as silver seven times tried. 8. To search carefully into. 9. To use as means; as, to try remedies for a disease.

Note: Webster’s dominant senses are testing, proving, refining, and examining — not soft half-effort. The modern spoken use of “I’ll try” often means “I am not committed and I have not prepared.” That is the corruption this entry names.

⚠️ Modern Corruption

“I’ll try” has become a socially acceptable way to avoid commitment, preparation, and ownership of results.

expand to see more

In modern speech, try often functions as a moral escape hatch. A man says “I’ll try” when he wants the appearance of effort without the burden of readiness. He did not sleep, train, plan, repent, budget, or seek counsel — but he still wants the credit of having tried. Culture celebrates participation trophies, process without product, and intention without integrity. In ministry and manhood language, this becomes deadly: men “try” purity without accountability, “try” marriage without pursuit, “try” leadership without presence. That is not biblical diligence. It is soft language covering an unprepared will. The opposite of unprepared trying is not perfectionism; it is preparation + action + ownership. Count the cost. Ready the field. Then do the work.

Usage

• “Don’t tell me you’ll try. Tell me what you prepared, what you will do, and by when.”

• “In this house, try means an attempt without adequate preparation — and that is not a badge of honor.”
