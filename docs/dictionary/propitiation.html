Propitiation
/prəˌpɪʃ.iˈeɪ.ʃən/
noun
From Latin propitiatio — appeasement, making favorable: propitius (favorable, gracious) + -atio (action noun). Greek: hilasmos (ἱλασμός) — that which appeases wrath; hilastērion (ἱλαστήριον) — the mercy seat, the place of propitiation. Not merely removing guilt (expiation) but satisfying the wrath of a holy God.

📖 Biblical Definition

Propitiation is the satisfaction of God's righteous wrath against sin through the sacrificial death of Christ. It is the most precise term for what the cross accomplishes toward God — not merely removing the record of sin (expiation) but actually turning away divine wrath. God is not simply a bureaucrat crossing debts off a ledger; He is a holy Judge whose moral nature is offended by sin. Propitiation means that Christ, as our representative, bore the full weight of that offended holiness so that God is now "just and the justifier" of the one who has faith in Jesus (Rom 3:26). Without propitiation, forgiveness is arbitrary — God merely looks the other way. With it, forgiveness is grounded in justice: the penalty has been paid, wrath has been absorbed, and the way to God is open.

PROPITIATION, n. The act of appeasing wrath and conciliating the favor of an offended person; the act of making propitious. 2. In theology, the atonement or atoning sacrifice; the sacrifice which Christ offered to the Father to expiate sin and render God propitious to sinners. Christ is the propitiation for our sins. 1 John 2:2.

PROPITIATORY, a. Having the power to make propitious; expiatory; as a propitiatory sacrifice.

📖 Key Scripture

• 1 John 2:2 — "He is the propitiation for our sins, and not for ours only but also for the sins of the whole world."

• Romans 3:25 — "God put forward [Christ] as a propitiation by his blood, to be received by faith."

• 1 John 4:10 — "In this is love, not that we have loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins."

• Hebrews 2:17 — "Therefore he had to be made like his brothers… to make propitiation for the sins of the people."

• Romans 5:9 — "Since, therefore, we have now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God."

Key distinction:
  Expiation  = removes the stain of sin (sin-ward action)
  Propitiation = turns away the wrath of God (God-ward action)
  Both happen at the cross; propitiation is the fuller term.

C.H. Dodd (1935) argued that hilasmos should be translated "expiation" (removing sin's stain) not "propitiation" (averting divine wrath) — claiming a personal, wrathful God is sub-biblical. This translation choice cascaded through 20th-century scholarship and shows up in the RSV's translation of 1 John 2:2 as "expiation." Leon Morris devastatingly refuted Dodd — the wrath of God is everywhere in Paul, and the sacrifice clearly has a God-ward direction. Modern theology is embarrassed by the idea that God has wrath. But a God without wrath is a God without holiness — and such a God needs no cross. The cross is only necessary because God is holy enough to be wrathful and loving enough to absorb that wrath Himself.
