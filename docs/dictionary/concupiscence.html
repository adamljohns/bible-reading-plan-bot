Concupiscence
/kɒnˈkjuː.pɪ.səns/
noun
From Latin concupiscentia — eager desire, strong longing; from concupiscere (to covet earnestly) → con- (intensive) + cupere (to desire, to long for). Related to Cupid (desire personified). Greek: epithumia (ἐπιθυμία) — strong desire, craving. In KJV, used to translate the disordered, sinful longing of the fallen nature.

📖 Biblical Definition

Concupiscence is the disordered desire that remains in fallen human nature — the persistent inner inclination toward sin that war against the soul even in the regenerate. It is not merely the act of sinning but the bent — the gravitational pull of the fallen will toward what is forbidden. The Reformers located concupiscence at the heart of original sin: Adam's fall corrupted not just behavior but the very orientation of human desire, turning it inward upon the self (cor incurvatum in se) and away from God. Paul describes it as "the law in my members waging war against the law of my mind" (Rom 7:23). It is the battleground of sanctification — the reason mortification is lifelong work, not a one-time conquest.

CONCU'PISCENCE, n. [L. concupiscentia, from concupisco, to covet; con and cupio, to desire.] Lust; unlawful or irregular desire of sexual pleasure. In a more general sense, the coveting of carnal things, or an irregular appetite for worldly good; inclination for unlawful enjoyments. "Let not sin reign in your mortal body, that ye should obey it in the lusts thereof." The word is used in Romans 7:8 (KJV): "For without the law sin was dead… sin, taking occasion by the commandment, wrought in me all manner of concupiscence."

📖 Key Scripture

• Romans 7:7–8 — "I would not have known what it is to covet if the law had not said, 'You shall not covet.' But sin, seizing an opportunity through the commandment, produced in me all kinds of covetousness (concupiscence)."

• Romans 7:23 — "I see in my members another law waging war against the law of my mind and making me captive to the law of sin that dwells in my members."

• James 1:14–15 — "Each person is tempted when he is lured and enticed by his own desire. Then desire when it has conceived gives birth to sin, and sin when it is fully grown brings forth death."

• Galatians 5:17 — "The desires of the flesh are against the Spirit, and the desires of the Spirit are against the flesh, for these are opposed to each other."

• 1 Peter 2:11 — "Abstain from the passions of the flesh, which wage war against your soul."

G1939 — epithumia (ἐπιθυμία): strong desire, craving, lust. Can be used positively (Luke 22:15) but predominantly refers to sinful desire in Paul's letters. Root of the English word "epithumia."

G1937 — epithumeō (ἐπιθυμέω): to desire strongly, to lust after. The verb from which epithumia derives.

H2530 — chamad (חָמַד): to desire, to covet; the word used in the 10th Commandment ("You shall not covet"). The disordered desire condemned in Ex 20:17.

H8307 — sherirut (שְׁרִירוּת): stubbornness, imaginations of the heart; the bent of the fallen will (Jer 3:17).

Modern psychology has reframed concupiscence as "natural desire" — neutral, even healthy when properly channeled. The sexual revolution went further: disordered desire is now called authentic self-expression, and resisting it is labeled repression. Contemporary Christianity has largely absorbed this framing, reducing sin to harmful behavior rather than disordered desire. The result: generations of Christians who confess actions but never confront the underlying lusts — who manage sin rather than mortify it. The Reformers understood that concupiscence itself (not just its expression) is sinful — it is the corruption at the root, not just the fruit. Naming it honestly is the first act of war.

Latin: concupiscentia → concupiscere
  → con- (intensive prefix: thoroughly, completely)
  → cupere (to desire, to wish for)
  → PIE root *kup- (to boil, to burn with desire)
  Related: Cupid (Roman god of desire), cupidity (greed)

Greek: ἐπιθυμία (epithumia)
  → ἐπί (epi) = upon, against
  → θυμός (thumos) = passion, life-force, soul-impulse
  Root image: desire pressing upon and overwhelming the soul

Hebrew: חָמַד (chamad) = to desire, to take pleasure in
  — appears in the 10th Commandment (Ex 20:17)
  — same root used of Eve seeing the fruit "desirable" (Gen 3:6)
