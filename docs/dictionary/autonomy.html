Autonomy
/ɔːˈtɒn.ə.mi/
noun
From Greek autonomia (independence, freedom to live by one's own laws), from autos (self) + nomos (law, custom). Literally: self-law. Originally applied to city-states governing themselves rather than being ruled by an external power. Its modern philosophical use — the individual as the ultimate source of moral authority — is a direct product of Enlightenment rationalism.

📖 Biblical Definition

Scripture affirms human agency and responsibility — God made man a choosing being, not a puppet. But Scripture never affirms human autonomy in the philosophical sense: the right to be a law unto oneself. "There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way to death" (Proverbs 14:12). The period of the Judges, when "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25), is presented not as a golden age of freedom but as a time of moral chaos and civilizational collapse. True freedom in Scripture is not self-rule but submission to God's rule: "If the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed" (John 8:36). Biblical freedom is not autonomy — it is theonomy: living under God's good law.

📜 Webster 1828 Definition

The power or right of self-government.

expand to see more

AUTON'OMY, n. [Gr. autos, self, and nomos, law.] The power or right of self-government. Applied to states and political bodies. Note: Webster applied autonomy to political self-governance of communities and nations — not to the individual's supposed right to define morality for himself. The modern application of autonomy to personal ethics and identity would have been recognized as the philosophical foundation of anarchy.

⚠️ Modern Corruption

Autonomy has become the supreme Western value — the individual as god of his own moral universe.

expand to see more

Modern Western culture has made autonomy its highest virtue — the non-negotiable right of every individual to define truth, morality, identity, and purpose for themselves. "My body, my choice." "My truth." "You do you." These are not casual slogans — they are creedal statements of the religion of self. This is the fulfillment of the serpent's original promise: "You will be like God, knowing good and evil" (Genesis 3:5) — that is, you will be the one who decides what is good and evil for yourself. Autonomy in this sense is not freedom; it is the original sin given a philosophical framework. Every biblical category — submission, obedience, authority, law, covenant — is an offense to the autonomous self. And that is precisely the point. The Gospel is not an invitation to self-actualization; it is a call to die to self and live to Christ. "I have been crucified with Christ. It is no longer I who live" (Galatians 2:20).
