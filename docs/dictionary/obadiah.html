Obadiah

/ˌoʊbəˈdaɪə/
proper noun / prophet

Etymology & Webster 1828

Hebrew Ovadyah, "servant of Yahweh." The shortest book in the Old Testament — 21 verses, a single chapter — and one of the most fiery. Date is debated: either just after the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem (586 BC, the majority view), or possibly during an earlier Edomite attack. The oracle is aimed entirely at Edom — the nation descended from Esau, Jacob's twin — for gloating over Jerusalem's fall, joining the plunder, and cutting off Jewish refugees trying to escape.

Biblical Meaning

Obadiah is the final chapter of the Jacob-and-Esau saga that began in Genesis 25. The feud between the twins became a feud between nations. When Babylon sacked Jerusalem, Edom rejoiced and joined in. Obadiah's indictment is precise: "On the day that you stood aloof, on the day that strangers carried off his wealth and foreigners entered his gates and cast lots for Jerusalem, you were like one of them... do not gloat over the day of your brother... do not stand at the crossroads to cut off his fugitives" (vv. 11-14). The judgment: "As you have done, it shall be done to you" (v. 15) — the lex talionis of divine justice. Edom was conquered by Nabonidus in the sixth century BC and finally erased from history by the Nabateans. The "kingdom shall be the LORD's" (v. 21) is the climactic line — all human kingdoms, including Edom's pride, yield to the universal reign of Yahweh. Modern application: God notices when you take satisfaction in a brother's downfall. Schadenfreude is a specific sin with specific consequences. Do not gloat — even over enemies — because it is the heart of Edom.

Key Scriptures

"For the day of the LORD is near upon all the nations. As you have done, it shall be done to you; your deeds shall return on your own head."— Obadiah 15
"Do not gloat over the day of your brother in the day of his misfortune."— Obadiah 12
"Saviors shall go up to Mount Zion to rule Mount Esau, and the kingdom shall be the LORD's."— Obadiah 21
