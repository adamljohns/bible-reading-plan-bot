Synderesis
/ˌsɪn.dəˈriː.sɪs/
noun (theological / philosophical)
From medieval Latin synderesis (also synteresis), likely derived from Greek συντήρησις (syntērēsis) — a preserving, a guarding. Possibly a scribal corruption of συνείδησις (syneidēsis — conscience). The term entered Western theology through Jerome's commentary on Ezekiel, where he described a "spark of conscience" (scintilla conscientiae) that remains even in the fallen soul — an inextinguishable inclination toward the good that sin can suppress but never fully destroy.

📖 Biblical Definition

The innate, indelible moral orientation of the human soul toward the good — the foundational habit of practical reason that inclines every person, even in a fallen state, to recognize that good is to be pursued and evil avoided. Synderesis is not the conscience itself (which can err and be corrupted) but the deeper principle beneath conscience: the moral bedrock that makes conscience possible.

Scripture testifies that God has written the work of His law on every human heart (Rom. 2:14–15). Even the pagan, who has never heard the commandments, possesses an internal witness — a moral compass that, however damaged by sin, still points true north. This is synderesis: the image of God in its moral dimension, scarred but not obliterated by the Fall, which is why even the most hardened sinner still experiences guilt, why tyrannies must construct elaborate justifications for their cruelty, and why no human culture in history has praised cowardice or celebrated treachery as virtues.

📜 Webster 1828 Definition

Not included in Webster 1828. The concept was primarily a scholastic theological term, central to Thomas Aquinas and the medieval moral tradition. Aquinas distinguished synderesis (the innate habit inclining toward good) from conscientia (the act of applying moral principles to particular cases). Synderesis provides the major premise — "do good, avoid evil" — while conscience draws the practical conclusion in specific situations. Synderesis cannot err; conscience can.

⚠️ Modern Corruption

Modern moral philosophy has largely abandoned the concept of synderesis, replacing it with either moral relativism ("morality is culturally constructed") or emotivism ("moral statements are merely expressions of preference"). The consequence is catastrophic: if there is no innate moral orientation, then no moral claim has any more authority than any other, and ethics becomes mere power negotiation. Meanwhile, the existentialists proclaimed that existence precedes essence — that humans have no nature, no built-in moral compass, and must create their own values from scratch. This directly contradicts the biblical teaching that humans are created with an intrinsic orientation toward the good (however corrupted), and that moral knowledge is not invented but discovered — or rather, remembered — from the law written on the heart.

✍️ Usage

Synderesis explains why no one is truly an atheist at the deepest level of their moral reasoning. The person who insists morality is relative still recoils at the torture of children. The person who denies objective truth still protests when lied to. The moral law written on the heart persists beneath every philosophical denial — and this persistence is the fingerprint of the Creator.

Jerome called it the "spark of conscience" — scintilla conscientiae — that "was not extinguished even in the breast of Cain." However far humanity falls, this spark remains, testifying to the image of God that sin has marred but cannot erase.

The recovery of synderesis as a concept would heal much of modern moral confusion: there is a moral order, it is built into human nature by God, and our task is not to construct values but to align ourselves with the ones already inscribed on our hearts.
