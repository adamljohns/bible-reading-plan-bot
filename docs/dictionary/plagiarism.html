Plagiarism
/PLAY-juh-riz-um/
noun (literary theft; the eighth commandment applied to words)
Latin plagium, kidnapping; plagiarius, a kidnapper — later a thief of another man’s writings. Webster 1828 names the crime plainly: literary theft.

Biblical Definition

Plagiarism is the taking of another man’s words, thoughts, or work and offering them as one’s own. Scripture never uses the later English noun, but it names the act. The eighth commandment — “Thou shalt not steal” (Exodus 20:15) — is not limited to cattle and coin. A man’s labor includes what he writes, preaches, and makes. To strip the name from the work and wear it yourself is theft of credit, which is theft of the man’s due. The Lord is explicit against the prophets who did this with His own speech: “Therefore, behold, I am against the prophets, saith the LORD, that steal my words every one from his neighbour” (Jeremiah 23:30). Stolen words are still stolen, even when the original Author is God.

The ninth commandment stands beside the eighth. Claiming authorship you do not have is a false witness about origin. Paul’s cure for the thief is the same here as for any other taking: stop taking, start working, and work in order to give (Ephesians 4:28). Honest dealing renders to each what is due (Romans 13:7) — including the name on the sentence. What hast thou that thou didst not receive? (1 Corinthians 4:7). The honest man cites, attributes, and builds; the plagiarist kidnaps.

Webster 1828 Definition

Literary theft: purloining another man’s writings and putting them off as one’s own. The older noun plagiary is a thief in literature, from Latin kidnapping.

PLA'GIARISM, noun [from plagiary.] The act of purloining another man’s literary works, or introducing passages from another man’s writings and putting them off as one’s own; literary theft.

Modern Corruption

The crime is renamed inspiration, remix, or “everyone does it,” while citation is treated as academic manners instead of honesty. The mirror error treats every uncited common knowledge as theft.

expand to see more

Modern culture often dissolves authorship. “Everything is a remix” becomes a license to take without naming. Uncredited AI paste, lifted sermons, stolen posts, and “I just made it my own” are the same old kidnapping with better lighting. The test is not whether the words moved you. The test is whether another man’s labor is wearing your name. If you would be ashamed to put his name beside yours in public, you already know what you did.

The opposite error is pedantry that calls every proverb, every public-domain phrase, and every shared doctrine “plagiarism” unless a footnote is attached. Scripture itself quotes, alludes, and repeats without modern citation apparatus — and it still condemns the prophet who steals his neighbor’s words and the man who withholds what is due. Honesty names sources when a particular man’s work is being used. It does not pretend that truth itself is private property. Common knowledge is not kidnapping. Passing off a brother’s paragraph as your insight is.

Greek & Hebrew Roots

No single biblical lemma equals the later English noun. The act sits under Hebrew ganab (to steal) and Greek klepto (to steal), with Jeremiah 23:30 as the plain prophetic case: stealing words.

expand to see more

Latin plagium — kidnapping; plagiarius — kidnapper, later a literary thief. English inherited the metaphor: words stolen as persons once were.

Hebrew ganab (H1589) — to steal, take by stealth; the verb of the eighth commandment, and the verb behind Jeremiah 23:30’s “steal my words.”

Greek klepto (G2813) / kleptes (G2812) — to steal / a thief; the New Testament shape of the same taking.

The ninth commandment (false witness) covers the lie about origin: this work is mine when it is not.
