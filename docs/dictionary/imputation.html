Imputation
/ˌim-pyü-ˈtā-shən/
noun
Latin imputatio, from imputare — to reckon, charge to one's account; in- (in, into) + putare (to reckon, count, calculate). Greek logizomai (λογίζομαι) — to reckon, credit, count as.

📖 Biblical Definition

Imputation is the legal act by which God credits righteousness or guilt to a person's account — not based on what they have done, but on the standing of another with whom they are united. Scripture speaks of three great imputations: (1) Adam's sin imputed to all humanity (Rom. 5:12–19); (2) humanity's sin imputed to Christ on the cross (2 Cor. 5:21; Isa. 53:6); (3) Christ's righteousness imputed to all who believe (Phil. 3:9; Rom. 4:22–24). Imputation is the mechanism of both the Fall and the Gospel. It is the great exchange: our sin to Christ, His righteousness to us. Without imputation, justification is impossible — there would be no basis on which a holy God could declare a sinner righteous.

📜 Webster 1828 Definition

The act of imputing; the charging of something to a person's account; in theology, the charging of Adam's sin to his posterity, and the charging of the sins of believers to Christ, and of Christ's righteousness to believers. "The imputation of Christ's righteousness is the ground of the sinner's justification."

⚠️ Modern Corruption

Modern theology, particularly New Perspective on Paul and progressive theologies, rejects forensic imputation as a "legal fiction" — arguing that righteousness must be something we grow into rather than something reckoned to us. This trades the Reformation's sola fide for a covenant nomism that makes human obedience a condition of standing before God. When imputation is denied, the assurance of salvation collapses: believers must look inward to their own righteousness rather than upward to Christ's. The entire Reformation debate (sola fide vs. infused righteousness) hinges on whether righteousness is imputed or imparted as the basis of justification.
