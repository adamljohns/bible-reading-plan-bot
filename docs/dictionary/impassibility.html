Impassibility
/ɪmˌpæs.əˈbɪl.ɪ.ti/
noun (theological attribute)
From Medieval Latin impassibilitas; from in- (not) + passibilis (capable of suffering, from pati — to suffer, endure). The doctrine that God cannot be acted upon or caused to suffer by something external to himself; that his emotional and volitional states are not reactive in the way creaturely emotions are. Not to be confused with apathy (indifference) — God is fully alive and deeply caring; impassibility concerns the mode and source of his internal states.

📖 Biblical Definition

Divine impassibility is the classical theological teaching that God does not undergo involuntary emotional changes caused by creatures or external forces. He is not subject to passions in the way fallen humans are — he is not manipulated, destabilized, or coerced by suffering or events. This is rooted in God's aseity (self-existence), immutability (unchangeableness), and perfection. God's love, wrath, grief, and delight are real — but they flow from his unchanging nature and sovereign will, not as reactions to stimuli he did not determine. Classically affirmed in the Westminster Confession (2.1): God is "without body, parts, or passions." The challenge of impassibility is holding it alongside the genuine emotional language of Scripture — God repents (Gen 6:6), grieves, is angry, delights. Orthodox theology handles this through the distinction between anthropopathism (human-emotion language applied to God to communicate real truths) and creaturely emotionalism.

IMPASSIBILITY, n. The quality of being incapable of pain, suffering or impression from external agents. Applied to the divine nature, it signifies that God cannot be caused to suffer, cannot be acted upon or changed by any external force or creature. It does not imply indifference or lack of feeling, but rather that God's being is not subject to diminishment or involuntary alteration.

📖 Key Scripture

• Malachi 3:6 — "I the LORD do not change." — The foundation of impassibility: God's being does not undergo external modification.

• Numbers 23:19 — "God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind." — Impassibility distinguishes God from creaturely instability.

• James 1:17 — "With whom there is no variation or shadow due to change."

• Psalm 102:26–27 — "They will perish, but you will remain…you are the same, and your years have no end."

• Genesis 6:6 — "The LORD regretted that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him to his heart." — Anthropopathism: God communicates real relational reality in creaturely terms.

Open Theism rejects impassibility entirely, claiming that God genuinely suffers with creatures, changes his mind in response to prayer, and does not exhaustively foreknow the future. Process Theology (Whitehead, Hartshorne) goes further: God is literally co-dependent with creation, growing and developing alongside it. Both views are theological capitulations to creaturely concepts of personhood — importing emotional reactivity as a divine virtue. The motive is pastoral (a "relatable" God who really suffers with us), but the result is a God who can be destabilized by events — which raises terrifying questions about ultimate sovereignty. The classical answer: God's love is more secure precisely because it is not reactive. He doesn't love you because you moved him; he loves you because of who he is.

Latin: impassibilitas → passibilis → pati (to suffer, undergo)
  → Proto-Indo-European *peh₁- (to protect, feed) → passio (passion, suffering)
  
Greek equivalent: ἀπάθεια (apatheia) — without passion/suffering
  → ἀ- (not) + πάθος (pathos) — suffering, experience
  Note: Greek apatheia ≠ modern "apathy" (cold indifference)
  Classical apatheia = freedom from being controlled by external forces

Theological trajectory:
  Aristotle: "Unmoved Mover" (God moves all without being moved)
  Patristics: Adopted impassibility to guard against Greek mythology
    (where gods had uncontrolled passions like humans)
  Westminster Confession (1647): "without body, parts, or passions"
  20th c. challenges: Moltmann (The Crucified God), Open Theism
