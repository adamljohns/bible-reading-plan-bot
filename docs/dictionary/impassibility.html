Impassibility
/ ɪmˌpæs·ɪˈbɪl·ɪ·ti /
noun (theological term)
Latin impassibilitas — from in- (not) + passibilis (capable of suffering or being acted upon; from patior, to suffer, to undergo). Literally: "not able to be acted upon from outside." Divine impassibility is the classical doctrine that God does not have passions in the creaturely sense — He is not subject to emotional perturbations, involuntary reactions, or suffering imposed upon Him by external forces. He acts; He is not acted upon. This does not mean God is unfeeling — it means His love, wrath, and grief are not reactions but expressions of His eternal, unchanging nature.

📖 Biblical Definition

Divine impassibility is one of the most contested and misunderstood attributes of God in contemporary theology. The classical position — held by virtually all the church Fathers and Reformers — is that God does not have passions in the sense that creatures do. Creaturely passions are involuntary, reactive, and subject to change: we are surprised by grief, overwhelmed by fear, provoked by anger. These states are imposed on us from outside and alter our condition. God, as the self-existent, immutable, sovereign Lord, is not subject to this kind of passional experience. He cannot be surprised, manipulated, emotionally destabilized, or made to suffer against His will.

What impassibility does NOT mean: it does not mean God is cold, indifferent, or Stoic. Scripture is saturated with divine "emotion" language — God's love (John 3:16), wrath (Rom. 1:18), jealousy (Ex. 20:5), grief (Gen. 6:6), delight (Ps. 149:4). The orthodox position reconciles this by distinguishing between: (1) anthropopathic language — Scripture's accommodation to human understanding by describing God's responses in emotional terms; and (2) the eternal divine will and nature from which those responses flow. God's love is not a feeling that comes and goes — it is an eternal attribute of His being. His wrath is not an emotional reaction — it is the necessary expression of His holiness encountering sin. These are real, not merely metaphorical — but they are not creaturely passions.

The Incarnation adds a critical dimension: the Son of God, in taking on human nature, became capable of suffering. He wept (John 11:35), grieved (Matt. 26:38), and cried out from the cross (Matt. 27:46). This is the suffering of the God-Man in His human nature — not a demonstration that the divine nature is passible, but a demonstration of the depth of the kenosis. The impassible God became passible in Christ's human nature — for us and our salvation.

📜 Webster 1828 Definition

IMPASSIBLE, adj.

expand to see more

IMPASSIBLE, adj. [Lat. impassibilis; Fr. impassible.] Incapable of suffering; not to be moved by passion; exempt from passion, pain or suffering. In theology, impassibility is attributed to God as a divine perfection, denoting that He is not subject to suffering, sorrow, or passion as creatures are — not acted upon from without — His blessedness being absolute, complete, and independent of all external circumstances. Distinguished from apathy, which in its vulgar sense implies indifference; the divine impassibility is not coldness but transcendence.

📖 Key Scripture

Numbers 23:19 — "God is not man, that He should lie, or a son of man, that He should change His mind." — God does not undergo the involuntary changes characteristic of creaturely emotional life.

Malachi 3:6 — "For I the LORD do not change; therefore you, O children of Jacob, are not consumed." — Immutability grounds covenant faithfulness.

James 1:17 — "Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change."

Psalm 102:27 — "But you are the same, and your years have no end."

Isaiah 46:10 — "My counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all my purpose." — The impassible God whose will is not subject to external interference.

⚠️ Modern Corruption

Modern theology has largely abandoned divine impassibility in favor of a "suffering God" who is genuinely, ontologically affected by creaturely pain. Jürgen Moltmann's The Crucified God argues that God genuinely suffered in the cross in His divine nature — that the cross changes God. Process theology goes further: God is in a constant state of "becoming," affected and enriched by creaturely experience. Open Theism posits a God who genuinely grieves, risks, and is emotionally invested in creaturely outcomes in ways that alter His state. The appeal is pastoral — a God who suffers with us seems more relatable. But the cost is catastrophic: a God who can suffer can be harmed; a God who can be harmed can be threatened; a God who can be threatened is not sovereign. The biblical God of impassibility is not cold — He is the rock, the anchor, the immovable foundation. His love does not fluctuate with our failures. His peace is not disrupted by our chaos. This is the God worth trusting.

H5162 — נָחַם (nacham) — to be sorry, to have compassion, to repent/relent; used in Gen. 6:6 of God being "grieved." Anthropopathic language — the word describes God's response in human-relatable terms, not a literal creaturely emotional state.

G3958 — πάσχω (paschō) — to suffer, to undergo, to experience; the verbal root of "passion" and "impassibility." In Christ's human nature, He suffered (1 Pet. 2:21); divine impassibility concerns the divine nature specifically.

G665 / ἀπαράλλακτος — unchanging; James 1:17 describes God as having "no variation" (parallage — shifting) — a key text for divine immutability underlying impassibility.
