Social Gospel
/ˈsoʊ.ʃəl ˈɡɒs.pəl/
noun phrase
A late 19th/early 20th-century Protestant movement associated with Walter Rauschenbusch that applied Christian ethics to social problems — poverty, inequality, labor rights — while simultaneously abandoning the supernatural content of the gospel: the deity of Christ, substitutionary atonement, bodily resurrection, and personal salvation from sin.

Biblical Definition

The biblical gospel is the good news that God saves sinners from the wrath to come through the death and resurrection of Jesus Christ. It is "the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes" (Romans 1:16). This gospel produces social fruit — transformed lives, compassion for the poor, justice, mercy, and love of neighbor. But the fruit is not the root. The social gospel inverts this: it makes social reform the substance of salvation and discards the supernatural gospel as outdated mythology. Christians are absolutely called to love their neighbors, care for the poor, and seek justice — but these flow from the gospel, they do not replace it. A "gospel" that feeds the body while abandoning the soul is no gospel at all.

Webster 1828 Definition

GOSPEL: The history of Jesus Christ's birth, life, actions, death, resurrection, ascension, and the doctrines taught by Christ and His apostles.

GOS'PEL, n. [Sax. godspel; god, good, and spel, history, relation, word, speech.] 1. The history of the birth, life, actions, death, resurrection and ascension of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. 2. The scheme of salvation as taught by Christ and his apostles. Note: Webster defined the gospel as the saving work of Christ — the social gospel movement would later strip these supernatural elements and reduce the gospel to a social ethic.

• 1 Corinthians 15:3-4 — "Christ died for our sins... He was buried... He was raised on the third day."

• Galatians 1:8 — "Even if we or an angel from heaven should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed."

Modern Corruption

The social gospel is the most successful substitution of a counterfeit gospel in modern church history.

expand to see more

The social gospel replaced salvation from sin with salvation from social ills. It traded personal regeneration for structural reform, the cross for the ballot box, and eternal life for temporal improvement. Its legacy continues in liberation theology, progressive Christianity, and the modern "justice" movement that makes racial equity, climate activism, and economic redistribution the mission of the church — while the actual gospel of Christ crucified and risen is treated as secondary or embarrassing. The tragedy is that the social gospel produced neither lasting social improvement nor personal salvation. The churches that embraced it are now empty, while the communities they claimed to serve remain in need of the one thing the social gospel refused to offer: the power of God unto salvation through faith in the crucified and risen Christ.

Usage

• "The social gospel traded the power of God for salvation for the power of government for reform — and lost both."

• "Christians must care for the poor and seek justice — but the moment social action replaces the proclamation of Christ crucified, you have the social gospel, not the gospel."
