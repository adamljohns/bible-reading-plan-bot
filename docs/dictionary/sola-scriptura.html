Sola Scriptura
/ ˈsō·lä ˈskrip·too·rä /
Latin phrase · theological principle
Latin: sola (alone, only) + scriptura (writing, Scripture; from scribere, to write). Literally: "by Scripture alone." One of the five Solas of the Protestant Reformation — the formal principle of the Reformation as opposed to the material principle (sola fide). The phrase was crystallized in the 16th-century Reformation, though the underlying principle traces to the patristic insistence that Scripture is the norming norm (norma normans) by which all other authorities are normed.

📖 Biblical Definition

Sola Scriptura is the conviction that the sixty-six books of the Old and New Testaments constitute the final, sufficient, and supreme authority for Christian faith and practice — that no council, pope, tradition, personal prophecy, or human reason holds equal or greater authority over the church's doctrine and life. It does not claim that Scripture is the only authority (that would be nuda scriptura — naked Scripture, the error of dismissing all tradition and scholarship), but that Scripture is the final authority, the court of last appeal, the standard by which all other authorities are tested and, if necessary, corrected. The principle rests on Scripture's own testimony: theopneustos (God-breathed, 2 Timothy 3:16), living and active (Hebrews 4:12), and sufficient to make the man of God complete, equipped for every good work (2 Timothy 3:17). When Luther stood before the Diet of Worms in 1521 and declared, "My conscience is captive to the Word of God...here I stand, I can do no other," he was not claiming private judgment as the supreme authority — he was claiming that Scripture, rightly interpreted, stands above councils, traditions, and papal declarations. This was not novelty; it was recovery. The Bereans tested apostolic preaching against Scripture and were commended for it (Acts 17:11). Jesus himself rebuked tradition that contradicted Scripture (Mark 7:9–13). The Reformation did not invent sola Scriptura — it recovered it from under centuries of accumulated tradition.

📜 Webster 1828 Definition

SCRIPTURE — In general, a writing; any written composition. But appropriately, and by way of eminence, the sacred writings of the Old and New Testament. "All Scripture is given by inspiration of God" (2 Timothy 3:16). Scriptura sola — the doctrine that the Holy Scriptures are the only and sufficient rule of faith and practice for Christians; the formal principle of the Reformation; the distinguishing mark of Protestant Christianity against Roman Catholicism's appeal to Scripture and Tradition as co-equal sources of authority.

⚠️ Modern Corruption

Two distortions dominate today. The first is nuda scriptura — the hyper-individualist position that every believer reads Scripture fresh, without reference to creeds, confessions, or the wisdom of the church through the ages. This produces both humility (individual interpretations are regularly wrong) and chaos (every man is his own magisterium). Sola Scriptura was never meant to isolate the individual reader from the community of interpretation — it meant the church must bow to Scripture, not that every personal reading is automatically correct. The second corruption is the functional abandonment of sola Scriptura in practice: churches that claim the principle but treat pastoral authority, therapeutic frameworks, political ideologies, or personal prophecies as having equal or greater practical authority over daily discipleship. The question is not what you confess about Scripture; it is what you actually submit to when Scripture contradicts your preferences. That is where the doctrine is tested.

✍️ Usage

• Sola Scriptura does not mean "the Bible only and nothing else matters" — it means the Bible is the supreme norm. Tradition, reason, and experience are real but subordinate authorities. Tradition informs; Scripture judges.

• The five Solas stand together: Sola Scriptura (the authority), Sola Fide (the instrument), Sola Gratia (the cause), Solus Christus (the mediator), Soli Deo Gloria (the end).

• Practically: when your counselor, your culture, your gut, and Scripture disagree — the test of whether you actually hold sola Scriptura is which one wins.
