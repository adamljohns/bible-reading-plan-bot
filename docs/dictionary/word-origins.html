Word Origins

The histories carried in the words we use.

Bible

WordOriginSources
ArmageddonAttestedGreek Harmagedon (Ἁρμαγεδών), from Hebrew Har Megiddo — "Mountain of Megiddo." The site where, according to Revelation 16:16, the kings of the earth will gather for the final battle against God Almighty.oed.com
BabelAttestedFrom Hebrew Babel (בָּבֶל) — "confusion, mixing"; the name given by God to the city where He confused the languages (Genesis 11:9). The Babylonians called their city Bab-ilu — "Gate of God" or "Gate of the gods" — a grandiose self-designation that Scripture ironically reframes as "confusion." English babble is often said to come from Babel, but the Oxford English Dictionary records babble before 1250 as a word formed within English; it is babel, a confused noise or scene, first recorded before 1529, that comes from the city’s name.oed.com · oed.com
BedlamAttestedFrom the proper name Bedlem (Oxford English Dictionary), the Middle English form of Bethlehem, used of the Hospital of Saint Mary of Bethlehem, a London institution for the mentally ill (American Heritage Dictionary). The OED’s earliest evidence for the word is from 1418.oed.com · ahdictionary.com
Behemoth (the English word)AttestedA borrowing from Hebrew bĕhēmōṯ (Oxford English Dictionary), which the American Heritage Dictionary gives as the plural of bĕhēmâ, beast. The OED’s earliest evidence for the word is from before 1382, in the Wycliffite Bible. The American Heritage Dictionary gives the general sense first: something enormous in size or power.oed.com · ahdictionary.com
Doubting ThomasAttestedAn English compound of doubting + the proper name Thomas (Oxford English Dictionary), after the apostle Thomas, who would not believe Christ was risen until he was shown proof (John 20:24–29; American Heritage Dictionary). The OED’s earliest evidence is from 1671, in the writing of William Annand, dean of Edinburgh.oed.com · ahdictionary.com
Goliath (the English word)AttestedFrom the proper name Goliath, the Philistine champion of Gath (1Sa 17:4). The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for the noun is from 1607, in the writing of Samuel Hieron, a Church of England clergyman. The American Heritage Dictionary defines it as a person or thing of colossal power or achievement.oed.com · ahdictionary.com
Holier Than ThouAttestedThe phrase is word for word from the King James Version of Isa 65:5, the boast of a rebellious people: "Stand by thyself, come not near to me; for I am holier than thou." The key word is holy (Oxford English Dictionary).oed.com
Jehu (the English word)AttestedFrom the proper name Jehu, king of Israel, of whom the watchman said, "he driveth furiously" (2Ki 9:20). The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for the noun is from 1682, in the writing of John Dryden. The American Heritage Dictionary notes that Jehu is proverbially known for his swift chariot driving.oed.com · ahdictionary.com
JeremiadAttestedA borrowing from French jérémiade (Oxford English Dictionary), which the American Heritage Dictionary traces to Jérémie, Jeremiah, the prophet of the book of Lamentations. The OED’s earliest English evidence is from 1780, in the writing of Hannah More.oed.com · ahdictionary.com
Jezebel (the English word)AttestedFrom the proper name Jezebel, the wife of Ahab (1Ki 21:25). The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for the noun is from 1558, in the writing of John Knox. The American Heritage Dictionary defines it as a woman regarded as evil and scheming.oed.com · ahdictionary.com
JotAttestedFrom the Greek iōta, the name of the smallest letter of the Greek alphabet, answering to the Hebrew yodh — the least character in the Hebrew square script, a mere stroke no bigger than a comma; hence the smallest possible particle of writing, and so of anything.oed.com
JubileeAttestedFrom Hebrew yōbēl (יוֹבֵל) — a ram's horn, the trumpet blast that announced the year; possibly related to yabal (to bring, lead away). Via Latin jubilaeus and Old French jubilé. The English "jubilee" also absorbed the sound of Latin jubilare (to shout for joy), though etymologically distinct.oed.com
Judas (the English word)AttestedFrom the proper name Judas Iscariot. The American Heritage Dictionary traces the name through Late Latin and Greek to Hebrew yĕhûdâ, Judah, and defines the noun as one who betrays another under the guise of friendship. The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for the noun is from 1396.oed.com · ahdictionary.com
Labour of LoveAttestedThe phrase is word for word from the King James Version, which uses it twice: "your work of faith, and labour of love" (1Th 1:3) and "your work and labour of love, which ye have shewed toward his name" (Heb 6:10). The key word is labour (Oxford English Dictionary).oed.com
LeviathanAttestedFrom Hebrew livyathan (twisted, coiled one), related to lavah (to twist, join). Used in Scripture to denote a great sea creature symbolizing chaos, evil, and the enemies of God. The word entered English through the Vulgate Latin and became a metaphor for any monstrous, overwhelming power.oed.com
MammonAttestedAramaic mamona; wealth treated as a rival deity; the wealth that demands worship.oed.com
MaudlinAttestedFrom the old English form of the name Magdalene. The American Heritage Dictionary gives it as an alteration of (Mary) Magdalene, who was often pictured as a tearful penitent. The Oxford English Dictionary records the noun maudlin from around 1300, in a text on St. Mary Magdalen, and the adjective from 1607, in Thomas Middleton.oed.com · oed.com · ahdictionary.com
Methuselah (the English word)AttestedFrom the proper name Methuselah, whose days were "nine hundred sixty and nine years" (Gen 5:27). The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for the noun is from 1509, in a translation by H. Watson. The American Heritage Dictionary defines it as an extremely old or long-lived man.oed.com · ahdictionary.com
My Brother's KeeperAttestedThe phrase is word for word from the King James Version of Gen 4:9, Cain’s answer to God: "Am I my brother’s keeper?" The key word is keeper (Oxford English Dictionary).oed.com
Nimrod (the English word)AttestedFrom the proper name Nimrod, the "mighty hunter before the LORD" (Gen 10:9). The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for the noun is from around 1548, in the writing of John Bale. The American Heritage Dictionary gives two senses, a hunter and, informally, a foolish person. It says the second probably comes from the cartoon character Bugs Bunny mocking the hunter Elmer Fudd as "poor little Nimrod."oed.com · ahdictionary.com
PhariseeAttestedFrom Greek Pharisaios (Φαρισαῖος), from Aramaic pĕrīshayyā (פרישיא), from Hebrew parush (פָרושׁ) — "separated one, one who is set apart." The root parash (פָרַשׁ) means "to separate, to distinguish, to make distinct." The name was likely self-chosen, reflecting their commitment to separation from ritual impurity and Gentile influence.oed.com
PhilistineAttestedFrom Hebrew Pelishtim, the sea-people settled on the coastal plain of Canaan — Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron, Gath. In later English, also a slur for the coarse or uncultured; that later sense is not the biblical one.oed.com
ProdigalDisputedFrom Latin prodigus — "wasteful, lavish"; from prodigere — "to drive forth, squander"; from pro- (forth) + agere (to drive). The word does not appear explicitly in the Greek of Luke 15, but the concept is expressed through asōtōs (ἀσώτως) — "wastefully, dissolutely" — in Luke 15:13: "he squandered his property in reckless living."oed.com
Salt of the EarthAttestedFrom Matthew 5:13 — Christ's image opening the salt-light-city cluster of the Sermon on the Mount.oed.com
SamaritanAttestedHebrew Shomeronim, inhabitants of Samaria. The mixed-race, mixed-religion population of central Israel after the Assyrian exile; despised by Jews of Christ's day; subject of three of the Lord's most subversive teachings.oed.com
ScapegoatAttestedWilliam Tyndale's English rendering (~1530) of Hebrew Azazel (עֲזָאזֵל) — "the goat that departs" or "the goat of removal." English scape = escape + goat. The Hebrew Azazel is debated: possibly a place name (a remote wilderness), a personal name (a demon), or a descriptive term meaning "complete removal." All interpretations point toward the same theological reality: the total removal of sin.oed.com
ShibbolethAttestedHebrew shibboleth, an ear of corn, or a flowing stream; left untranslated by the Authorized Version at Judges 12:6, where it serves as a test word.oed.com
Skin of My TeethAttestedThe phrase is word for word from the King James Version of Job 19:20: "I am escaped with the skin of my teeth." English keeps it in the idiom by the skin of my teeth, meaning by the narrowest margin. The key word is skin (Oxford English Dictionary).oed.com
TalentAttestedHebrew kikkār (a round, a circuit; hence a round weight); Greek talanton, “a balance, weight, sum of money.” The largest unit of weight in the ancient world; estimates vary but a talent was approximately 75 lb (34 kg) of silver, equivalent to many years’ wages for a laborer.oed.com
The Thorn in the FleshAttestedFrom the Greek skolops te sarki, “a stake” or “thorn for the flesh,” the affliction given Paul to keep him humble, in which God’s grace was made sufficient.oed.com
TittleAttestedFrom Latin titulus, “a heading or superscription,” which came to mean a small mark written above a letter. In the KJV it is the tiniest stroke of the pen — the little horn or serif that distinguishes one Hebrew letter from another.oed.com

Civil War

WordOriginSources
BushwhackerAttestedAn English compound of bush + whacker, per the Oxford English Dictionary. OED’s earliest evidence is from 1809, in the writing of Washington Irving, well before the war, and OED records the military sense from the 1860s. The American Battlefield Trust notes that in Missouri and the other border states guerrilla fighters of either side were commonly called bushwhackers.oed.com · battlefields.org
CarpetbaggerAttestedFrom carpet-bag, a traveling bag made of carpet, + -er. The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for carpet-bagger is from 1868, in the London Daily News. Britannica describes it as a derogatory term first used during Reconstruction (1865–77) for a Northerner who moved to the South.oed.com · britannica.com
DeadlineAttestedAn English compound of dead + line. The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for the word is from 1814, before the war. Its grim Civil War sense was the line in a military prison that a prisoner could not cross without being shot. The National Park Service notes that such deadlines were common in the open stockade prisons of the era, Andersonville among them, and not unique to it. The OED tags the word in fishing from the 1810s, in military use from the 1860s and in printing from the 1910s; the printing sense, the last moment copy could go to press, became today’s time limit.oed.com · nps.gov · npr.org
GreenbackAttestedAn English compound of green + back. The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for the word is from before 1682, in the writing of Sir Thomas Browne. The money sense comes from the Civil War. The U.S. Currency Education Program records that in 1861 Congress authorized the Treasury to issue non-interest-bearing Demand Notes to finance the war, and that these notes earned the nickname “greenbacks” because of their color.oed.com · uscurrency.gov
HardtackAttestedAn English compound of hard + tack (an old word for food), so “hard food.” The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence is from 1830, in the United Service Magazine. It became the everyday ration of the Civil War soldier: a cracker of flour, water and salt that kept for a very long time.oed.com · nps.gov
JayhawkerDisputedProbably a compound of jay + hawker, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which hedges the derivation. OED’s earliest evidence is from 1860, in the Ashtabula Sentinel. The American Battlefield Trust notes that the term originated in the prewar Bleeding Kansas period and was used for pro-Union partisan fighters.oed.com · battlefields.org
ScalawagDisputedOf unknown origin, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which enters the word as scallywag. OED’s earliest evidence is from 1848, in John R. Bartlett’s dictionary, and it records the word in United States politics from the 1860s. Britannica describes the Reconstruction sense: a pejorative term for a white Southerner who supported the federal plan of Reconstruction.oed.com · britannica.com
SeceshAttestedA clipping of secession, per the Oxford English Dictionary. OED’s earliest evidence is from 1861, the first year of the war, in the Ohio Statesman.oed.com
SideburnsDisputedFrom Ambrose Everett Burnside (1824–1881), Union general, later governor of Rhode Island and U.S. senator, famous for the bushy whiskers that ran down his cheeks into his mustache with a shaved chin. His style was called burnsides by the 1870s; the syllables were reversed into sideburns by 1876, the Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence, since they grew on the sides of the face. Long, thick sideburns returned as a Boomer fashion in the 1950s (Elvis) and above all in the late 1960s and 1970s. Burnside commanded the Army of the Potomac at the Battle of Fredericksburg, December 11–15, 1862, a disastrous Union defeat.oed.com
SkedaddleDisputedAmerican, first recorded in 1861 among Union soldiers in the opening year of the Civil War, used of troops retreating in a hurry; origin uncertain (fanciful explanations from Greek or Scots dialect are unproven). The word outlived the war as comic, mock-grand slang for leaving fast, and it was the grandparents’ word for it all through the twentieth century: "Now skedaddle!" to children underfoot in the 1930s, 1940s, and 1950s. The Silent Generation did not coin it, but they are the generation that handed it down to the rest of us.oed.com

Eponyms

WordOriginSources
AmpereAttestedFrom a proper name, per the Oxford English Dictionary: André-Marie Ampère (1775–1836), the French physicist and mathematician. The OED records its earliest evidence in 1846, in a translation by E. West. An international convention at the 1881 International Exposition of Electricity made the ampere a standard unit.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org
BowdlerizeAttestedFrom a proper name, with the suffix -ize: Thomas Bowdler (1754–1825), who published The Family Shakspeare, an edition of Shakespeare with passages he judged unfit for family reading removed. The Oxford English Dictionary dates bowdlerize to the 1830s.oed.com
Bowie KnifeAttestedFrom a proper name combined with knife, per the Oxford English Dictionary: James Bowie (1796–1836). The OED records its earliest evidence in 1830, in the Daily National Intelligencer of Washington, D.C.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org · en.wikipedia.org
BoycottAttestedFrom a proper name: Captain Charles Cunningham Boycott, an English land agent in County Mayo, Ireland. In 1880 his tenants and neighbours, organized under the Irish Land League, refused to work for him, sell to him, or deal with him at all. The Oxford English Dictionary dates both verb and noun to the 1880s.oed.com · oed.com
BrailleAttestedFrom a proper name, per the Oxford English Dictionary: Louis Braille (1809–1852), the French teacher of the blind who invented the system. The OED records its earliest evidence in 1853, in the writing of E. C. Johnson, the year after Braille’s death.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org
CardiganAttestedFrom a proper name: James Thomas Brudenell, 7th Earl of Cardigan (1797–1868), the British cavalry officer who led the Charge of the Light Brigade at Balaclava in the Crimean War (25 October 1854). The knitted woollen jacket was named for him. The Oxford English Dictionary dates the word to the 1850s.oed.com
DerrickAttestedFrom a proper name: Derrick, a hangman at Tyburn in London around 1600. His name was first given to the gallows itself, and from there to a hoisting apparatus with a beam, and later to the framework over an oil well. The Oxford English Dictionary dates derrick to the early 1600s.oed.com
DieselAttestedFrom a proper name: Rudolf Diesel (1858–1913), the German engineer who patented the compression-ignition engine in the 1890s. In 1913 he disappeared overboard from a steamer crossing to England. The Oxford English Dictionary dates diesel to the 1890s.oed.com
DunceAttestedFrom a proper name, per the Oxford English Dictionary: the Scottish theologian John Duns Scotus (c. 1265/66–1308). The OED records its earliest evidence in 1530, in the Tyndale Bible. Its own third edition cites mid-sixteenth-century uses for a follower of Duns Scotus, for a person given to ridiculous pedantry, and for a fool.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org · en.wikipedia.org
GerrymanderAttestedA blend of a proper name and salamander. In 1812 the Massachusetts legislature, under Governor Elbridge Gerry, redrew a state senate district into a shape that critics said looked like a salamander, and the Gerry-mander was born. The Oxford English Dictionary dates the word to the 1810s. Gerry pronounced his name with a hard G; the word is now usually said with a soft one.oed.com
GuyAttestedFrom a proper name, per the Oxford English Dictionary: Guy Fawkes (1570–1606), of the Gunpowder Plot of 1605. The OED records its earliest evidence for this noun in 1796, in the Oracle.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org · en.wikipedia.org
LeotardAttestedFrom a proper name: Jules Léotard (1838–1870), the French acrobat who introduced the flying trapeze act in Paris in 1859 and performed in a close-fitting one-piece garment. The Oxford English Dictionary dates leotard to the 1920s.oed.com
LutheranAttestedFrom a proper name with the suffix -an, per the Oxford English Dictionary: Martin Luther. The OED records its earliest evidence in 1521, in a letter by W. Warham. The name began as a term of abuse: Johann Maier von Eck used it against Luther at the Leipzig Debate in July 1519, following the practice of naming a heresy after its leader.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org
MackintoshAttestedFrom a proper name, per the Oxford English Dictionary: Charles Macintosh (1766–1843), the Scottish chemist. The OED records its earliest evidence in 1835, in the Analyst. Writers added a k to his name, and the spelling mackintosh is now standard.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org · en.wikipedia.org
MaverickAttestedFrom a proper name: Samuel Augustus Maverick (1803–1870), a Texas lawyer and landowner and a signer of the Texas Declaration of Independence, who did not brand his calves. Unbranded cattle on the range came to be called mavericks. The Oxford English Dictionary dates the word to the 1860s, with the political sense of an independent who will not run with the herd from the 1880s.oed.com
PanderAttestedFrom a proper name, per the Oxford English Dictionary: Pandarus, the go-between in the medieval story of Troilus and Criseyde. The OED records its earliest evidence from around 1450. The noun comes from Chaucer’s use of the character, the verb from Shakespeare’s.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org
PasteurizeAttestedFrom a proper name combined with an English element, modelled on a French word, per the Oxford English Dictionary: Louis Pasteur (1822–1895), the French chemist and microbiologist. The OED records its earliest evidence in 1881, in the Pharmaceutical Journal & Transactions.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org · en.wikipedia.org
ProcrusteanAttestedFrom a proper name with the suffix -an, per the Oxford English Dictionary: Procrustes, the bandit of Greek legend. The OED records its earliest evidence in 1647, in the writing of John Goodwin, Independent minister. The Greek name means “the stretcher.”oed.com · en.wikipedia.org
QuislingAttestedFrom a proper name: Vidkun Quisling (1887–1945), the Norwegian Nazi party leader who proclaimed himself head of government on the day Germany invaded Norway in April 1940 and later served as head of the occupation government. He was tried and executed for treason in October 1945. The Oxford English Dictionary dates quisling, a traitor who collaborates with an occupying enemy, to the 1940s.oed.com
RaglanAttestedFrom a proper name, per the Oxford English Dictionary: FitzRoy Somerset, 1st Baron Raglan (1788–1855). The OED records its earliest evidence in 1854, in the Ipswich Journal, the year Raglan took command in the Crimea.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org · en.wikipedia.org
SandwichAttestedFrom a proper name: John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich (1718–1792). The Oxford English Dictionary dates the food sense to the mid 1700s. The familiar story that the earl called for meat between slices of bread so he need not leave the gaming table comes from a French visitor’s account and cannot be confirmed; the name itself is not in doubt.oed.com
SaxophoneAttestedFrom a proper name combined with Greek: Adolphe Sax (1814–1894), the Belgian instrument maker who patented the instrument in Paris in 1846, + Greek -phonos, “sounding.” The Oxford English Dictionary dates saxophone to the 1850s.oed.com
ShrapnelAttestedFrom a proper name: Henry Shrapnel (1761–1842), an officer of the British Royal Artillery who devised a hollow shell filled with musket balls and a bursting charge, timed to break open over enemy troops. The British Army adopted it in 1803. The Oxford English Dictionary dates shrapnel to the 1800s; it later came to mean any fragments thrown out by an exploding shell or bomb.oed.com
SilhouetteAttestedFrom a proper name: Étienne de Silhouette (1709–1767), Controller-General of Finances in France in 1759, whose severe economies made his name a byword for anything cheap or bare. The outline portrait cut from dark paper took his name. The Oxford English Dictionary dates the English word to the late 1700s.oed.com
SpoonerismAttestedFrom a proper name with the suffix -ism, per the Oxford English Dictionary: the Rev. William Archibald Spooner (1844–1930) of New College, Oxford. The OED records its earliest evidence in 1891, in the Western Morning News of Plymouth.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org · en.wikipedia.org
StetsonAttestedFrom a proper name, per the Oxford English Dictionary: John Batterson Stetson (1830–1906), the American hat maker. The OED records its earliest evidence from around 1900.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org
WattAttestedFrom a proper name, per the Oxford English Dictionary: James Watt (1736–1819), the Scottish engineer. The OED records its earliest evidence in 1882, in the writing of C. W. Siemens, who proposed the name for the unit that year in his presidential address to the British Association for the Advancement of Science.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org · en.wikipedia.org
ZeppelinAttestedFrom a proper name, per the Oxford English Dictionary: Count Ferdinand von Zeppelin (1838–1917), the German general and inventor of the rigid airship. The OED records its earliest evidence in 1896, in the Washington Post.oed.com · en.wikipedia.org

WordOriginSources
BroncoAttestedA borrowing from Spanish bronco, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1861, in the writing of J. R. Browne.oed.com
CorralAttestedA borrowing from Spanish corral, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence for the word in 1582, in a translation by Nicholas Lichefield. The OED lists uses in agriculture from the late 1500s, hunting from the late 1700s, and fortification from the 1840s.oed.com
GreenhornAttestedFormed within English by compounding green and horn, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence for the word in 1455. The OED lists uses in military contexts from the mid-1600s and for animals from the 1870s.oed.com
HornswoggleDisputedProbably an arbitrary formation, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1829, in the Virginia Literary Museum. In other words, it is a made-up word with no traceable parts.oed.com
LassoAttestedA borrowing from Spanish lazo, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1808, in the Narrative of the Expedition under General Craufurd. The OED lists uses in agriculture from the 1800s.oed.com
MustangDisputedProbably a borrowing from Spanish mestengo or mostrenco, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1808, in the writing of Zebulon Pike, soldier and explorer. The OED’s “probably” marks the source as not certain.oed.com
StampedeAttestedA borrowing from Spanish estampida, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1828. The OED lists uses in agriculture and of animals from the 1820s, in mining and United States politics from the 1840s.oed.com
TenderfootAttestedFormed within English by compounding tender and foot, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1881, in the writing of Linus Brockett. The OED lists uses in education from the 1880s and in scouting from the 1900s.oed.com

Jacob's LadderAttestedFrom the proper name Jacob and ladder, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which records its earliest evidence in 1733, in a dictionary by the horticulturist Philip Miller, and lists the nautical sense from the 1840s. The Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC) says the name alludes to the patriarch Jacob, who dreamed of a ladder reaching to heaven (Gen 28:12).oed.com · history.navy.mil
