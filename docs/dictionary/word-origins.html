Civil War

WordOriginSources
BushwhackerAttestedAn English compound of bush + whacker, per the Oxford English Dictionary. OED’s earliest evidence is from 1809, in the writing of Washington Irving, well before the war, and OED records the military sense from the 1860s. The American Battlefield Trust notes that in Missouri and the other border states guerrilla fighters of either side were commonly called bushwhackers.oed.com · battlefields.org
CarpetbaggerAttestedFrom carpet-bag, a traveling bag made of carpet, + -er. The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for carpet-bagger is from 1868, in the London Daily News. Britannica describes it as a derogatory term first used during Reconstruction (1865–77) for a Northerner who moved to the South.oed.com · britannica.com
DeadlineAttestedAn English compound of dead + line. The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for the word is from 1814, before the war. Its grim Civil War sense was the line in a military prison that a prisoner could not cross without being shot. The National Park Service notes that such deadlines were common in the open stockade prisons of the era, Andersonville among them, and not unique to it. The OED tags the word in fishing from the 1810s, in military use from the 1860s and in printing from the 1910s; the printing sense, the last moment copy could go to press, became today’s time limit.oed.com · nps.gov · npr.org
GreenbackAttestedAn English compound of green + back. The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence for the word is from before 1682, in the writing of Sir Thomas Browne. The money sense comes from the Civil War. The U.S. Currency Education Program records that in 1861 Congress authorized the Treasury to issue non-interest-bearing Demand Notes to finance the war, and that these notes earned the nickname “greenbacks” because of their color.oed.com · uscurrency.gov
HardtackAttestedAn English compound of hard + tack (an old word for food), so “hard food.” The Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence is from 1830, in the United Service Magazine. It became the everyday ration of the Civil War soldier: a cracker of flour, water and salt that kept for a very long time.oed.com · nps.gov
JayhawkerDisputedProbably a compound of jay + hawker, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which hedges the derivation. OED’s earliest evidence is from 1860, in the Ashtabula Sentinel. The American Battlefield Trust notes that the term originated in the prewar Bleeding Kansas period and was used for pro-Union partisan fighters.oed.com · battlefields.org
ScalawagDisputedOf unknown origin, per the Oxford English Dictionary, which enters the word as scallywag. OED’s earliest evidence is from 1848, in John R. Bartlett’s dictionary, and it records the word in United States politics from the 1860s. Britannica describes the Reconstruction sense: a pejorative term for a white Southerner who supported the federal plan of Reconstruction.oed.com · britannica.com
SeceshAttestedA clipping of secession, per the Oxford English Dictionary. OED’s earliest evidence is from 1861, the first year of the war, in the Ohio Statesman.oed.com
SideburnsDisputedFrom Ambrose Everett Burnside (1824–1881), Union general, later governor of Rhode Island and U.S. senator, famous for the bushy whiskers that ran down his cheeks into his mustache with a shaved chin. His style was called burnsides by the 1870s; the syllables were reversed into sideburns by 1876, the Oxford English Dictionary’s earliest evidence, since they grew on the sides of the face. Long, thick sideburns returned as a Boomer fashion in the 1950s (Elvis) and above all in the late 1960s and 1970s. Burnside commanded the Army of the Potomac at the Battle of Fredericksburg, December 11–15, 1862, a disastrous Union defeat.oed.com
SkedaddleDisputedAmerican, first recorded in 1861 among Union soldiers in the opening year of the Civil War, used of troops retreating in a hurry; origin uncertain (fanciful explanations from Greek or Scots dialect are unproven). The word outlived the war as comic, mock-grand slang for leaving fast, and it was the grandparents’ word for it all through the twentieth century: "Now skedaddle!" to children underfoot in the 1930s, 1940s, and 1950s. The Silent Generation did not coin it, but they are the generation that handed it down to the rest of us.oed.com

Eponyms

WordOriginSources
BowdlerizeAttestedFrom a proper name, with the suffix -ize: Thomas Bowdler (1754–1825), who published The Family Shakspeare, an edition of Shakespeare with passages he judged unfit for family reading removed. The Oxford English Dictionary dates bowdlerize to the 1830s.oed.com
BoycottAttestedFrom a proper name: Captain Charles Cunningham Boycott, an English land agent in County Mayo, Ireland. In 1880 his tenants and neighbours, organized under the Irish Land League, refused to work for him, sell to him, or deal with him at all. The Oxford English Dictionary dates both verb and noun to the 1880s.oed.com · oed.com
CardiganAttestedFrom a proper name: James Thomas Brudenell, 7th Earl of Cardigan (1797–1868), the British cavalry officer who led the Charge of the Light Brigade at Balaclava in the Crimean War (25 October 1854). The knitted woollen jacket was named for him. The Oxford English Dictionary dates the word to the 1850s.oed.com
DerrickAttestedFrom a proper name: Derrick, a hangman at Tyburn in London around 1600. His name was first given to the gallows itself, and from there to a hoisting apparatus with a beam, and later to the framework over an oil well. The Oxford English Dictionary dates derrick to the early 1600s.oed.com
DieselAttestedFrom a proper name: Rudolf Diesel (1858–1913), the German engineer who patented the compression-ignition engine in the 1890s. In 1913 he disappeared overboard from a steamer crossing to England. The Oxford English Dictionary dates diesel to the 1890s.oed.com
GerrymanderAttestedA blend of a proper name and salamander. In 1812 the Massachusetts legislature, under Governor Elbridge Gerry, redrew a state senate district into a shape that critics said looked like a salamander, and the Gerry-mander was born. The Oxford English Dictionary dates the word to the 1810s. Gerry pronounced his name with a hard G; the word is now usually said with a soft one.oed.com
LeotardAttestedFrom a proper name: Jules Léotard (1838–1870), the French acrobat who introduced the flying trapeze act in Paris in 1859 and performed in a close-fitting one-piece garment. The Oxford English Dictionary dates leotard to the 1920s.oed.com
MaverickAttestedFrom a proper name: Samuel Augustus Maverick (1803–1870), a Texas lawyer and landowner and a signer of the Texas Declaration of Independence, who did not brand his calves. Unbranded cattle on the range came to be called mavericks. The Oxford English Dictionary dates the word to the 1860s, with the political sense of an independent who will not run with the herd from the 1880s.oed.com
QuislingAttestedFrom a proper name: Vidkun Quisling (1887–1945), the Norwegian Nazi party leader who proclaimed himself head of government on the day Germany invaded Norway in April 1940 and later served as head of the occupation government. He was tried and executed for treason in October 1945. The Oxford English Dictionary dates quisling, a traitor who collaborates with an occupying enemy, to the 1940s.oed.com
SandwichAttestedFrom a proper name: John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich (1718–1792). The Oxford English Dictionary dates the food sense to the mid 1700s. The familiar story that the earl called for meat between slices of bread so he need not leave the gaming table comes from a French visitor’s account and cannot be confirmed; the name itself is not in doubt.oed.com
SaxophoneAttestedFrom a proper name combined with Greek: Adolphe Sax (1814–1894), the Belgian instrument maker who patented the instrument in Paris in 1846, + Greek -phonos, “sounding.” The Oxford English Dictionary dates saxophone to the 1850s.oed.com
ShrapnelAttestedFrom a proper name: Henry Shrapnel (1761–1842), an officer of the British Royal Artillery who devised a hollow shell filled with musket balls and a bursting charge, timed to break open over enemy troops. The British Army adopted it in 1803. The Oxford English Dictionary dates shrapnel to the 1800s; it later came to mean any fragments thrown out by an exploding shell or bomb.oed.com
SilhouetteAttestedFrom a proper name: Étienne de Silhouette (1709–1767), Controller-General of Finances in France in 1759, whose severe economies made his name a byword for anything cheap or bare. The outline portrait cut from dark paper took his name. The Oxford English Dictionary dates the English word to the late 1700s.oed.com
