Resurrection
/ˌre-zə-ˈrek-shən/
noun
From Latin resurrectio — "a rising again," from re- (again) + surgere (to rise). The bodily rising from death to immortal life.

📖 Biblical Definition

The literal, bodily raising of the dead to life — not resuscitation, not metaphor, not spiritual renewal only, but the physical return of the soul to a transformed, glorified body. The resurrection of Jesus Christ is the cornerstone of Christian faith: if it did not happen, "your faith is futile and you are still in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17). Christ was the first fruits of a great harvest — all who are His will also be raised. Resurrection is God's final verdict on death: death is defeated, not merely mourned. It is the vindication of Christ, the guarantee of believers, and the defeat of the last enemy (1 Corinthians 15:26).

📜 Webster 1828 Definition

RESURRECTION, n. A rising from the dead; the revival of the dead; particularly, the rising of the dead from their graves at the last day; generally used in this specific sense, as the resurrection. "I am the resurrection and the life." John 11:25.

⚠️ Modern Corruption

Liberal theology spiritualizes the resurrection into mere metaphor — a "resurrection of hope," a "rising consciousness," or the enduring memory of Jesus in his followers' hearts. This strips the resurrection of its power and its terror. Even within evangelicalism, the resurrection is often treated as an afterthought to the cross, when Paul's letters make clear it is equally essential (Romans 4:25). A faith in a merely spiritual resurrection is not Christianity — it is philosophy.

🔗 Greek & Hebrew Roots

G386 — anastasis (ἀνάστασις): a standing up again; resurrection from the dead. The primary NT word for the resurrection.

G1453 — egeirō (ἐγείρω): to rouse, to raise up; used of Christ's resurrection throughout the NT.

H2421 — chāyāh (חָיָה): to live, to revive; used in OT passages anticipating resurrection (Ezekiel 37).

✍️ Usage

Christianity does not offer escape from the body — it promises the resurrection of the body, the redemption of all creation, the making new of all things.

The resurrection is not a legend; it is the most attested event of the ancient world — witnessed by over 500 people, many still living when Paul wrote (1 Corinthians 15:6).

To preach the resurrection is to preach hope that is not wishful thinking but historical fact: the tomb is empty, and that changes everything.
