Kenosis
/kɪˈnoʊ.sɪs/
noun (theological)
From Greek κένωσις (kenosis) — an emptying. From κενόω (kenoō) — to empty, to pour out, to make void. Derived from Philippians 2:7 — "He emptied Himself" (ἑαυτὸν ἐκένωσεν).

📖 Biblical Definition

The self-emptying of the eternal Son of God in the Incarnation. In taking on human flesh, Christ voluntarily laid aside the independent exercise of His divine prerogatives — not abandoning His divine nature, but veiling His glory, submitting to human limitation, and taking the form of a servant (Phil. 2:5–8). Kenosis is the supreme act of divine humility: the Lord of glory born in a manger, the Creator submitting to creation's constraints, not because He was diminished but because He loved. Kenosis demonstrates that true power freely chooses servanthood; true greatness descends to lift others up.

📜 Webster 1828 Definition

Webster 1828 did not include "Kenosis" as an English entry — the theological term entered common theological usage primarily in the mid-19th century following German kenotic theology debates. The underlying concept, however, was well established: the Incarnation involved the Son of God taking on the "form of a servant" and the "likeness of men," voluntarily condescending from divine glory to human limitation for the purpose of redemption.

⚠️ Modern Corruption

19th-century kenotic theology (Thomasius, et al.) erred by suggesting Christ actually divested Himself of essential divine attributes (omniscience, omnipotence) in becoming man — effectively making the incarnate Christ less than fully divine. Orthodox Christianity affirms that Christ retained all divine attributes in the Incarnation but chose not to use them independently, operating instead through the Spirit in submitted dependence on the Father. The error matters: a Christ who emptied Himself of deity cannot save; only the fully divine-fully human mediator can bridge the gap between God and man (1 Tim. 2:5).

📖 Key Scripture

Philippians 2:5–8 — "Though He was in the form of God…He emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men."

John 1:14 — "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen His glory."

2 Corinthians 8:9 — "Though He was rich, yet for your sake He became poor, so that you by His poverty might become rich."

Hebrews 4:15 — "We do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin."

🔗 Greek & Hebrew Roots

G2758 — κενόω (kenoō) — to empty, to make of no effect; used in Phil. 2:7 of Christ's self-emptying in the Incarnation.

G3444 — μορφή (morphē) — form, nature; Christ existed in the "form of God" and took the "form of a servant" — denoting real participation in each nature, not mere appearance.

✍️ Usage

Kenosis is the theological engine of Christian humility: the Son of God became the least so that the least might become sons of God.

Christian leadership is kenotic by nature — not the exercise of power over others, but the voluntary laying down of privilege for their good (Mark 10:45).

The kenosis of Christ is the ultimate refutation of the prosperity gospel: God in the flesh chose poverty, obscurity, and suffering as the path to redemption.
