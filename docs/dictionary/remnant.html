Remnant
/ˈrem-nənt/
noun
From Old French remanant — that which remains, from Latin remanere (to remain, stay behind). In Scripture, it carries the weight of divine preservation against all odds.

📖 Biblical Definition

The faithful few whom God preserves within or out of a larger apostate or decimated population. The remnant doctrine runs throughout Scripture as a thread of sovereign grace: when Israel fell into idolatry, God kept a remnant who had not bowed to Baal (1 Kings 19:18). When Judah went into exile, a remnant returned. Paul cites this in Romans 11 to show that God has not abandoned Israel — "even so then at this present time there is a remnant according to the election of grace" (Rom. 11:5). The remnant is never defined by human worthiness but by divine choice and preservation. It is the seed that survives winter, the coal that keeps the fire, the root that produces new life.

📜 Webster 1828 Definition

REMNANT, n. Residue; that which is left after the separation and removal of a part. In Scripture, the small number of Israelites who, after the dispersion, preserved their faith and maintained their integrity before God; also applied to any small number of true believers remaining in times of general apostasy.

⚠️ Modern Corruption

In secular use, "remnant" is merely a retail term for leftover fabric. Theologically, the danger is the church's temptation to measure faithfulness by numbers and to lose hope when the faithful few seem overwhelmed by cultural apostasy. The remnant doctrine answers this despair: God has always worked through the few, not the many. Faithfulness, not popularity, is the measure of a remnant. The danger is also pride — those who identify as "remnant" can become isolationist or judgmental, forgetting that the remnant exists by grace, not superior virtue.
