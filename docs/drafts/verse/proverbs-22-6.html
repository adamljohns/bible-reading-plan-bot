Proverbs 22:6

Everyone quotes it as a guarantee with a delay on it. Read the words and it is something better, and heavier: a command with a seventy-year horizon.

Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it.

— World English Bible (public domain)

Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it.

— King James Version (public domain) · margin: “Train: or, Catechise” · “in: Heb. in his way”

1. The Text and Its Words

The sentence has two halves, and only one of them is addressed to you. The first half is an order given to a parent. The second half is a statement about a grown man, decades out. Nearly all the trouble people have with this verse comes from reading the second half as the payment due on the first, as though the proverb were a receipt. It is not. It is a proverb — a true thing said in the shortest form that will stick — and its subject is the parent's job, not the parent's leverage.

The King James translators themselves flagged where the sentence bends. Their margin, printed in the text on this site, offers Catechise as an alternative to Train up, and notes that the Hebrew behind “the way he should go” is literally “in his way.” Hold onto both notes. The words will explain them.

  • חָנַךְ chanak · “Train up” · Strong's H2596

    In the Strong's-tagged King James text this site runs on, this verb occurs four times in the entire Old Testament. Three of them are buildings: the man who has built a new house and has not yet dedicated it (Deuteronomy 20:5), and Solomon dedicating the temple (1 Kings 8:63; 2 Chronicles 7:5). The fourth is this verse. Three buildings and a boy. The word is not “educate.” It is what a man does when he hands a finished structure over to the use it was built for. That is why the margin reaches for catechise: the act is consecration, and the instrument is teaching.

  • נַעַר naʿar · “a child” · Strong's H5288

    Not a toddler, and not a narrow window. The word covers the infant Moses crying in the basket (Exodus 2:6), Isaac carrying the wood up Moriah (Genesis 22:5), Joseph at seventeen keeping the flock (Genesis 37:2), and David when Saul told him he was too young to face Goliath (1 Samuel 17:33). It is the whole span of a son still under someone's roof and someone's authority. And nine verses later Solomon uses the same word with no sentiment at all: “Folly is bound up in the heart of a child” (Proverbs 22:15). Whatever chanak means, it is being done to material that is already bent.

  • דֶּרֶךְ derek · “in the way” · Strong's H1870

    A road. Then, by the oldest metaphor in the book, a manner of life. Proverbs runs on this word: there is a way of the wise and a way of the fool, and there is no third road. A father is not offering his son a philosophy to consider. He is putting him on a road that goes somewhere.

  • פֶּה peh · “he should go” · Strong's H6310

    Here is the surprise. There is no verb of walking under “he should go.” The word is the ordinary Hebrew noun for a mouth. Used this way it means according to — Numbers has Israel breaking camp and pitching camp “according to the commandment of the LORD,” which is literally according to the mouth of the LORD (Numbers 3:16; 9:20). That is the language of marching orders. So the clause is not “the way he would probably enjoy.” It is the way speaking, and the boy set to it.

  • זָקֵן zaqen · “and when he is old” · Strong's H2204

    Grey hair, and the standing that comes with it — this is the root behind Israel's elders, the men at the gate. The horizon of the verse is not graduation, or the day he moves out, or whether he behaves at fourteen. It is the man at seventy. You are being told to work on a timescale you will mostly not live to audit.

  • סוּר sur · “he will not depart” · Strong's H5493

    To turn aside. It is the word for putting foreign gods away, and the word for Israel wandering off the commandment. Two kings show both ends of it inside one family. Amon “walked in all the ways that his father walked in, and served the idols that his father served” (2 Kings 21:21). His son Josiah, crowned at eight, “walked in all the way of David his father, and didn't turn aside to the right hand or to the left” (2 Kings 22:2) — derek and sur, the two words of this proverb's second half, in one sentence about a boy who had every reason to turn.

Now the margin's note bites. The Hebrew says his way, and men have read that two ways. Some take it as the boy's own frame — his gifts, his temperament, the particular shape of the man God is making — so that training is fitted to a son rather than stamped on a category. Others have read it bitterly: let a boy have his own way and he will keep it, and you will not get it back. The book itself rules on the question. Nine verses down, the child's own way is named outright, and its name is folly. So the road here is not the one the boy would pick. It is a road handed to him — but handed to him, this son, with this frame, by a father who has bothered to learn the difference between his sons. Both halves of the truth survive.

2. Where It Sits

Proverbs 22 is not an argument. It is a string of couplets, and you have to read the neighbors to hear the tone. A good name is worth more than money (v. 1). Rich and poor were made by the same LORD (v. 2). The prudent man sees trouble coming and hides himself; the simple walk on into it and pay for it (v. 3). “Thorns and snares are in the path of the wicked” (v. 5). And immediately after our verse: “The rich rule over the poor. The borrower is servant to the lender” (v. 7).

That is the neighborhood — roads, traps, and who ends up owning whom. Verse 6 is not a domestic aside dropped into a chapter about money and power. It is the same subject seen from the front end. A life is a road; the roads are already laid; and somebody teaches a boy which one to take long before he is old enough to know there was a choice.

The book has been saying so from its first page. “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge” (1:7) is followed at once by “My son, listen to your father's instruction, and don't forsake your mother's teaching” (1:8). In chapter 4 Solomon remembers being on the receiving end of it — his own father taught him, and told him to keep the words and live. Proverbs 22:6 is not a parenting tip filed among proverbs about money. It is the book describing what the book is.

And it is a proverb. That word is load-bearing. A proverb compresses what is generally and reliably true into a form a man can carry into the field. “The borrower is servant to the lender” is true, and there are men who borrowed and prospered. “A good name is more desirable than great riches” is true, and good men have died poor and slandered. Read verse 6 the way you already read verse 7 and the difficulty dissolves: it tells you what training does, not what training guarantees. It describes the ordinary grain of God's world. And notice which end of it is in the imperative — the parent is commanded; the outcome is described.

  • Deuteronomy 6:7

    and you shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise up.

  • Ephesians 6:4

    You fathers, don’t provoke your children to wrath, but nurture them in the discipline and instruction of the Lord.

  • Titus 2:4

    that they may train the young women to love their husbands, to love their children,

  • Luke 2:52

    And Jesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin's commentary on Proverbs is not in this library; the Institutes are, in full. So what follows is Calvin not on this verse but on the commandment this verse serves — the fifth — and it turns out to be exactly the help a reader of Proverbs 22:6 needs.

Authority on loan

Calvin will not let a father think the honour he receives is his own property. God distributes His titles, and a man who bears one holds it as a trust:

we must consider that our earthly father possesses something of a divine nature in him, because there is some reason for his bearing a divine title

Calvin, Institutes II.8 · read the chapter →

Which cuts in both directions at once, and Calvin says both. To the child: the deference you owe your father is not finally about your father.

Therefore the submission yielded to them should be a step in our ascent to the Supreme Parent, and hence, if they instigate us to transgress the law, they deserve not to be regarded as parents, but as strangers attempting to seduce us from obedience to our true Father.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 · read the chapter →

He states the limit flatly a sentence earlier — we are ordered to obey parents only in the Lord. And to the father, when he comes to list what each station owes, the duty lands in one sentence:

Let parents be careful to bring up, guide, and teach their children as a trust committed to them by God.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 · read the chapter →

A trust committed to them. That is chanak in a Reformer's vocabulary. The house is not yours; you are dedicating it for its owner. Calvin adds immediately that this is no license for a heavy hand — fathers are not to exasperate or alienate their children by cruelty, but to cherish them.

How to read a promise attached to a duty

The harder question — whether the second half of Proverbs 22:6 is a guarantee — Calvin had already worked through on the same commandment. Paul calls the fifth the first commandment with a promise, and the promise is long life in the land. Calvin refuses both the wooden reading and the escape hatch. He will not pretend obedient children never die young, and he will not let that fact make God a liar:

Wherefore, if any one who is obedient to parents happens to be cut off before mature age (a thing which not infrequently happens), the Lord nevertheless adheres to his promise as steadily as when he bestows a hundred acres of land where he had promised only one.

We must consider that long life is promised only in so far as it is a blessing from God, and that it is a blessing only in so far as it is a manifestation of divine favour.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 · read the chapter →

Apply that here and the pressure comes off without anything being surrendered. The second half of this proverb is a promise of exactly that kind: real, reliable, God's own word about how He ordinarily works — and not a mechanism a man can operate. God adheres to it. He does not always pay it out on the schedule or in the currency a father had in mind. A father who reads it as a contract will end up either presumptuous or destroyed, and both are ways of not believing it.

4. Confessional Anchor

Neither the 1689 Confession nor Keach's original catechism quotes this verse. This site's edition of the Baptist Catechism adds three questions on the Christian household, and the middle one takes Proverbs 22:6 as a proof-text.

Baptist Catechism · Question 116

Q. What does God require of parents?

God requires parents to bring up their children in the instruction and discipline of the Lord: to teach them his Word diligently, to pray for them and with them, and to set before them a godly example — not trusting in their own labor, but in the grace of God to save.

The Baptist Catechism, Q116
Work through the Catechism →

Read the last clause again. The catechism builds the limit into the duty itself. Teach diligently, pray, model it — and do not trust any of it. The command and the disclaimer arrive in the same breath, which is precisely how the proverb wants to be held.

The question before it says what the training is actually made of, and it is not a curriculum:

Baptist Catechism · Question 115

God has appointed the Christian home to be a place of worship, where parents and children together fear the Lord — praying, singing his praise, and feeding on his Word — so that the knowledge of God is kept alive and handed on from one generation to the next.

The Baptist Catechism, Q115
Work through the Catechism →

The Confession puts the same thing in its chapter on worship, and puts a clock on it:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 22, Paragraph 6

God is to be worshiped everywhere in spirit and in truth — as in private families daily, and in secret each one by himself, so more solemnly in the public assemblies, which are not carelessly nor wilfully to be neglected or forsaken, when God by his Word or providence calls thereunto.

1689 LBCF, ch. 22 · read the chapter →

In private families daily. That is what dedication looks like when it stops being a sentiment and becomes a habit with a time attached to it.

And then the wall the confession will not let you climb over:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 10, Paragraph 2

This effectual call is of God's free and special grace alone, not from anything at all foreseen in man, nor from any power or agency in the creature.

1689 LBCF, ch. 10 · read the chapter →

No father has ever regenerated a son. Training is a means, and God honours means; it is not a cause, and no amount of it obliges Him. Proverbs 22:6 is a command about the means. Chapter 10 is about the cause. Confuse the two and you will either take credit that is not yours or take blame that is not yours either.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the Family Captain memorize pack — carry it.

Dedication, not decoration. Three of the four times this verb appears, someone is handing a building over to its purpose. A man dedicates a house before he lives in it. Your children are being dedicated to some use whether or not you are the one doing it — the culture will gladly take the job, and it is not neutral about which road. Deciding not to decide is a decision, and it is the one the proverb is written against.

Put it on the calendar. The confession says private families daily. Not a program, not a curriculum you will buy in January. A Bible open, a psalm or a hymn, a prayer, at a time that recurs whether or not anyone in the room feels like it. Ten minutes that actually happen beat an hour you keep meaning to schedule. This is the ordinary machinery God uses, and it is unimpressive on any given Tuesday, which is exactly why men quit it.

Train the son in front of you. “In his way.” Same road, different boy. One needs the brakes and one needs the throttle, and a father who has not bothered to learn which is which is not training a child, he is running a policy. That takes time you would rather spend elsewhere. Spend it.

Do not pretend the material is neutral. Nine verses on, folly is bound up in the boy's heart. Correction is part of the job, not an interruption of it. So is the other half — Paul's line for fathers is not to provoke their children to wrath. A man who only corrects is not training either; he is just venting on someone smaller than he is.

Work on a seventy-year clock. Zaqen is grey hair. You will spend most of your fathering unable to see whether any of it took, and the years when it looks worst are frequently the years it is going in. That is not a reason for optimism; it is a reason to stop grading yourself on quarterly results you were never given the authority to produce.

And if he has already turned aside. Sur means turned off the road. It does not mean the road moved. Amon walked in his father's idolatry — and Amon's son was Josiah, who at eight years old took the road his father and his grandfather Manasseh had both abandoned, and would not leave it in either direction. That is not a formula, and it is not a promise anyone can hold God to. It is a reminder that the God who does the calling is not working off your timeline and was never depending on your labour. Keep the door open, keep the table set, keep the Book on it, and pray for him by name.

Last thing. Do not use this verse as a whip — not on yourself, and not on another man. Faithful fathers have wept over grown sons, and God did not break His word when they did. The proverb never promised you a result you could bank. It handed you a job, told you the ordinary grain of the world runs with it, and left the outcome where it has always been.
