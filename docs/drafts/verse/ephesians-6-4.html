Verse Study · Ephesians

Ephesians 6:4

One sentence, two commands, one man. The prohibition is the half most fathers never think they are breaking.

You fathers, don’t provoke your children to wrath, but nurture them in the discipline and instruction of the Lord.— World English Bible (public domain)

And, ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath: but bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Paul has just spent three verses on children. He spends one on fathers, and it is longer than it looks. The sentence has two halves hinged on a hard but, and both are addressed to the same man.

Notice what each half assumes. The first assumes a father can manufacture rage in his own child — routinely, at close range, without ever raising a hand. Paul does not warn fathers against brutality; he warns them against provocation, which is a subtler crime and a commoner one. A man can keep every rule of his house and still be guilty of this verb. The second half assumes the remedy is not to leave the child alone. The opposite of exasperating a child is not neutrality. It is feeding him.

  • πατήρ patēr · “ye fathers” · Strong's G3962

    Three verses earlier Paul told children to obey their parents — both of them. Here he narrows to the father, not because mothers do no raising, but because the man is answerable for the climate of the house. And this is the word the New Testament uses for God: patēr is Jesus’s primary designation for the One he taught his disciples to address in prayer. Paul is about to say whose discipline this actually is, and he has already put both fathers in the same sentence.

  • παροργίζω parorgizō · “provoke … to wrath” · Strong's G3949

    This is the pivot of the verse. The verb is para — beside, alongside, to the point of — joined to a form of orgē, wrath. To exasperate; to push a person into anger. The KJV splits it across the line as “provoke … to wrath,” which is exactly right: the wrath is the destination and the father is the road. Note where the word is aimed. This is not a warning about angry children. It is a warning about the men who make them.

    The negation attached to it is mē, the Greek negative of will and command rather than the negative of bare fact. Paul is not observing that fathers do not do this. He is forbidding it — reaching past the behavior to the intent behind it.

  • ἐκτρέφω ektrephō · “bring them up” / “nurture” · Strong's G1625

    To nourish to full growth. The prefix ek- carries the sense of all the way out — to feed a thing until it is finished, not until it is quiet. Paul used this verb once already in this letter, eight verses back, of a husband and his own body: no man hates his own flesh, he says there, but nourishes and cherishes it, as the Lord does the church. Same word, same letter, with Christ tending his people as the measure — and that is the standard he then hands a father over his own child. Not management. Feeding, to maturity, at the cost of the one doing the feeding.

  • παιδεία paideia · “the nurture” / “the discipline” · Strong's G3809

    The whole business of raising a child — instruction, correction and discipline held together as one process, not three departments. Hebrews takes the word up to say that the hardship God sends his sons is not abandonment but fatherly training, grievous now and fruitful later. That is the word Paul puts in a father’s hands. Discipline here is not the punishment wing of parenting. It is the shaping of a whole man.

  • νουθεσία nouthesia · “admonition” / “instruction” · Strong's G3559

    Our lexicon puts it in five words: mind-correction — adjusting thinking to truth. It is the spoken half of the pair. Paideia is largely what a father does to form a child; nouthesia is what he says to correct what the child believes. Paul joins the two with a simple and, and the joint is load-bearing. Formation without words leaves a child obedient and uninstructed. Words without formation leave him informed and unchanged.

One more word governs both nouns and settles the whole verse: kyriou — of the Lord. Not a father’s discipline with a Christian coat of paint on it. The Lord’s own discipline, the Lord’s own admonition, administered by a man who did not author either one. That single genitive takes the verse out of the category of technique. A father is not the source of what he is handing his children. He is the delivery.

2. Where It Sits

This verse is the fourth beat of a passage that begins back at Ephesians 5:18. Paul says to be filled with the Spirit, and then, instead of describing an experience, he describes a household — singing, thanksgiving, submitting to one another in the fear of Christ, then wives and husbands, children and fathers, servants and masters. The evidence of a Spirit-filled man in this letter is not what happens to him in a meeting. It is what happens at his table.

Read the three pairs together and a pattern surfaces. In each one, the party with less power is addressed briefly, and the party with more power is addressed at length and given the harder word. Wives get three verses; husbands get nine, and are told to love as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for it. Servants get four verses; masters are told to do the same things to them and to give up threatening. Children get three verses of straightforward command. Fathers get this one — and it is the only place in the passage where a man is told to stop something before he is told to start anything.

What comes immediately before is the fifth commandment, quoted: honor your father and mother, which Paul calls the first commandment with a promise. So verse 4 is not free-standing advice. It is the other side of a commandment God gave. Children owe honor; fathers owe them a father worth honoring. Paul refuses to let the commandment run in one direction only.

What comes immediately after is the armor. Six verses past this one Paul says be strong in the Lord, put on the whole armor of God, our wrestling is not against flesh and blood. There is no break in his argument between the household and the war. The first ground where that armor gets tested is not a public arena. It is a kitchen at the end of a long day, with a child who has asked the same thing four times.

The book runs the same way. Chapters 1 through 3 are what God has done — chosen, raised, seated, reconciled. Chapters 4 through 6 are how to walk, and every command in them draws its power from the first half. This verse is not a parenting tip bolted onto a theology. It is what the theology looks like on a Tuesday.

  • Colossians 3:21

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin’s commentary on Ephesians is not part of this library, so we take him where he is: the Institutes, expounding the fifth commandment — the commandment Paul has just quoted in verse 2. He starts by asking what the command is for.

The end of this commandment is, that since the Lord takes pleasure in the preservation of his own ordinance, the degrees of dignity appointed by him must be held inviolable.

— Calvin, Institutes II.8

So the honor a child owes is not owed to the father’s merit. It is owed to an office God set up. That cuts hard in the child’s direction, and Calvin says so bluntly enough elsewhere. But it cuts back on the father the moment you ask where the office came from — and Calvin does ask.

we are ordered to obey parents only in the Lord. This is clear from the principle already laid down: for the place which they occupy is one to which the Lord has exalted them, by communicating to them a portion of his own honour. Therefore the submission yielded to them should be a step in our ascent to the Supreme Parent, and hence, if they instigate us to transgress the law, they deserve not to be regarded as parents, but as strangers attempting to seduce us from obedience to our true Father.

— Calvin, Institutes II.8

Read that against Paul’s genitive. A father holds a portion of God’s own honor on loan, and the point of the arrangement is that a child climbing toward his father finds himself climbing toward God. So a father who provokes has not merely been unpleasant. He has put a stumbling block on the one staircase he was given to build. Calvin will not soften it: a parent who pulls a child away from God has, in that act, stopped functioning as a parent.

For it were unbecoming and absurd that the honour of God should be impaired by their exaltation—an exaltation which, being derived from him, ought to lead us up to him.

— Calvin, Institutes II.8

That is the Reformed reading of Ephesians 6:4 in one line. Derived authority that does not lead upward is authority working against its own reason for existing. A father is a signpost. A signpost pointed at itself is a hazard.

4. Confessional Anchor

The 1689 Confession cites this verse in its chapter on the communion of saints — an unexpected home for it, until you notice what the paragraph does with the household.

This communion, according to the rule of the gospel, though especially to be exercised by them in the relation wherein they stand — whether in families or in churches — yet, as God offers opportunity, is to be extended to all the household of faith, even all those who in every place call upon the name of the Lord Jesus.

— 1689 LBCF, ch. 27, paragraph 2 (Ephesians 6:4 among its proof-texts)

Families and churches sit in the same clause on purpose. What a father does at his table is not a private warm-up for the real thing on Sunday; it is the same duty, in the first relation a man stands in. Ephesians 6:4 is the communion of saints happening at close range, to people who did not choose you and cannot leave.

The Baptist Catechism is more direct. It gives the home two questions, and the second is very nearly a translation of our verse.

God has appointed the Christian home to be a place of worship, where parents and children together fear the Lord — praying, singing his praise, and feeding on his Word — so that the knowledge of God is kept alive and handed on from one generation to the next.

— The Baptist Catechism, Q115: What has God appointed the Christian home to be?

God requires parents to bring up their children in the instruction and discipline of the Lord: to teach them his Word diligently, to pray for them and with them, and to set before them a godly example — not trusting in their own labor, but in the grace of God to save.

— The Baptist Catechism, Q116: What does God require of parents?

Everything before the dash is the command. Everything after it is the thing that keeps the command from destroying you. A man can teach diligently, pray faithfully, live cleanly — and still not save his child, because saving was never his job. The catechism settles that before a father can start keeping score. He is responsible for the nurture. He is not the one who gives the growth.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the Family Captain memorize pack — carry it. Carrying it does something specific: it puts a command in your pocket that indicts you before it equips you, and there is no other verse in the pack that does that to a father.

Name the provocation you actually commit. Almost no man reading this beats his children, and almost every man reading this provokes them. The common forms are quiet ones. A standard that moves, so the child can never be safe, only lucky. Correction delivered in front of an audience. Mockery filed under humor, which a child cannot appeal because you will say he can’t take a joke. Punishing the confession harder than the offense, which teaches him to hide instead of to repent. And the one that does the most damage with the least noise: a father who is reliably present for the failure and absent for everything else, so the child learns that the only guaranteed way to get his attention is to fail.

Do not confuse fencing with feeding. Rules are cheap. You can issue a rule in four seconds from another room. Ektrephō costs the one thing you actually do not want to spend, which is unhurried time — the drive, the workbench, the long walk where the real question finally surfaces because the child had enough silence to find it. If your ratio of correction to nourishment is honest and you do not like the number, that is the number the verse is asking about.

Use both hands. Some men are all discipline and no admonition: the boy knows exactly what it costs and has never once been told why. He will keep the rules until the day the enforcement stops. Other men are all admonition and no discipline: endless explaining, no consequence, a child who has heard everything and obeyed nothing. Paul gave you both words. Neither one works alone.

Hand on what is not yours. The discipline is of the Lord, which means you are under it too. The practical test is repentance. When you sin against your child — and you will, this week — say so, out loud, to him, without a clause that shifts it back onto what he did first. A father who never apologizes has taught his children that authority sits above repentance. He has also taught them that the gospel he keeps talking about is for people smaller than him.

And then the relief, which the catechism already handed you: not trusting in their own labor, but in the grace of God to save. You are commanded to nurture. You are not commanded to produce the outcome, because you cannot, and a father who takes that weight on will either crush his children trying to guarantee it or quit when it looks like he has failed. Feed them the Lord’s discipline and the Lord’s instruction. Do it for years, without a scoreboard. The growth is His to give.
