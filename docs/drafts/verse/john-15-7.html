Verse Study · John

John 15:7

Men quote the back half of this verse like a blank check. The front half is where the signature is.

If you remain in me, and my words remain in you, you will ask whatever you desire, and it will be done for you.— World English Bible (public domain)

If ye abide in me, and my words abide in you, ye shall ask what ye will, and it shall be done unto you.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The sentence is built in two halves, and the whole difficulty of the verse comes from the fact that men memorize the second and skip the first. Two conditions, then one promise. If you abide in me. And if my words abide in you. Then ask what you will, and it will be done. Strip the two conditions off the front and you are left with a magic formula. Leave them attached and you have something far better and far more demanding — a promise made to a particular kind of man, and a description of what has to be true of him before the promise means anything.

Notice first that the conditions are not two hoops but one reality described from two sides. Three verses earlier Jesus said it the other way around: Remain in me, and I in you (v. 4). Here in verse 7 the second half of that mutual indwelling has changed its wording. Not and I in you, but and my words in you. That is not a downgrade. It is a definition. How does the ascended Christ abide in a man who cannot see him? Through his words, lodged and living in that man. John does not leave this to inference — he says the same thing flat in his first letter: the word of God remains in you, and you have overcome the evil one (1 John 2:14).

And notice second that the promise is not you may ask but something closer to you will ask. A man in whom the words of Christ have taken up residence is a man whose asking has been reshaped by them. The verse is not handing him unlimited leverage over God. It is telling him what will happen to his wanting.

  • μένω menō · abide / remain · Strong's G3306

    To remain, stay, or abide — continuing in a place, relationship, or state. The word appears over forty times in John's Gospel alone, and this discourse is its home ground: eleven occurrences in the first eleven verses of chapter 15.

    It is a settling word, not a striving word. A branch does not clamp itself to the vine by effort; it is where it is, and it stays. But John never lets that become passivity — the same verb is an imperative in verse 4 and a warning in verse 6. Abiding is the one thing commanded of a branch, and it is not a technique. It is the refusal to relocate. What kills it is not weakness but drift: a man who last week wanted what Christ wants and this week has quietly moved his life somewhere else and not noticed.

  • ῥῆμα rhēma · words · Strong's G4487

    A spoken word or specific utterance — more concrete than logos. It is the word behind man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of God (Matthew 4:4) and behind the sword of the Spirit in Ephesians 6:17.

    The plural matters, and so does the choice of noun. Jesus does not say if my message abides in you, which a man could satisfy with a general good opinion of Christianity. He says my words — particular things he actually said, which can be quoted, remembered, obeyed or dodged. This is the difference between a man who admires Christ and a man who can tell you what Christ told him to do. Only one of those two has anything abiding in him.

  • αἰτέω aiteō · ask · Strong's G154

    To ask for something, to make a request. It is distinguished from erōtaō, to inquire or ask a question, and it emphasizes requesting something of someone in authority — the verb Jesus uses again and again when he teaches about prayer.

    This is a beggar's word, not a customer's. It is the word of a child at a father's knee and of a subject before a king, and it presumes the whole distance between the one asking and the One asked. Which is worth remembering, because the promise attached to it in this verse is so large that men instinctively reach for a stronger verb — a claiming, a decreeing. The Greek will not carry it. Whatever the promise is, it is a promise about asking.

  • θέλω thelō · will / desire · Strong's G2309

    To wish, will, want, or desire — an orientation of the will, what a man actively wants. It ranges from casual preference to firm resolve, and it is the verb behind Christ's own prayer in the garden: not what I will, but what you will.

    Here is the hinge of the whole verse. The promise covers whatever you desire, and men read that as though the desiring were a fixed quantity and God had just agreed to underwrite it. But the two conditions have already gone to work on exactly that faculty. Words that abide in a man do not sit in a drawer; they retrain what he wants. The same verb runs through Gethsemane and through Romans 7, where Paul cannot get his willing and his doing to agree. Verse 7 is the answer to that civil war, not an exemption from it.

  • γίνομαι ginomai · it shall be done · Strong's G1096

    To become, to come into being, to happen, to be made. It is the verb of Genesis in Greek — let there be light, and light came into being — and the verb of the incarnation: the Word became flesh.

    The English "it shall be done unto you" sounds like an order being filled. The Greek is heavier than that. It is the verb of things coming into existence. What the abiding man asks for does not get approved; it happens. And the passive is the reverent one — the doer is not named, because everyone in the room knows who it is.

Two smaller things repay attention. The conditional particle is ἐάν (ean, G1437), the ordinary Greek conjunction for a condition that is genuinely possible — not a wistful if only, and not a foregone conclusion either. Jesus is describing something these men can actually be, and can actually fail to be; verse 6 has just described the failure. And every "you" in the verse is plural. This is not a private key handed to an individual mystic. It is said to a room, and the letters that later work the same promise say whatever we ask. The asking Christ has in view happens among his people, not off by yourself in a corner with a technique.

2. Where It Sits

It is the same night as John 14. Judas has gone out. The feet have been washed, the betrayal named, the denial predicted. Jesus is walking these men through the last hours of the only arrangement they have ever known with him — his body in the room, his voice in the air — and telling them that the arrangement is about to change and the relationship is not. Chapter 14 answered that with a promise of presence: another Counselor, the Spirit of truth, I will not leave you orphans. Chapter 15 gives them a picture for it.

John 15:5 — I am the vine. You are the branches. He who remains in me, and I in him, the same bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing.

The image is not decorative. Israel had been God's vine in the prophets, and every time the prophets used it they used it to indict — a vine that yielded wild grapes, a vine planted for fruit and producing none. Jesus takes the national symbol and says I am the true vine. He is not a branch of Israel. He is the vine itself, and the only way anyone now belongs to the people of God is by being in him.

Which means the verb abide in verse 7 is not a mood. It is a location. And the whole paragraph is about what a branch can and cannot do:

John 15:3 — You are already pruned clean because of the word which I have spoken to you.

John 15:4 — Remain in me, and I in you. As the branch can't bear fruit by itself, unless it remains in the vine, so neither can you, unless you remain in me.

John 15:6 — If a man doesn't remain in me, he is thrown out as a branch, and is withered; and they gather them, throw them into the fire, and they are burned.

Verse 3 is the one men skip, and it governs everything after it. Before a single command to abide, before any promise about asking, Jesus tells them they are already clean — and he tells them why: because of the word he has spoken to them. The word did the cleaning first. So when verse 7 asks that his words abide in them, it is not introducing a new requirement. It is asking them to go on living in the thing that already made them what they are.

Then verse 6 puts the knife on the table. This is not a promise floating free of consequence. There is a way to not abide, and it ends in a fire. And immediately after our verse, verse 8 tells you what all that asking is for:

John 15:8 — In this is my Father glorified, that you bear much fruit; and so you will be my disciples.

Fruit, and the Father's glory. That is the purpose clause the promise sits inside. Nine verses later Jesus says the same thing again with the missing piece filled in — you didn't choose me, but I chose you, and appointed you, that you should go and bear fruit… that whatever you will ask of the Father in my name, he may give it to you (v. 16). The asking serves the fruit-bearing. It is a working man's tool, issued for a job.

The rest of the New Testament reads verse 7 exactly this way, and never once as a blank check:

  • John 14:13

    Whatever you will ask in my name, that will I do, that the Father may be glorified in the Son.

  • John 16:24

    Until now, you have asked nothing in my name. Ask, and you will receive, that your joy may be made full.

  • 1 John 3:22

    and whatever we ask, we receive from him, because we keep his commandments and do the things that are pleasing in his sight.

  • 1 John 5:14

    This is the boldness which we have toward him, that, if we ask anything according to his will, he listens to us.

  • James 4:3

    You ask, and don't receive, because you ask with wrong motives, so that you may spend it for your pleasures.

Every one of them attaches a qualifier to the same enormous promise: in my name, according to his will, because we keep his commandments, that the Father may be glorified. John 15:7 is not the odd one out. Its qualifier is simply put at the front instead of the back, and it is put in the form of a condition of life rather than a formula of words. Which is harder, and more honest.

One more line belongs here, from earlier in the same Gospel: If you remain in my word, then you are truly my disciples (John 8:31). Same verb, same object, and there the abiding is the test of whether a professed faith is real at all. Verse 7 is not an advanced technique for elite Christians. It is a description of an ordinary one.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin — nothing is ours until he is

Calvin's commentary on John is not held in this repository, but the doctrine underneath verse 7 is the opening move of Book III of the Institutes, and he states it with the bluntness the verse deserves. Everything Christ did remains useless to a man who is not in him:

the first thing to be attended to is, that so long as we are without Christ and separated from him, nothing which he suffered and did for the salvation of the human race is of the least benefit to us. To communicate to us the blessings which he received from the Father, he must become ours and dwell in us.

Calvin, Institutes III.1 — The Benefits of Christ Made Available to Us by the Secret Operation of the Spirit

That is the vine and the branches in prose. Not Christ did enough for you, but Christ must be yours, and the difference between those two sentences is the difference between a severed branch and a living one. And on the second condition — the words abiding — Calvin will not let a man count head knowledge as possession:

The word is not received in faith when it merely flutters in the brain, but when it has taken deep root in the heart, and become an invincible bulwark to withstand and repel all the assaults of temptation.

Calvin, Institutes III.2 — Of Faith

Flutters in the brain. That is the state of most Bible reading, and it is exactly what verse 7 does not mean by abiding. A word that abides is one that has gone down far enough to hold weight when something leans on it.

Calvin — prayer as the digging up of a fortune

Calvin's chapter on prayer is the longest in the Institutes, and its governing image is worth setting beside this verse. To know that God holds all good and then not ask him for any of it is not humility. It is waste:

To know God as the sovereign disposer of all good, inviting us to present our requests, and yet not to approach or ask of him, were so far from availing us, that it were just as if one told of a treasure were to allow it to remain buried in the ground.

prayer digs up those treasures which the Gospel of our Lord discovers to the eye of faith.

Calvin, Institutes III.20 — Of Prayer

So the promise in verse 7 is not a hazard to be defused. It is meant to be used, and a man who reads it and asks God for nothing has failed it as surely as the man who reads it and asks for a boat.

Calvin — the rule that keeps asking from becoming appetite

But then Calvin says the thing that the second condition of our verse says, and he says it in the same order Jesus does — word first, then asking:

since it is only in faith that God desires to be invoked, and he distinctly enjoins us to frame our prayers according to the rule of his word: in fine, since faith is founded on the word, and is the parent of right prayer, the moment we decline from the word, our prayers are impure.

Calvin, Institutes III.20 — Of Prayer

The moment we decline from the word. That is verse 7 read backwards: where the words are not abiding, the asking has already gone wrong, whatever the man happens to feel while he is doing it. And Calvin is unsparing about what unregulated asking actually looks like, because he had evidently listened to a lot of it:

men in prayer give greater license to their unlawful desires than if they were telling jocular tales among their equals. God does not suffer his condescension to be thus mocked, but vindicating his own light, places our wishes under the restraint of his authority.

Calvin, Institutes III.20 — Of Prayer

A man will say things to God in prayer he would be embarrassed to say to his friends over a beer. The condition in verse 7 is the cure for that, and the cure is not a shorter list of permitted requests. It is a rearranged appetite. Calvin makes the same point about the man who prays outside that rule:

Those who, disregarding the Master's rule, indulge their own wishes, not only have not the word of God, but as much as in them lies oppose it.

Calvin, Institutes III.20 — Of Prayer

Calvin — on the answer that does not look like one

And because the promise is stated so absolutely — it shall be done unto you — Calvin's honesty about the experience of praying is a mercy. God answers; God does not always answer in the shape of the request:

we must in all our prayers carefully avoid wishing to confine God to certain circumstances, or prescribe to him the time, place, or mode of action.

although God complies with our request, he does not always give an answer in the very terms of our prayers but while apparently holding us in suspense, yet in an unknown way, shows that our prayers have not been in vain.

Calvin, Institutes III.20 — Of Prayer

He also names, exactly, the failure mode of a man who has taken verse 7 as a contract:

Many have a practice of formally bargaining with God on certain conditions, and, as if he were the servant of their lust, binding him to certain stipulations; with which if he do not immediately comply, they are indignant and fretful, murmur, complain, and make a noise.

Calvin, Institutes III.20 — Of Prayer

That is what this verse turns into in the hands of a man who took the promise and left the conditions on the table. And the alternative Calvin offers is not lowered expectation but a longer horizon: deferring our hope with well tempered equanimity, let us insist with that perseverance which is so strongly recommended to us in Scripture.

4. Confessional Anchor

The 1689 Confession does cite this paragraph of John — chapter 16, on good works, rests on John 15:4–5, the two verses immediately before ours. The divines went to the vine and the branches to answer a question about ability:

Their ability to do good works is not at all of themselves, but wholly from the Spirit of Christ; and that they may be enabled thereunto, besides the graces they have already received, there is necessary an actual influence of the same Holy Spirit to work in them to will and to do of his good pleasure

1689 LBCF, ch. 16, para. 3 — Of Good Works

Note the phrase to will and to do. Even the wanting is worked in a man from outside himself. Which is precisely why the promise about whatever you desire is not reckless: in a branch that is actually in the vine, the desiring is itself downstream of the Spirit.

On the asking half of the verse, the Confession's paragraph on prayer is the anchor, and it reads like a summary of everything the New Testament attaches to this promise:

Prayer, with thanksgiving, being one part of natural worship, is by God required of all men. But that it may be accepted, it is to be made in the name of the Son, by the help of the Spirit, according to his will — with understanding, reverence, humility, fervency, faith, love, and perseverance; and when with others, in a known tongue.

1689 LBCF, ch. 22, para. 3 — Of Religious Worship and the Sabbath Day

Its proof-texts are John 14:13–14, Romans 8:26, and 1 John 5:14 — the sibling verses of ours. And the next paragraph adds the plain limit that spares a man a great deal of confusion:

Prayer is to be made for things lawful, and for all sorts of men living, or that shall live hereafter

1689 LBCF, ch. 22, para. 4 — Of Religious Worship and the Sabbath Day

The Baptist Catechism compresses the whole business into one sentence, and it is worth learning alongside the verse itself, because it is John 15:7 with the conditions built into the definition:

Prayer is an offering up of our desires to God, by the assistance of the Holy Spirit, for things agreeable to his will, in the name of Christ, believing, with confession of our sins, and thankful acknowledgment of his mercies.

The Baptist Catechism, Q105 — What is prayer?

Our desires — the catechism does not pretend a man comes to God with no wants of his own. But for things agreeable to his will, and offered by the assistance of the Holy Spirit. Then the very next question asks where a man learns which desires those are, and the answer is our verse's second condition in confessional dress:

The whole Word of God is of use to direct us in prayer; but the special rule of direction is that prayer which Christ taught his disciples, commonly called the Lord's Prayer.

The Baptist Catechism, Q106 — What rule has God given for our direction in prayer?

And on how the words come to abide in a man in the first place, the catechism is entirely practical:

That the Word may become effectual to salvation, we must attend to it with diligence, preparation, and prayer; receive it with faith and love, lay it up in our hearts, and practice it in our lives.

The Baptist Catechism, Q95 — How is the Word to be read and heard, that it may become effectual to salvation?

Lay it up in our hearts, and practice it in our lives. That is abiding, described as a set of things a man does on a Tuesday.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in The Wheel memorize pack — carry it. It is the Prayer spoke, and it is the one verse in that pack that holds two spokes at once: the Word abiding, and the asking that follows. Which is the point of the diagram. A wheel with a Word spoke and no Prayer spoke is a man who studies and never asks; a wheel with a Prayer spoke and no Word spoke is a man who asks and does not know what he is asking for. This verse bolts them together.

Stop quoting the second half. If John 15:7 comes to mind when you want something, you have learned it backwards. It is not a verse about getting. It is a verse about being in a position from which asking makes sense. The test is not whether you got the thing. The test is the two conditions, and you can check both without waiting on an outcome.

Audit the second condition, because it is the checkable one. Abiding in Christ is hard to measure from the inside; a man's sense of his own communion with God swings with his sleep. But do the words of Christ abide in you is answerable in about a minute. Can you say back, without looking, five things he actually said? Not five doctrines about him — five sentences out of his mouth. If not, that is not a mystery to pray about. It is a gap with an obvious remedy, and the catechism already named it: attend to the Word with diligence, lay it up in your heart, practice it in your life.

Expect your wanting to change, and let that be the evidence. The promise is ask whatever you desire, and men treat that as the risky part. It is actually the mechanism. Words that live in a man reshape what he wants, slowly and without a ceremony, until the things he asks God for are things God was already committed to. That is why the promise can be stated so absolutely. If your prayer list looks exactly the same as it did five years ago, that is worth knowing — not as an occasion for guilt, but as a reading on a gauge.

Then actually ask, and ask big. The error runs both directions. Calvin's line about the buried treasure is aimed at the cautious man, and it is a harder word than it looks: to know that God holds every good thing, to be invited to ask, and to ask for nothing, is not modesty. A man who is genuinely abiding and has genuinely let the words in has been handed an astonishing license, and the appropriate response is not to be careful with it. Ask for the conversion of the man you work next to. Ask for your children's faith. Ask for the thing that would take an act of God.

And when the answer does not come in the shape you asked for. It often will not. Calvin's honesty is the sane word here: God does not always give an answer in the very terms of our prayers. That is not the promise failing. It is the promise being kept by someone who can see the whole board. The failure mode to avoid is the one he described — bargaining, then indignation when the terms are not met on schedule. The alternative is not to want less. It is to wait longer.

Last, remember what the asking is for. Verse 8 is the purpose clause: in this is my Father glorified, that you bear much fruit. The promise is issued to a working branch. It is not a private amenity; it is the supply line for a job you were appointed to do. A man who understands that will find that a surprising number of his prayers get answered — because he has started asking for what the vine was always growing him to produce.
