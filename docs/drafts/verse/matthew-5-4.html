Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:4

The most obviously false sentence in the Sermon on the Mount, unless you know which mourning He means.

Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted.— World English Bible (public domain)

Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Read it cold and it is offensive. Blessed are those who mourn. Say that at a graveside and you would deserve what you got.

But the beatitudes are not eight independent maxims; they are a portrait deepening, and this one follows directly from the last. The man of verse 3 has just discovered he is spiritually bankrupt. Verse 4 is what happens next in an honest man. He does not shrug. He grieves.

So the mourning here is not bereavement in general, though the promise reaches that too. It is the specific sorrow of a man who has seen what he is.

  • μακάριος makarios · “Blessed” · Strong’s G3107

    The Greek word for blessedness — not emotional happiness dependent on circumstances, but the deep well-being of those who are right with God. The Beatitudes (Matt 5) open with makarios.

    Makarios again, and this verse is the proof that it cannot mean “happy.” Nobody is cheerful while mourning, and Jesus is not asking anyone to pretend otherwise. Makarios is the well-being of a man rightly related to God — a status, not a mood — which is why it can be pronounced over someone in tears.

  • πενθέω Pentheo · “are they that mourn” · Strong’s G3996

    The Greek verb pentheo means to mourn, grieve, or lament — often with a deeper, more settled grief than klaio (weeping). It describes the mourning of bereavement, of deep remorse, and of spiritual grief over sin.

    Pentheo is the strongest mourning word available. Greek has klaio for weeping, the audible kind. Pentheo is deeper and more settled — the grief of bereavement, the sorrow that changes how a man carries himself rather than merely how he sounds.

    It is the word used of the grief that will not be hidden. Which rules out the polite religious version of this beatitude, where a man mentions his sinfulness in general terms and moves on.

  • παρακαλέω parakaleo · “shall be comforted” · Strong’s G3870

    Parakaleo (G3870) covers the full pastoral range: to comfort the grieving, to exhort the sluggish, to urge the wavering, to appeal to the hard-hearted. It shares its root with parakletos — the Comforter/Advocate, the Holy Spirit.

    Parakaleo is the great pastoral verb, covering the whole range: to comfort the grieving, to exhort the sluggish, to urge the wavering, to plead with the hard. It shares a root with parakletos — the name Jesus gives the Holy Spirit in John’s Gospel, the Comforter, the one called alongside.

    That connection is the promise’s content. They shall be comforted is a divine passive: someone will do the comforting, and the New Testament names Him. The comfort is not a feeling that eventually arrives; it is a Person who is sent.

The tense that keeps it honest

Shall be comforted — future. Unlike verse 3, this promise is not present. Jesus does not say the mourning stops now, and He does not say the mourner will feel better this week.

That reticence is worth trusting. A great deal of Christian consolation goes wrong by promising the present tense — that if you have enough faith the grief lifts. Jesus is more careful. He blesses the man in the mourning and puts the comfort in the future, which is exactly where Revelation puts it: God shall wipe away all tears, and there shall be no more sorrow.

2. Where It Sits

The Sermon opens with Jesus seated on a mountain, disciples around Him, the crowds from four regions listening in. Matthew has spent four chapters presenting Him as the new Moses, and the first words of the new law are blessings rather than commands.

Our verse is second in the sequence, and the sequence is the argument: bankrupt (v. 3), grieving over it (v. 4), no longer asserting yourself (v. 5), hungry for a righteousness you do not have (v. 6). Only then do the outward-facing ones arrive — merciful, pure, peacemaking.

There is also an Old Testament passage standing behind this. Isaiah 61, which Jesus reads aloud in the synagogue at Nazareth and applies to Himself, promises to comfort all who mourn, to give them beauty for ashes and the oil of joy for mourning. Matthew’s Greek here echoes that promise closely enough that the original hearers, who knew Isaiah, would have caught it.

So the beatitude is not a new idea. It is Jesus claiming that the comfort Isaiah promised has arrived, and identifying who gets it.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition read this beatitude alongside Paul’s distinction in 2 Corinthians 7 between godly sorrow, which works repentance unto salvation, and the sorrow of the world, which works death.

That distinction is the whole pastoral usefulness of the verse. Both look like grief over sin from the outside. The difference is direction: godly sorrow moves a man toward God and ends in relief; worldly sorrow moves him in on himself and ends in despair or in self-punishment, which is despair with a religious face.

The tradition also insisted, following the confession, that this mourning is not a season a Christian graduates from. Repentance is lifelong. A man who has stopped grieving over sin has not usually improved; he has more often stopped looking.

4. Confessional Anchor

This saving repentance is an evangelical grace, whereby a person being by the Holy Spirit made sensible of the manifold evils of his sin, does, by faith in Christ, humble himself for it with godly sorrow, detestation of it, and self-abhorrence, praying for pardon and strength of grace, with a purpose and endeavor by supplies of the Spirit, to walk before God unto all well-pleasing in all things.

1689 LBCF, ch. 15 — Of Repentance Unto Life and Salvation

Godly sorrow is the confession’s name for the mourning in this beatitude, and notice how much company it keeps. It is not left alone. It stands with faith in Christ, with praying for pardon, and with a purpose and endeavor… to walk before God.

That is the safeguard. Sorrow by itself is not the beatitude; sorrow inside a movement toward God is. The confession will not let a man sit in self-abhorrence as though the sitting were the virtue.

Such is the provision which God has made through Christ in the covenant of grace for the preservation of believers unto salvation, that although there is no sin so small but it deserves damnation, yet there is no sin so great that it shall bring damnation on them that repent.

1689 LBCF, ch. 15 — Of Repentance Unto Life and Salvation

And there is the comfort, in doctrinal form. The mourning is warranted — no sin so small but it deserves damnation is the reason a man grieves at all. The comfort is not that it was less serious than he thought.

5. For the Man Reading This

Learn to tell the two sorrows apart, because the counterfeit will consume years. Ask where the grief is pointing. Godly sorrow says I have sinned against God and I want to go to Him about it, and it moves. Worldly sorrow says I am the kind of man who does this, and it circles — replaying the failure, rehearsing the disgust, never once actually addressing God.

The second one feels more serious. It is not. It is a way of paying for the thing yourself, and it keeps you exactly where you are.

Then let the future tense do its work. If you are in real grief today — over sin, or over a loss, because this promise is wide enough for both — Jesus does not tell you the comfort is available now if you access it correctly. He tells you it is coming, and who is bringing it. Some seasons the whole of faith is agreeing that the sentence is true while nothing about your circumstances has changed.

And do not try to manufacture this. Like poverty of spirit, mourning that is worked up is a performance. The men who fulfil this beatitude are almost never trying to; they are simply looking honestly at something and reacting the way an honest man reacts. The blessing is pronounced over them while they are still crying.

In the Sermon on the Mount memorize pack — carry it.
