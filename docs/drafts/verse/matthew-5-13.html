Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:13

Christ does not tell His disciples to become salt. He tells them what they already are — and then tells them, in one sentence, what the world does with salt that has stopped being salt.

“You are the salt of the earth, but if the salt has lost its flavor, with what will it be salted? It is then good for nothing, but to be cast out and trodden under the feet of men.— World English Bible (public domain)

Ye are the salt of the earth: but if the salt have lost his savour, wherewith shall it be salted? it is thenceforth good for nothing, but to be cast out, and to be trodden under foot of men.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The first thing to see is the mood of the verb. This is not a command. It is a statement of fact: you are. Whatever a disciple is supposed to do about it, he is not being asked to manufacture the status. He already has it, handed to him by the same voice that just finished pronouncing him blessed. Everything the verse asks of him afterward runs downhill from that.

The second thing to see is the shape of the sentence. One indicative, then a condition, then a verdict handed down in three stages. Good for nothing. Cast out. Trodden under foot. The Lord does not soften the landing, and He does not include a route back. In the middle of it sits a question — wherewith shall it be salted? — that the verse never answers, because in the world of the picture there is no answer. You can salt meat. You cannot salt salt.

  • ὑμεῖς humeis · "Ye" · Strong's G5210

    The second person plural personal pronoun — "you all." English merges the singular and the plural into one word and loses the distinction; Greek keeps it. Humeis addresses a community, and it stresses collective identity and responsibility.

    Greek does not need the pronoun at all here — the verb already carries the person. So its presence is emphasis, and its number is the point. The sentence is not "each of you, privately, should be salty." It is a word spoken over a body of men together, the same way the next verse says you are the light of the world. A grain of salt on its own does nothing you would notice. Salt works in a quantity, worked through a mass.

  • ἅλας halas · "the salt" · Strong's G217

    Salt. In the ancient world it was precious — worth its weight in trade, used to preserve food, seal covenants, and purify offerings.

    Three jobs, and the picture is poorer if you keep only one. Salt preserves, which means it works against rot rather than for growth; its usefulness is measured in what does not happen. Salt seasons, which means it is noticed — the whole complaint against a saltless disciple is that nobody can taste him. And salt seals: Israel was ordered to put it on every grain offering and told plainly not to leave the salt of the covenant out. Whatever else the disciples heard in this word, they heard the altar.

  • γῆ gē · "of the earth" · Strong's G1093

    One of the commonest nouns in the New Testament, carrying three senses: the physical earth as the habitat of men, a particular land or region, and the ground or soil itself. It renders the Hebrew erets throughout the Greek Old Testament.

    Note the address. Not the salt of the synagogue, not the salt of the congregation — the salt of the earth. Salt is not stored for its own sake; it is applied to something that would go bad without it. The genitive puts the disciple out where the spoilage is. And gē is the ordinary, dirty, agricultural word: soil, ground, the place where things are grown and where things rot. That is the assignment.

  • μωραίνω mōrainō · "have lost his savour" · Strong's G3471

    To make foolish, or to become foolish. It is kin to mōros, the fool. Applied to salt, the KJV renders the picture have lost his savour. Salt that has gone foolish is salt that no longer does what salt is for.

    This is the word to sit with. Greek had perfectly good vocabulary for going flat, weakening, losing strength. Christ reaches instead for the verb He uses of men who build on sand and virgins who bring no oil. The failure is not chemical, it is moral: a thing that has forgotten its own purpose and does not know it. Which is exactly how it happens. No man decides to stop being salt. He simply stops tasting like anything, one accommodation at a time, and keeps the name.

  • καταπατέω katapateō · "to be trodden under foot" · Strong's G2662

    To tread down, to trample under foot — kata, down, plus pateō, to walk or tread.

    This is not accidental scuffing. It is contempt. What men walk on is what they have already judged worthless, and the picture is deliberate: the spoiled salt goes out the door onto the path, where it is useful for nothing except not being mud. Luke's version of the saying is even harder — the stuff is fit neither for the soil nor for the manure pile. Even the dunghill has standards.

One more verb finishes the sentence. "Wherewith shall it be salted" is halizō, the verb built directly on halas — to salt, to season with salt, and in the Old Testament's usage, to mark a thing as set apart. Grammatically it is a fair question. Practically it is a closed door. Salt is the last agent in the chain; there is nothing further back to treat it with. Christ asks the question and moves on, and the silence where the answer should be is the whole force of the warning.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 5:12 — Rejoice, and be exceedingly glad, for great is your reward in heaven. For that is how they persecuted the prophets who were before you.

Matthew 5:14 — You are the light of the world. A city located on a hill can’t be hidden.

The Beatitudes have just ended, and they ended badly — on persecution, insult, and slander told for Christ's sake. A reasonable man, having heard that, would start looking for cover. Verse 13 is the Lord's answer to the impulse, and it arrives before the impulse has time to form. You have just been told you will be hated. Now be told what you are: the preservative in the very society that is going to hate you.

The placement is the argument. Salt cannot do its work at a distance. It has to be in contact with the meat, which is to say the disciple has to stay in the room with people who do not want him there. Withdrawal is not an available reading of this text. Neither is dissolving into the room, since salt that cannot be tasted is the failure the verse describes. Contact and contrast, both at once, and there is no way to have either one cheaply.

Verse 14 pairs with it: salt of the earth, light of the world. The images are complementary rather than repetitive. Salt is hidden and works by penetration; light is visible and works by exposure. One holds back decay, the other reveals what is there. Then the paragraph closes at verse 16 with the purpose of both — that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven. That is the only end permitted. The disciple is not distinct so that he can be admired for his distinctness; the whole apparatus is aimed past him at his Father.

And immediately after that comes verse 17, where the Lord insists He has not come to destroy the law but to fulfil it, followed by a long stretch of teaching that makes the law heavier rather than lighter. That is the flavor. A church that has quietly negotiated the Sermon on the Mount down to something the world already agrees with has not become winsome. It has become tasteless, and this verse tells it what happens next.

The salt image was not new to the men on that hillside. Scripture had been handing it to them their whole lives:

  • Leviticus 2:13

    Every offering of your meal offering you shall season with salt. You shall not allow the salt of the covenant of your God to be lacking from your meal offering. With all your offerings you shall offer salt.

  • Numbers 18:19

    All the wave offerings of the holy things, which the children of Israel offer to the LORD, I have given you, and your sons and your daughters with you, as a portion forever. It is a covenant of salt forever before the LORD to you and to your offspring with you.

  • 2 Chronicles 13:5

    Ought you not to know that the LORD, the God of Israel, gave the kingdom over Israel to David forever, even to him and to his sons by a covenant of salt?

  • Mark 9:50

    Salt is good, but if the salt has lost its saltiness, with what will you season it? Have salt in yourselves, and be at peace with one another.”

  • Luke 14:35

    It is fit neither for the soil nor for the manure pile. It is thrown out. He who has ears to hear, let him hear.”

  • Colossians 4:6

    Let your speech always be with grace, seasoned with salt, that you may know how you ought to answer each one.

A "covenant of salt" is a covenant that does not spoil. That is the background note under Christ's warning and it is what makes the warning bite: salt is the standing biblical figure for what lasts. Salt that goes bad is a contradiction in terms — the one substance whose job was permanence, and it did not keep. Paul takes the same image into the mouth: speech seasoned with salt is speech with grace in it and something in it, gracious and pointed at once, which is a good deal harder than being one or the other.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin comes at this verse from an angle most modern readers do not expect. He reads it first as a statement about the ministry — the preaching office — and he uses it to argue that the office is not optional furniture but the means God has appointed to keep a church alive at all:

For neither are the light and heat of the sun, nor meat and drink, so necessary to sustain and cherish the present life, as is the apostolical and pastoral office to preserve a Church in the earth.

— Calvin, Institutes IV.3, §2 (Beveridge, 1845)

Notice the verb he chose: preserve. That is the salt's job, moved indoors. In the next section he lists the honors God has put on that office, and our verse is one of them:

He declares, that in raising up teachers, he confers a special benefit on men … when he calls the apostles the light of the world and the salt of the earth

— Calvin, Institutes IV.3, §3 (Beveridge, 1845)

He says the same thing again when he is defining what an apostle is and is not permitted to do — that they are commended by many distinguished titles, the Light of the world and the Salt of the earth, and then that they have no license to speak their own pleasure but must faithfully deliver the commands of the One who sent them. (Institutes IV.8, §4 →) The title is a dignity and a leash in the same breath.

It is worth being honest that Calvin's application is narrower than Matthew's setting. In the Institutes he presses the verse on ministers; on the hillside it is spoken to disciples. Both are true, and the narrower reading sharpens the wider one rather than replacing it. If the men who handle the Word are salt in a concentrated form, then a congregation gets its savour where the preaching is faithful, and loses it where the preaching has gone agreeable.

Which is exactly the use Calvin makes of it when he turns from theory to the men actually holding office in the church of his day. He has just finished describing bishoprics held by the ignorant and by boys, and rectors who no more think of feeding the flock than a cobbler thinks of ploughing. Then he asks the question this verse hands him:

But if we descend to conduct, where is that light of the world which Christ requires, where the salt of the earth, where that sanctity which might operate as a perpetual censorship?

— Calvin, Institutes IV.5, §14 (Beveridge, 1845)

He answers it himself, at length and without charity, cataloguing the luxury and licentiousness of the order and observing that the world was disgusted enough with them that he need not exaggerate. That is Matthew 5:13 used the way Christ meant it — not as a compliment to the church, but as a measure held against her, with the trampling already underway.

And in one aside Calvin says plainly what he thinks the savour actually is. He is dismantling the rite of anointing with oil, and he ends the argument with the image from this verse:

Their unction, therefore, is without savour; it wants salt, that is, the word of God.

— Calvin, Institutes IV.19, §31 (Beveridge, 1845)

Take that gloss seriously and the parable stops being vague. What makes a disciple taste like anything is not intensity, sincerity, or personality. It is the Word of God in him. A man does not lose his savour by getting tired; he loses it by getting empty. And a ministry can keep every ceremony it ever had, perform all of them correctly, and still be — Calvin's word — without savour, because the one ingredient was left out.

4. Confessional Anchor

No confessional paragraph cites this verse as its proof-text, but the doctrine it belongs to is not hard to find. The confession files the savour under good works, and it takes care to say what they are for.

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 16, Paragraph 2

"These good works, done in obedience to God's commandments, are the fruits and evidences of a true and lively faith; and by them believers manifest their thankfulness, strengthen their assurance, edify their brethren, adorn the profession of the gospel, stop the mouths of the adversaries, and glorify God; whose workmanship they are, created in Christ Jesus thereunto, that having their fruit unto holiness they may have the end eternal life."

Read Chapter 16, Of Good Works →

Among the proof-texts hung on that paragraph is Matthew 5:16 — the verse three lines down from ours, where the good works are seen and the Father is glorified. So the divines read the salt-and-light paragraph as one unit and drew the same conclusion from it that we did above: the works are the taste, and the taste is aimed at God's glory, not the disciple's reputation. Note also the order. Fruits and evidences of a true and lively faith. Salt does not become salt by salting. It salts because it is salt.

The corporate half of the warning has its own paragraph, and the confession does not blink at it:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 26, Paragraph 3

"The purest churches under heaven are subject to mixture and error; and some have so degenerated as to become no churches of Christ, but synagogues of Satan. Nevertheless, Christ always has had, and ever shall have, a kingdom in this world unto the end of it, of such as believe in him and make profession of his name."

Read Chapter 26, Of the Church →

That is Matthew 5:13 written as ecclesiology. A congregation can keep the name, the building, the letterhead and the calendar, and be no church of Christ at all — savourless salt, still called salt out of habit. The confession says it about assemblies that once had a real profession. Nobody is exempt by pedigree.

But it refuses to end in despair, and so does the catechism. If the question the verse leaves open is wherewith shall it be salted, the answer is not found in the salt:

Baptist Catechism · Question 38

Q. What is sanctification?
A. "Sanctification is the work of God's free grace, by which we are renewed in the whole man after the image of God, and are enabled more and more to die to sin, and live to righteousness."

Work through the Catechism →

The work of God's free grace. Not the achievement of a determined man. Which is why the warning in verse 13 is meant to drive a disciple toward God rather than into himself: the flavor was never his own property, and the way back is the way he came.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the Sermon on the Mount memorize pack — carry it. Carrying it does one specific thing: it keeps a sentence in your mouth that grades you by usefulness rather than by comfort, which is not the grade you will give yourself.

You have already been made salt; act like it. The verse is an indicative before it is anything else. You are not working toward a status, and you cannot earn one. That kills two errors at once — the man who thinks his distinctness is his own achievement, and the man who thinks he has not yet qualified to be any use. Neither of you gets to sit down.

Contact is not optional, and neither is contrast. Salt in the shaker preserves nothing. Salt stirred into the meat until it cannot be tasted preserves nothing either. Both failures look responsible from the inside — one calls itself holiness, the other calls itself relevance. Ask a plain question about your week: is there anyone who is not a Christian who has actually been near you? And has anything you said to them cost you a thing?

The way you lose savour is by degrees, and it is called being reasonable. The verb Christ used means to become foolish. That is not how it feels while it is happening. It feels like maturing, like picking your battles, like learning not to be difficult. A man rarely renounces anything. He just stops mentioning it, then stops meaning it, and keeps the name on the church roll the whole time.

Know which kind of contempt you are getting. Two verses back, being reviled for Christ's sake is called blessed and gets a reward in heaven. Here, being trodden under foot is the fate of the useless. They look similar from the outside and they are opposites. If men despise you because your loyalty to Christ is inconvenient, rejoice — verse 12 is yours. If men despise you because you are a professing Christian who makes no difference to anything, that is verse 13, and the answer to it is repentance, not encouragement.

The savour comes from the Word, so go get some. Calvin's gloss is the practical one. If what makes salt salt is the Word of God, then a saltless season in a man's life is almost never fixed by trying harder to be distinctive. It is fixed by sitting under preaching that has not been sanded smooth, and by reading the book itself until it starts arguing with you again. That is slower than a resolution and it is the only thing that works.

And the hard edge: this verse assumes you are being watched by men who will make a judgment. That is not vanity, it is the situation. Your neighbors, your crew, your children are all forming a verdict about whether the thing you claim to believe does anything. Christ says they will either taste it or trample it. He does not offer a third outcome where they politely ignore you.
