Verse Study · 1 John

1 John 1:9

The most-used verse in Christian confession, and the strangest word in it is “just.” Not merciful. Just.

If we confess our sins, he is faithful and righteous to forgive us the sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.— World English Bible (public domain)

If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

You would expect the sentence to run: if we confess our sins, He is merciful to forgive us. That is how mercy language usually works, and it would be true. John writes something else. He is faithful and just — and the second adjective changes the whole character of the promise.

Justice is not what you appeal to when you are guilty. Justice is the thing you are hoping to avoid. John says it is the ground of your forgiveness, which only makes sense if the debt has already been settled somewhere else. Read the previous verses and you find it: the blood of Jesus His Son cleanses us from all sin.

So the logic is: the price was paid, therefore refusing to forgive a confessing sinner would be unjust — it would charge twice for the same debt.

  • ὁμολογέω homologeo · “we confess” · Strong’s G3670

    To say the same thing — from homos (same) + legō (to say). Confession is agreeing with God's assessment: of sin (1 John 1:9), of Christ (Rom 10:9), and of truth before others.

    Homologeo is homos (same) plus lego (to say) — to say the same thing. Confession in Scripture is not informing God of news, and it is not performing remorse. It is agreement. You stop arguing and say about your sin what God says about it.

    That definition removes a great deal of anxiety about doing confession properly. The question is not whether you were sorry enough or eloquent enough. The question is whether you agreed.

  • πιστός pistos · “faithful” · Strong’s G4103

    Pistos (πιστός) is the adjective form of the pist- word family (cf. pistis, G4102, faith; pisteuō, G4100, to believe).

    Pistos — faithful, reliable, trustworthy. The kind of thing you can put weight on. John grounds the promise in God’s consistency, not in the impressiveness of your repentance. He is faithful; that is the guarantee, and it does not fluctuate with the quality of your contrition.

  • δίκαιος dikaios · “just” · Strong’s G1342

    Righteous, just, conforming to God's standard. Dikaios describes one who is in right standing — either through personal conduct or through God's declaration.

    Dikaios — righteous, just, conforming to God’s standard. This is the word that makes the verse extraordinary. Forgiveness here is not God relaxing His standard; it is God upholding it. The cross means that mercy toward the confessing sinner and justice about sin are now the same act.

  • ἀφίημι Aphiēmi · “forgive” · Strong’s G863

    The Greek verb aphiēmi means to send away, release, let go, leave, permit, or forgive. In its theological usage it is the primary New Testament verb for forgiveness — the divine act of sending away sin, releasing the debtor from their obligation.

    Aphiemi means to send away, release, let go. It is the word for cancelling a debt and for releasing a prisoner. The picture is not that the sin is overlooked or excused, but that it is removed — carried off, put at a distance.

  • καθαρίζω Katharizō · “to cleanse” · Strong’s G2511

    Katharizō (G2511) means to cleanse, purify, or make clean — physically, ceremonially, or morally. Jesus used it when cleansing lepers (an act that restored them to community); the Holy Spirit applies it to the conscience and the heart.

    The second half of the promise, and the one people forget. Katharizo is to cleanse, to purify — the word used when Jesus cleansed lepers, which restored them not only to health but to the community. Forgiveness deals with the record; cleansing deals with the defilement. John promises both, and the second is why a forgiven man can walk back into the room.

“All unrighteousness”

Note the scope, and note that it is broader than what you confessed. You agree with God about the sins you can see; He cleanses from all unrighteousness, including what you have not noticed and could not name. That is a mercy for the scrupulous man who lies awake wondering whether he missed one.

2. Where It Sits

1 John is written against a specific error. Teachers had left the church claiming a superior spiritual knowledge, and among their claims was that sin no longer applied to the truly enlightened. John answers this in a tight sequence in chapter 1.

Verse 6: if we say we have fellowship with Him and walk in darkness, we lie. Verse 8: if we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves. Verse 10: if we say we have not sinned, we make Him a liar. Three denials, each one demolished.

Verse 9 sits between the last two, and it is the only positive sentence in the run. The alternative to denying sin is not despair; it is confession. John gives the way out from between two walls.

That context also guards the verse from misuse. It is not written to make sin manageable through a repeatable procedure. It is written to people being told they had outgrown sin, and its point is that honesty is the mark of fellowship with God, not perfection.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition read this verse carefully to avoid two errors it saw on either side. Rome, in its judgment, had turned confession into a mechanism that produced forgiveness through the act. Certain enthusiasts had concluded that a justified man need not confess at all, since his sins were already dealt with.

The tradition’s answer was to distinguish a man’s state from his communion. Justification is settled and cannot be lost. Fellowship with the Father can be disrupted by unconfessed sin — not because God has withdrawn His acceptance, but because a son hiding something cannot enjoy his father’s company. Confession restores the enjoyment, not the sonship.

4. Confessional Anchor

God continues to forgive the sins of those that are justified; and although they can never fall from the state of justification, yet they may, by their sins, fall under God’s fatherly displeasure; and in that condition, they have not usually the light of his countenance restored unto them, until they humble themselves, confess their sins, beg pardon, and renew their faith and repentance.

1689 LBCF, ch. 11 — Of Justification

The confession cites this verse, and it draws exactly the distinction the text requires. They can never fall from the state of justification — the verdict is not at risk. Yet they may fall under God’s fatherly displeasure — the relationship has weather.

Fatherly is the crucial adjective. This is not a judge reconsidering a case; it is a father who is not pleased, which is an entirely different thing and only possible inside a family. Nobody speaks of the fatherly displeasure of a magistrate.

5. For the Man Reading This

Confession is agreement, so do it in plain words. Not “if I have sinned in any way” — that is not agreement, that is a legal hedge. Name the thing the way God names it. Most of the relief in this verse is unavailable to men who confess in the abstract, because abstraction is a way of not quite admitting it.

Then believe the second adjective. When you have confessed and still feel unforgiven, you are treating the promise as though it rested on God’s mood. It rests on His justice. The debt was collected at the cross, and a just God does not collect it twice from you. Your feelings are not a reliable readout of a legal fact.

And take the cleansing as seriously as the forgiveness. Many men accept that they are forgiven and still live as if they were contaminated — forgiven, but not fit to lead, not fit to speak, not fit to be near God. John says both verbs. Sent away, and made clean. The leper was not merely pardoned; he was sent back into the camp.

In the Foundations memorize pack — carry it.
