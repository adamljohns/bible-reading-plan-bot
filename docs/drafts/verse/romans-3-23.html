Verse Study · Romans

Romans 3:23

The verse everyone learns first, and the one most easily reduced to a slogan. Paul is not saying nobody is perfect. He is saying something far more specific about what was lost.

for all have sinned, and fall short of the glory of God;— World English Bible (public domain)

For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God;— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The famous half is all have sinned. The half that carries the argument is and fall short of the glory of God. Cut the second clause and you get a truism: nobody’s perfect. Everybody agrees with that, and nobody is changed by it.

Paul is not measuring men against an average. He is measuring them against a glory. That is why the verse cannot be answered by comparison — the standard is not the man next to you, and being above the median is not relevant to the question being asked.

  • ἁμαρτάνω hamartanō · “have sinned” · Strong’s G264

    Hamartanō (ἁμαρτάνω) is the primary New Testament verb for sinning, appearing about 43 times. The related noun hamartia (G266) appears over 170 times.

    Hamartano is the archery word: to miss the mark. The picture is not primarily a broken rule but a missed target — an arrow that was aimed at something and did not arrive. Which means sin in Scripture is a failure of purpose before it is a violation of a code. You were made for a target. You are not hitting it.

  • δόξα doxa · “of the glory” · Strong’s G1391

    The Greek doxa is one of the richest words in the New Testament, meaning glory, honor, splendor, renown, or the divine radiance. In secular Greek, it primarily meant 'opinion' or 'reputation', but the LXX's use of doxa to translate the Hebrew kavod (weightiness, glory) transformed the word into a primary vehicle for divine revelation.

    Here is the word that makes the verse sting properly. Doxa means glory, splendor, weight, radiance. In ordinary Greek it meant reputation or opinion; the Greek Old Testament used it to translate the Hebrew for God’s manifest weight and presence, and the New Testament keeps that sense.

    So what men fall short of is not a standard of behavior but a glory — the honor and likeness they were made to bear and reflect. Adam was made in the image of God. The tragedy Paul names is not chiefly that men break rules; it is that image-bearers are dim.

The tense that is easy to miss

“All have sinned” is past — a completed fact about every human being. “Fall short” is present, continuous. The condition is not a historical event you can put behind you; it is an ongoing state. Paul is not describing a single bad afternoon in a garden. He is describing where humanity presently stands, all the time.

2. Where It Sits

This verse is the closing move of the longest argument in the letter. From 1:18 Paul has been building a legal case. First the pagan world: they knew God and did not honor Him, and were given over. Then, in chapter 2, the moralist who agrees with all of that and condemns it — and Paul turns on him: you who judge do the same things. Then the Jew, entrusted with the very words of God, who has the law and breaks it.

By 3:9 the verdict is stated: both Jews and Greeks are all under sin. Then Paul stacks Old Testament quotations — none righteous, no, not one — and concludes in verse 19 that every mouth may be stopped and the whole world held accountable before God.

Then verse 23. It is a summary, not an opening. Its function is to close every remaining exit.

And it is not the end of the sentence. Verse 24 follows immediately: being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus. That participle attaches to the same subject. The same “all” who have sinned is the “all” being justified freely. Quoting 3:23 without 3:24 is quoting a diagnosis and withholding the treatment.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformers took this passage as the foundation for the doctrine of total depravity — a phrase that has been badly misunderstood ever since. It has never meant that every man is as wicked as he could possibly be. It means the corruption reaches every part: mind, will, affections, body. Not maximal evil, but comprehensive damage.

That distinction matters pastorally. A doctrine of maximal evil is obviously false to experience — unbelievers love their children and keep their promises. A doctrine of comprehensive damage explains something truer and stranger: that our best moments are also compromised, that even our virtues have a self-interested thread running through them, and that we cannot get behind ourselves to fix it.

4. Confessional Anchor

Our first parents, by this sin, fell from their original righteousness and communion with God, and we in them; whereby death came upon all: all becoming dead in sin, and wholly defiled in all the faculties and parts of soul and body.

1689 LBCF, ch. 6 — Of the Fall of Man, of Sin, and of the Punishment Thereof

The confession cites this very verse in support, which is why it is the right anchor. Notice its two claims.

Fell from their original righteousness — there was something to fall from. That is Paul’s glory. The doctrine of sin in the Reformed tradition is not pessimism about human nature; it is a high view of what humanity was made for, and grief about the distance.

Wholly defiled in all the faculties and parts — that is comprehensiveness, not intensity. It is the confession’s careful way of saying the damage is everywhere without saying every man is a monster. And it is why self-improvement cannot be the answer: the instrument you would use to fix yourself is among the parts that need fixing.

5. For the Man Reading This

Resist the two easy readings. One says everyone sins, so relax — which turns a universal indictment into a universal excuse and is precisely the move Paul demolishes in chapter 2. The other says I have sinned, so I am beyond help — which stops reading mid-sentence, before the word that comes next.

Verse 24 is the next word, and it is freely. The remedy in this passage is not proportional to the diagnosis; it is disproportionate. Nothing in verse 23 is qualified, and nothing in verse 24 is either.

The practical use of this verse in a real life is to end comparison. Most men manage their conscience by ranking — I am not as bad as him, I have never done what he did. That arithmetic works right up until it is measured against glory, at which point the whole ledger becomes irrelevant. Which is a mercy. A man who has stopped comparing has stopped defending, and a man who has stopped defending is finally in a position to be given something free.
