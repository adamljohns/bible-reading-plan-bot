Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:6

Jesus blesses an appetite. Not an achievement, not a temperament — a hunger for a righteousness the hungry man does not own. Then He promises to feed it.

Blessed are those who hunger and thirst after righteousness, for they shall be filled.— World English Bible (public domain)

Blessed are they which do hunger and thirst after righteousness: for they shall be filled.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The sentence has a strange shape and it is worth slowing down for. Four beatitudes in, Jesus has blessed the bankrupt, the grieving, and the man who will not grab. Now He blesses a man by what he is missing. Hunger is not a virtue. Hunger is the evidence of an empty stomach. Jesus takes the emptiness and makes it the qualification.

Watch the object of the craving. Not comfort. Not vindication. Not a better life, a quieter house, a settled mind. Righteousness. A man can want relief and want nothing to do with God. This verse is about the man who wants to be right — right in standing and right in conduct — and knows he is not.

And watch who is left out. There is no beatitude for the full. Fourteen verses later Jesus names the men who already have a righteousness: the scribes and the Pharisees, professionals, and He says yours has to exceed theirs. They are not in this sentence because a full man does not ask for food.

  • μακάριος makarios · “Blessed” · Strong’s G3107

    The Greek word for blessedness — not emotional happiness dependent on circumstances, but the deep well-being of those who are right with God. The Beatitudes open with makarios.

    The same word that opened verse 3, and it does the same work here. Not a mood. The settled well-being of a man God has taken up. The timing is the thing: Jesus calls him blessed while he is still hungry. The blessing is not withheld until the plate arrives. If it were, the sentence would read blessed are the filled, and no one on that hillside would have qualified.

  • πεινάω peinaō · “do hunger” · Strong’s G3983

    To hunger — to feel the empty ache that only food, or something better than food, can fill. In the Beatitude the hunger is not for bread first. A full man does not ask. A hungry man will.

    This is not appetite in the sense of preference, the way a man prefers steak. This is the body reporting that it is running out. The word is used flatly of ordinary starvation elsewhere in the New Testament — Paul says he was instructed both to be full and to be hungry. Jesus borrows the plainest physical want there is and hangs eternal blessing on it. The form is a participle: not the men who once hungered, the men who are hungering. A standing condition, not a decision made at a camp meeting in 1997.

  • διψάω dipsaō · “thirst” · Strong’s G1372

    To thirst or be thirsty — literally, and in its metaphorical uses describing spiritual longing and the promise of ultimate satisfaction in Christ. Dipsao diagnoses a deep longing that no earthly satisfaction can quench.

    Doubling hunger with thirst is not decoration. A man can live weeks without food. He dies in days without water. Adding thirst removes the option of treating this as a hobby you get around to. It is also the word Christ speaks from the cross — I thirst. The Lord who blesses the thirsty went thirsty to make good on the blessing.

  • δικαιοσύνη dikaiosynē · “after righteousness” · Strong’s G1343

    One of the central theological terms of the New Testament: righteousness, justice, right standing. It denotes both the character of being right and the legal status of being declared right.

    This is the hinge of the verse, and the place most sermons pick one half and lose the other. Make it status only and the verse says nothing about Tuesday. Make it conduct only and the verse is a treadmill with a promise nailed to the far wall you never reach. Matthew will not let you split them. In 5:20 the righteousness has to exceed that of men who kept records for a living. In 6:33 it is something you seek and are given, along with everything else. The right standing is credited; the right living follows it; the same word carries both and the hungry man wants both.

  • χορτάζω chortazō · “shall be filled” · Strong’s G5526

    To fill — to feed a hungry man until the ache is gone. It is the language of a trough and a table, not of a nibble. The empty man is not left empty.

    Not a taste. Not a sample to keep you civil. The word is the one you would use for the animals at the trough and the crowd on the grass after the loaves. And it is passive: shall be filled. Someone else does the filling. The hungry man does not cater his own meal, does not cook, does not earn the seat. He is fed. That single passive verb is why this beatitude is good news instead of a taller fence.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 5:5 — Blessed are the gentle, for they shall inherit the earth.

Matthew 5:7 — Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy.

The frame from verses 1 and 2 still holds. He saw the crowds, went up the mountain, sat down — the posture of a rabbi about to teach, not a man about to rally a crowd — and His disciples came to Him. The first words out of His mouth in the great sermon are blessings, not commands. The commands come. They come hard, before this chapter is over. But they land on men He has already called blessed.

Verse 6 sits fourth, at the seam. Read the first four together and they are one man in four frames: bankrupt in spirit, grieving over it, no longer grabbing for himself, and now starving for a righteousness he cannot manufacture. Read the last four and the direction reverses — merciful, pure in heart, making peace, taking the blows. That is a man with something in him. Verse 6 is the turn. Everything before it is emptiness named. Everything after it is what comes out of a man who has been fed.

Look forward in the chapter and the placement gets sharper. Jesus says He did not come to destroy the law or the prophets but to fulfill. Then He sets the bar: unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, there is no way you will enter. Read cold, that is a closed door. Read after verse 6, it is not. The righteousness that exceeds theirs is not a higher score on the same test. It is the righteousness a starving man is handed. And in the next chapter He names the order of operations: seek first God's Kingdom, and His righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well.

None of this is new with Jesus. He is preaching the prophets on a hillside.

  • Isaiah 55:1

    “Hey! Come, everyone who thirsts, to the waters! Come, he who has no money, buy, and eat! Yes, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price.

  • Amos 8:11

    Behold, the days come,” says the Lord the LORD, “that I will send a famine in the land, not a famine of bread, nor a thirst for water, but of hearing the LORD’s words.

  • Isaiah 29:8

    It will be like when a hungry man dreams, and behold, he eats; but he awakes, and his hunger isn’t satisfied; or like when a thirsty man dreams, and behold, he drinks; but he awakes, and behold, he is faint, and he is still thirsty.

  • John 6:35

    Jesus said to them, “I am the bread of life. He who comes to me will not be hungry, and he who believes in me will never be thirsty.

  • John 4:14

    but whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never thirst again; but the water that I will give him will become in him a well of water springing up to eternal life.”

Amos names the famine. Isaiah 29 names the counterfeit — the man who dreams he ate and wakes up empty, which is a fair description of most of what gets sold to hungry men. Isaiah 55 opens the invitation and prices it at nothing. And in John, the One doing the blessing on the mountain names Himself as the meal. Matthew 5:6 does not tell you what the food is. The Gospels get around to it.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed reading of this verse turns on one question: can a man have real faith and still be hungry? If the answer is no, then this beatitude belongs to nobody, because nobody is full. Calvin answers it directly, arguing about what it means to believe with the whole heart:

In this passage, therefore, to believe with the whole heart is not to believe Christ perfectly, but only to embrace him sincerely with heart and soul; not to be filled with him, but with ardent affection to hunger and thirst and long after him.

Calvin, Institutes IV.14

That is the whole tension of Matthew 5:6 in one line. A true faith is not a full one. The mark of the genuine article is not satiation but ardent hunger. Which means the ache you have been treating as evidence against your conversion is closer to evidence for it.

Calvin will not let the hunger stay a private feeling, either. Writing on the first petition of the Lord's Prayer, he says the hallowing of God's name must be earnestly hungered and thirsted after — the same appetite, aimed at God's glory, doing its work in a man's praying. Hunger that never reaches the mouth is not hunger. It is opinion.

And he puts the table where the starving can reach it. On the Supper:

let us remember that this sacred feast is medicine for the sick, comfort for the sinner, and generosity for the poor; while to the healthy, the righteous, and the rich, if any such could be found, it would be of no value at all. For while Christ is given to us there for food, we recognize that without him we fail, waste away, and perish, just as hunger destroys the strength of the body.

Calvin, Institutes IV.17

Note the aside — if any such could be found. Calvin does not believe there are healthy, righteous, rich men in the room. He believes there are hungry men and men who have not noticed yet. The meal is for the first kind. This is exactly the sorting Jesus is doing in verse 6, and it is why the beatitude is not a bar to clear but a door to walk through.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession keeps both halves of dikaiosynē in their places, and it is worth seeing them in that order. First, where the filling comes from:

Those whom God effectually calls, he also freely justifies; not by infusing righteousness into them, but by pardoning their sins, and by accounting and accepting their persons as righteous; not for anything wrought in them, or done by them, but for Christ's sake alone.

1689 LBCF, ch. 11 — Of Justification

Not infused. Not poured in a little at a time to be topped up by effort. Reckoned, on Christ's account, whole. That is the passive verb in the beatitude spelled out in doctrine: shall be filled, by Someone, for Someone else's sake. A man who understands this stops trying to cook.

Then why the hunger does not stop:

This sanctification is throughout the whole man, yet imperfect in this life; there abide still some remnants of corruption in every part, from which arises a continual and irreconcilable war; the flesh lusting against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh.

1689 LBCF, ch. 13 — Of Sanctification

Continual. Irreconcilable. There is no truce clause in that paragraph, which means there is no point in this life at which the appetite is supposed to quit. The Baptist Catechism says the same thing from the other side — sanctification is God's free grace, by which we are renewed in the whole man after the image of God, and are enabled more and more to die to sin, and live to righteousness. More and more is the phrase to sit with. It is not the vocabulary of arrival.

The two halves, held together

Justification says the righteousness you are starving for has already been credited to you and cannot be improved by a better week. Sanctification says the appetite goes on, because the war goes on. Drop the first and the hunger becomes despair. Drop the second and the hunger becomes complacency. Matthew 5:6 holds both: filled, and still hungry, until the day the war ends.

5. For the Man Reading This

Start with the uncomfortable part. This verse does not measure you by your record. It measures you by your appetite. A man can have a clean sheet — no arrests, no affairs, church every Sunday, kids in line — and no hunger at all. That was the Pharisee's exact profile, and Jesus put him outside this sentence.

So run the honest test. Not what you do when someone is watching. What you actually want when nothing is required of you. Sunday afternoon, nobody in the house needs anything, no one is grading you. What does your appetite reach for? That is the reading this verse takes.

Second: stop treating the ache as a malfunction. Most men, when they feel the gap between what they are and what they ought to be, go looking for a way to stop feeling it. Numb it, explain it, lower the standard until the gap closes. Every one of those is a way of killing the thing Jesus called blessed. Losing the hunger is not progress. It is the beginning of the Isaiah 29 dream — eating in your sleep and waking up faint.

Third: hunger has to reach a table or it is just starving. Appetite that never eats is not spirituality, it is a man admiring his own longing. The food is where God has always put it — the Word read and preached, the Supper, prayer that actually asks. If you are hungry and dry at the same time, the question is usually not whether God is feeding but whether you showed up where He serves.

Fourth, and this one is a knife: do not confuse hunger for righteousness with hunger for the reputation of righteousness. They feel similar from the inside and they are opposites. Jesus spends the front half of the next chapter on it — giving, praying, and fasting done to be seen. A man who wants to be righteous will do the hard right thing when it costs him standing. A man who wants to look righteous will not. Find one place this week where being right will cost you something and no one will know. Then do it. That is a hunger test with a real answer.

And carry the promise as it is written. They shall be filled. Future tense, passive voice, no conditions attached to your performance. You are not being asked to produce the righteousness. You are being asked to want it — and told plainly that the wanting will be answered, by God, at His table, in full.

In the Sermon on the Mount memorize pack — carry it. Twelve words in English. Say them the next time you catch yourself trying to talk your own conscience down.
