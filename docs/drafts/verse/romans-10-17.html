Verse Study · Romans

Romans 10:17

Nine words in the King James, and every one of them is carrying load. Paul is not handing out a maxim about the devotional life. He is closing an argument, and the first two words say so.

So faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God.— World English Bible (public domain)

So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The verse opens on a conclusion word. Most men meet Romans 10:17 alone — on a mug, on a bulletin cover, stitched on a wall — where it reads like a general observation about how a soul gets built. That is not where Paul put it. He put it at the bottom of a paragraph about missionaries and about a nation that would not believe, and he began it with a particle whose only job is to say therefore. Read it without the four verses in front of it and you are reading the verdict without the case.

What he concludes is a chain with two links, and he states it backwards. Faith comes out of hearing. The hearing comes through God's spoken word. Three verses earlier he had run the same chain forward and longer — calling, believing, hearing, preaching, sending — and it ended out on the road at the feet of men who had been sent. Here he runs it back down and stops one rung lower than he started, at the mouth of God.

Notice that Paul does not use the same little word twice. English flattens it — by hearing, by the word — but the Greek has two different prepositions doing two different jobs. The first marks a source: where the thing came out of. The second marks a channel: what the thing came through. Faith's origin is the hearing. The hearing's conduit is God's utterance. Faith is not portrayed as arriving straight off the page, bypassing the ear; and the hearing is not credited with any content of its own. It is the pipe. It is not the water.

  • ἄρα ara · "so then" · Strong's G686

    An inferential particle: therefore, then, so, consequently. It draws a conclusion from what has just been stated — the "therefore" of doctrinal reasoning, as our lexicon puts it, the load-bearing joint that unites doctrine and practice. It is the same particle that opens Romans 8:1. A man who quotes this verse without an argument standing behind it has quoted the joint and thrown away both timbers.

  • πίστις pistis · "faith" · Strong's G4102

    Faith, trust, reliance, firm conviction — around 243 occurrences in the New Testament, and the cornerstone of everything Paul has argued since chapter three. It covers both the act of trusting and the body of truth trusted. Here it is the thing being accounted for. Paul is not telling you what faith is in this verse. He has done that elsewhere. He is telling you where it comes from, and the answer is: from outside you.

  • ἐκ ek · "cometh by" / "out of" · Strong's G1537

    One of the commonest prepositions in the New Testament, always taking the genitive, and always answering one question: where did this come from? Out of, from, by means of. It is the preposition of origin — the church is called out of the world, believers are born of God, the righteous live by faith. Paul uses it here of faith itself. Faith has an origin outside the man who has it, and the verse names that origin without flinching.

  • ἀκοή akoē · "hearing" · Strong's G189

    The hinge of the verse, and the word most often misread. It is a noun built from the verb to hear, and it means both the act of hearing and — just as regularly — the thing heard: a report, a message, news. The King James translators knew it. One verse earlier they rendered this same word "report" — Lord, who hath believed our report? — and put a note in the margin telling the reader that the Greek says the hearing of us. So when the next sentence says faith comes by hearing, "hearing" is not chiefly your attentiveness. It is the message that got delivered. Faith comes out of the report.

  • διά dia · "by" / "through" · Strong's G1223

    With the genitive, this is the preposition of channel — the means by which something passes. Water through a pipe, work done through an agent. It is the word Paul uses for justification through faith and John uses for creation through the Son. Set it against the previous preposition and the sentence tightens: the report is where faith comes out of; God's word is what the report comes through. Nothing here originates with the messenger.

  • ῥῆμα rhema · "the word" · Strong's G4487

    A spoken word, a specific utterance — more concrete than logos. Not the idea of a message but the thing said out loud. This is the word behind "man shall not live by bread alone" and behind the sword of the Spirit in Ephesians 6, and it frames this whole paragraph: it is the word that is near you, in your mouth and in your heart, in verse 8 — the word of faith, which we preach — and it is the "words" that go out to the ends of the world in verse 18. Verse 17 sits between them. Paul is not reasoning about revelation in the abstract. He means a sentence somebody said.

  • θεός theos · "of God" · Strong's G2316

    The one true God of Israel, Creator and Redeemer, revealed definitively in Jesus Christ. In this chapter that last clause is not decoration: the word being preached is the word about the Lord whom Israel would not call on. Whose word it is, is the point of the sentence. The report has authority because of whose mouth it started in.

One honest note on the text. The King James and the World English Bible read the word of God. Several modern translations read the word of Christ instead, following a different family of Greek manuscripts, and our own lexicon entry for rhema renders the verse that way. This is a genuine difference in the manuscript evidence, not a translator's liberty, and it is worth knowing about rather than being surprised by. It does not move the doctrine an inch. The word in question is the gospel word this chapter has been preaching since verse 8, and verse 9 has already told you whose name is in it.

2. Where It Sits

Romans 9 through 11 is Paul on his own people. He opens chapter 10 not with an argument but with a prayer — my heart's desire and my prayer to God is for Israel, that they may be saved — and then makes the hardest diagnosis a man can make about people he loves: they have zeal, and it is not according to knowledge. They went looking to establish a righteousness of their own and would not submit to God's. Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes.

From there he sets out the righteousness of faith in the plainest terms in the letter. The word is near you, in your mouth and in your heart. Confess with the mouth, believe in the heart, and you will be saved. There is no distinction between Jew and Greek. Whoever calls on the name of the Lord will be saved.

And then the four questions, fired off one after another with no pause between them. How will they call on one they have not believed in? How will they believe in one they have not heard? How will they hear without a preacher? And how will they preach unless they are sent? Each question knocks the floor out from under the one before it, until the whole weight of a man's salvation is resting on somebody having been sent. Paul quotes Isaiah on the feet of the messengers, and for a moment the argument sounds triumphant.

Then verse 16 lands, and it is a cold verse. But they didn't all listen to the glad news. He has just built a chain that reaches all the way from the sending of preachers to the salvation of the hearer, and immediately he concedes that the chain does not run automatically. Isaiah had said it first, and said it as a lament: Lord, who has believed our report?

Verse 17 is what Paul says while standing in that wreckage. Read in place, it is doing something more careful than the mug version suggests. He does not say that hearing produces faith. He says faith has no other source. It is a statement about the pipe, not a promise about the flow. The preaching Isaiah described had been done, and largely refused; and Paul's conclusion is not that preaching failed but that preaching is still the only place faith ever comes from.

Which is exactly why the next verse is an indictment rather than an excuse. But I say, didn't they hear? — and the answer is a flat yes, their sound went out into all the earth. The chain was intact. The report went out. Nobody can plead that the word never reached them. Verse 17 is laid down so that verse 18 can close the door on the only defence Israel had left, and every man since who has heard the gospel and shrugged is standing in the same room.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

A limit worth naming before we start: Calvin's commentary on Romans is not part of this site's library, so nothing here is drawn from it. What follows is from the Institutes, which is — and Calvin quotes this verse in it by name.

Calvin — on the inseparable relation between faith and the Word

In the chapter where he defines faith, Calvin makes the same connection Paul makes here, and he makes it structural rather than sentimental. Faith is not a disposition that happens to find the Word useful. Faith has no existence apart from it.

First, we must remember, that there is an inseparable relation between faith and the word, and that these can no more be disconnected from each other than rays of light from the sun. … The same word is the basis on which it rests and is sustained. Declining from it, it falls. Take away the word, therefore, and no faith will remain.

Calvin, Institutes III.2 — Of Faith

The image is worth sitting with, because it settles a question men ask constantly in the wrong order. Rays are not something the sun acquires. Remove the sun and you do not have dimmer light; you have none. So the answer to my faith feels thin is not, first, to examine the faith. It is to ask what has happened to your exposure to the word, because in Calvin's picture the one is simply the other arriving.

He then says what the word actually does for a man, and it is not primarily information:

the word itself, whatever be the way in which it is conveyed to us, is a kind of mirror in which faith beholds God.

Calvin, Institutes III.2 — Of Faith

Calvin — on Romans 10 itself

Calvin quotes Romans 10:14 and 10:17 together in the chapter on prayer, where he is arguing that a man cannot sincerely call on a God he has not been told about. He introduces the passage with a jab that has aged extremely well:

This is the meaning of Paul in the well known passage to which dull men give too little heed… Gradually deducing the origin of prayer from faith, he distinctly maintains that God cannot be invoked sincerely except by those to whom, by the preaching of the Gospel, his mercy and willingness have been made known, nay, familiarly explained.

Calvin, Institutes III.20 — Of Prayer

Well known, and given too little heed — that is the exact fate of this verse. And note where Calvin lands: not on a general religious sense of God, but on familiarly explained. Somebody sat down and made it plain to you. That is the chain in verse 14 running in the direction of your own life.

Calvin — on why God uses men's mouths

The hardest part of Paul's chain for a modern man is the preacher. Why should saving faith come through a fallible man in a room on a Sunday, when a book and a private hour are available? Calvin answers that the arrangement is a mercy, not an inconvenience.

among the many noble endowments with which God has adorned the human race, one of the most remarkable is, that he deigns to consecrate the mouths and tongues of men to his service, making his own voice to be heard in them.

Calvin, Institutes IV.1 — Of the True Church

Then he takes away the obvious escape route, and he is blunt about who takes it:

Pride, or fastidiousness, or emulation, induces many to persuade themselves that they can profit sufficiently by reading and meditating in private, and thus to despise public meetings, and deem preaching superfluous.

Calvin, Institutes IV.1 — Of the True Church

He is careful not to overclaim, and the qualification is the sharpest sentence of the three:

although the power of God is not confined to external means, he has, however, confined us to his ordinary method of teaching, which method, when fanatics refuse to observe, they entangle themselves in many fatal snares.

Calvin, Institutes IV.1 — Of the True Church

God is not boxed in. You are. He can convert a man on a road with no preacher in sight and has done it. But that is His liberty, not your plan, and a man who builds his spiritual life around the exception has already told you what he thinks of the rule.

The counterweight — Hebrews and Isaiah

Nothing above should be read as saying that a man who hears will therefore believe. Paul himself has just denied it in verse 16, and the writer to the Hebrews states it as bluntly as it can be stated:

For indeed we have had good news preached to us, even as they also did, but the word they heard didn't profit them, because it wasn't mixed with faith by those who heard.

Hebrews 4:2, World English Bible

Same preaching, opposite outcomes. So Romans 10:17 is not a lever. It tells you where faith comes from; it does not put you in charge of whether it comes. Both halves of that have to be held, or you end up either despising the means or trusting them as a mechanism.

4. Confessional Anchor

Romans 10:17 is hung as a proof-text under two different chapters of the 1689, and the two chapters pull in opposite and complementary directions. The first says how faith is ordinarily produced. The second says that the Word is the only place the gospel is found — and then, two paragraphs later, that the Word by itself will not do it.

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 14, Paragraph 1 — proof-texts include Romans 10:14, 17

"The grace of faith, whereby the elect are enabled to believe to the saving of their souls, is the work of the Spirit of Christ in their hearts; and is ordinarily wrought by the ministry of the Word; by which also, and by the administration of baptism and the Lord's Supper, prayer, and other means appointed of God, it is increased and strengthened."

Read Chapter 14, Of Saving Faith →

Two words in that sentence are doing careful work. Ordinarily — the confession will not say God is unable to work otherwise, which is Calvin's point about external means exactly. And increased and strengthened — the same ministry that starts faith is the ministry that grows it. There is no graduation from the means of grace to something more advanced. The man who has believed for forty years is fed the same way the new convert was.

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 20, Paragraph 2 — proof-texts include Romans 10:14–15, 17

"This promise of Christ, and salvation by him, is revealed only by the Word of God. Neither do the works of creation or providence, with the light of nature, reveal Christ or grace by him, so much as in a general or obscure way — much less that men destitute of the revelation of him by the promise or gospel should be enabled thereby to attain saving faith or repentance."

Read Chapter 20, Of the Gospel →

That is Romans 10:17 turned into policy. Creation preaches something, and Paul says in chapter 1 that it preaches enough to leave a man without excuse. It does not preach Christ. A sunset will not tell anybody that Jesus is Lord and that God raised Him from the dead. Only the report does that, and the report has to be carried by somebody.

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 20, Paragraph 4

"Although the gospel be the only outward means of revealing Christ and saving grace, and is — as such — abundantly sufficient thereunto, yet that men who are dead in trespasses may be born again, quickened, or regenerated, there is moreover necessary an effectual, insuperable work of the Holy Spirit upon the whole soul, for the producing in them a new spiritual life. Without this no other means will effect their conversion unto God."

Read Chapter 20, Of the Gospel →

The only outward means. That adjective is the whole balance. The gospel preached is sufficient as an outward means and it is the only one there is — and it is still outward. Something has to happen inside the man that no preacher can perform. This is why the same men who built their whole church life around preaching also prayed like their lives depended on it.

The Baptist Catechism puts both halves into two consecutive questions, and it lists Romans 10:13–17 among the proofs of the first.

The Baptist Catechism · Question 94 — How is the Word made effectual to salvation?

"The Spirit of God makes the reading, but especially the preaching, of the Word an effectual means of convincing and converting sinners, and of building them up in holiness and comfort through faith to salvation."

Work through the Catechism →
The Baptist Catechism · Question 95 — How is the Word to be read and heard?

"That the Word may become effectual to salvation, we must attend to it with diligence, preparation, and prayer; receive it with faith and love, lay it up in our hearts, and practice it in our lives."

Work through the Catechism →

Question 94 makes the Spirit the agent and preaching the ordinary instrument. Question 95 hands you something to actually do about it, and the list is unromantic on purpose: diligence, preparation, prayer, then receive, then store, then practice. Six verbs, and only the last one happens outside the ear. That is the confessional shape of Romans 10:17 for a man's week.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the memorize pack called The Word Hand, and it holds a specific position there: the pinky, the finger for hearing. The pack runs hear, read, study, memorize, meditate — five ways of taking hold of Scripture, ordered from the weakest grip to the strongest. Try to pick something up with your little finger alone and you learn the lesson without anybody teaching it. Hearing is the grip you cannot do without and cannot rely on by itself. Romans 10:17 is the verse for that finger because it is where every other finger has to start.

Faith is not something you manufacture, so stop trying to. The most common thing a struggling man does is turn inward and go looking for more belief, as though faith were a reserve he could draw down harder. Paul says faith comes out of hearing. It arrives from outside. When it is thin, the move is not deeper introspection; it is more exposure to the word — which, as it happens, is a thing you can actually schedule.

Sit under preaching, and do it on purpose. The catechism says the reading, but especially the preaching, and Calvin names exactly the excuse a capable, self-directed man reaches for: that he can profit sufficiently on his own. He calls it pride or fastidiousness, and he is not being unkind — he is being accurate. Podcasts and a Bible on your phone are good things. They are not a man standing under the word in a room with the people he is accountable to. Get in the room.

Somebody paid for your hearing. Verse 15 says preachers have to be sent. Whoever first made the gospel plain to you was fed, housed and sent by people you will never meet, and most of them did it with money that was tight. The verse has a bill attached. Pay it forward: give to your church, support the men who are sent, and be honest that missions is not a department of the Christian life but the mechanism this verse describes.

You are somebody's report. The chain in verse 14 needs a mouth in it, and it does not require an ordained one. There is a man on your crew who has never once had the gospel explained to him by a person who knew him. He is not waiting for an argument. He is waiting for the report, and Calvin's phrase is the standard to aim at — familiarly explained. Plain, in normal words, by somebody he trusts.

And leave the outcome where Paul left it. He preached to his own nation, quoted Isaiah's lament, and admitted that they did not all obey. Hearing is not a guarantee; Hebrews says the same word profited one group and not the other. So speak, send, sit under it, and pray for the insuperable work of the Spirit that no amount of good preaching can substitute for. You are responsible for the report. You are not responsible for the result, and a man who confuses the two will either burn out or start lying about his numbers.

The verse ends where a man's confidence ought to end: not on the messenger, not on the hearer, but on whose word it was in the first place.
