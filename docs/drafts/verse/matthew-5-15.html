Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:15

Nobody lights a lamp in order to hide it. Christ gives no command in this verse — He describes something every man in that crowd had done at dusk, and lets the ordinary sense of it fall on the conscience.

Neither do you light a lamp, and put it under a measuring basket, but on a stand; and it shines to all who are in the house.— World English Bible (public domain)

Neither do men light a candle, and put it under a bushel, but on a candlestick; and it giveth light unto all that are in the house.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Verse 14 shut one door. A city that is set on an hill cannot be hid — exposure is not on the table, and a man who wanted a quiet, unnoticed Christianity has already been told he cannot have one. But there is a second door still standing open, and this verse shuts it. A man can be perfectly visible as a Christian and give off no light whatever. Everyone in the room knows exactly where the lamp is. The room is still dark.

So Christ moves from what is impossible to what is merely absurd. And notice how He argues it. There is no imperative in this sentence any more than in the last one. It is a flat observation about what men do: neither do men light a candle, and put it under a bushel. He does not say the thing is forbidden. He says nobody does it. That is a harder argument to get out from under than a rule, because a rule can be disputed and a habit cannot. Every man listening had lit a lamp that week. Not one of them had ever lit one and then covered it.

He also does not leave the negative standing by itself, and this is where most preaching on the verse stops short. A great deal of Christian exhortation about worldliness gets as far as the basket — do not hide, do not shrink, do not compromise — and never says where the lamp goes instead. Christ says it in the same breath: but on a candlestick. The verse is about placement. Placement is in fact the only decision anywhere in it.

Because look at what the lamp does and does not do. In this sentence it is acted upon twice and acts once. It is lit — by somebody else. It is put — by somebody else. And then it shines, which is the one verb it owns, and which it does automatically once the first two have been done properly. Nobody has to coach a lamp on a stand. Nobody has to remind it to burn. Put a lit lamp where a lit lamp belongs and the shining takes care of itself.

That is why the last clause is a result and not a third duty. And it giveth light unto all that are in the house. Indicative, like ye are in verse 14 and unlike let your light so shine in verse 16. The shining is not the assignment. The stand is the assignment; the shining is what follows.

And the alternative is not neutral. A flame under a clay measure is not a modest flame — it is a dying one. It eats the air in that little space and goes out, and while it is going out it blackens the vessel and wastes the oil, and in a house full of cloth and straw a smothered wick is a good deal more dangerous than a burning one. Covering a lamp does not preserve it. It destroys it, quietly, and takes the room's light with it.

  • καίω kaiō · "do men light" · Strong's G2545

    The Greek verb kaio means to burn, to set on fire, to cause to shine by burning. It describes both literal fire and metaphorical burning — the burning of lamps, hearts, and judgment.

    The word is about kindling, and kindling is something done to a lamp, not by it. There were no matches. Fire in that house came from another fire — a coal carried from a neighbour's hearth, a wick touched to a flame already burning. Nothing in the picture starts itself. The same verb stands behind John's description of the Baptist as a burning and shining lamp, and the point there is identical: he was lit. That is the first thing this verse quietly settles about you, and it settles it before any duty is mentioned.

  • λύχνος lychnos · "a candle" · Strong's G3088

    A portable lamp, typically an oil lamp — the primary source of indoor illumination in the ancient world. Used metaphorically for spiritual illumination, divine guidance, and the believer's witness.

    Not a candle in our sense and not anything grand. A small clay dish of oil with a wick laid in the lip, cheap enough that a poor household had one and no more. KJV says candle because that was the household light of 1611; the object underneath is humbler than either word suggests. That is the right scale for the picture. Verse 14 was a walled city visible for miles; verse 15 is a two-penny lamp in a one-room house. Christ hands out the enormous title and then, immediately, brings it down to the size of an object you could hold in one hand — which is where obedience to it will actually happen.

  • τίθημι tithēmi · "put" · Strong's G5087

    The Greek verb tithemi means to put, place, set, lay, or appoint. It is one of the most common verbs in the NT, appearing about 100 times. It carries the sense of purposeful placement or appointment.

    It is the verb of deliberate positioning — not where a thing drifted, but where somebody meant it to be. It governs both halves of the sentence: the same act that could put the lamp under the basket puts it on the stand. There is no third option in the verse and no neutral shelf. The lamp is somewhere, and wherever it is, somebody put it there on purpose. Christ is not asking a man to feel more zealous. He is asking him where he has set the thing down.

  • μόδιος modios · "a bushel" · Strong's G3426

    The Greek noun modios is a household grain measure — a basket large enough to cover a lamp. KJV renders it bushel. The word is ordinary kitchen gear, not temple furniture.

    The KJV's own translators thought this needed a footnote, and printed one in the margin: the word in the original signifieth a measure containing about a pint less than a peck. A grain tub. It is the sting of the whole verse and it is easy to miss. Christ does not say the light is hidden by persecution, or by the state, or by some enemy with a plan. It is hidden by the kitchenware. A man does not need a palace or a prison to put his light out. He only needs a basket he already owns — and every one of us owns several, and uses them daily for perfectly respectable purposes.

  • λυχνία lychnia · "a candlestick" · Strong's G3087

    The Greek noun lychnia is a lampstand — the raised place made for a lamp so the house can see. KJV renders it candlestick.

    The stand is not the light and never was. It gives off nothing. It is a bracket, a shelf, a peg — the place the house had already agreed the lamp goes. A lamp on the floor still burns; it simply serves nobody past its own two feet. So the stand is the office of the light rather than its substance, and the difference between a wasted Christian and a useful one is frequently not the amount of grace in him but the position he has consented to occupy. Note also that the same word names the seven lampstands in Revelation, and there the lampstands are churches. The stand is a public place, appointed by somebody else, and it is not comfortable: a lamp on a stand is up where the draft is.

  • λάμπω lampō · "it giveth light" · Strong's G2989

    The Greek verb lampō means to shine — to beam, to give light, to radiate brilliance, whether of a lamp, a face, or a light God Himself commands. It is a primary verb.

    Here it is a statement of fact; three lines later, in verse 16, the same shining becomes the command. That order is the whole pastoral logic of the paragraph. First Christ tells you what a properly placed lamp does, and only then does He tell you to do it — so that when the imperative lands, it is asking you for something you have already been told is natural to what you are. Obedience here is not the manufacture of light. It is the removal of an obstruction.

  • οἰκία oikia · "the house" · Strong's G3614

    The Greek noun oikia names a house — the dwelling and, often, the people in it.

    Verse 14 said the world. Verse 15 says the house, and the drop in scale is deliberate and merciful. The reach of this lamp is one room and everybody in it. It is worth sitting with how small that is before rushing to make it large, because the men who fail this verse almost never fail it at the level of the world. They fail it at the level of the kitchen. A man can be a recognised Christian voice in his city and a rumour in his own hallway. Whatever else all that are in the house means, it means the people who are there when the door is shut and there is nobody left to impress.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 5:14 — You are the light of the world. A city located on a hill can’t be hidden.

Matthew 5:16 — Even so, let your light shine before men; that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father who is in heaven.

Four verses run as one movement. Verse 13 is salt: preservation, working by contact, largely unseen. Verse 14 is light and a city: identity, and exposure that cannot be undone. Verse 15 is a lamp and a stand: the household object that takes the enormous title of verse 14 and makes it something a man can actually do something about. Verse 16 is the imperative, with the purpose clause attached that governs everything before it.

Verse 15 is the hinge, and the hinge is doing real work. Without it, verse 16 would arrive with nothing under it — let your light shine, and a man would be left to guess what that involves. Verse 15 supplies the picture, which is why verse 16 opens by pointing back at it: even so. Shine the way a lamp on a stand shines. That is, in the ordinary place, at the ordinary height, doing the ordinary thing the lamp was made for, in front of the people who live there.

Remember what has just finished. The Beatitudes end on being reviled and persecuted and slandered for Christ's sake, and any man with sense starts looking for cover the moment he hears it. Salt, light, city, lamp — the whole cluster is the Lord's answer to that instinct, and He closes every exit in turn. You are in the meat already. You are on the hill already. And the basket you were reaching for does not work; it only kills the flame.

Then verse 17 turns to the law, and the long stretch that follows makes the law heavier rather than lighter: anger under murder, the eye under adultery, love pushed out past the enemy line. That stretch is the content of the shining. Whatever else letting your light shine has been made to mean in a hundred sermons, in Matthew it means the righteousness described in the rest of this sermon, lived in front of people who are watching.

The saying itself was not preached only once. Mark and Luke both carry it, and they hang it in a different place — in the teaching about the sower and the seed, where the lamp is the word and the message of the kingdom, and where a further sentence is attached that Matthew leaves out: there is nothing hidden, except that it should be made known; neither was anything made secret, but that it should come to light. That is the reason underneath the picture. Concealment is not merely wasteful; it is temporary. God has arranged the world so that hidden things come out. A man who covers his lamp is not only wasting oil, he is working against the direction the whole creation is moving, and he will lose.

Luke gives the saying twice, once with the sower and once alongside the eye as the lamp of the body — which is worth noticing, because it means the lamp image runs in two directions. There is a lamp inside a man that determines whether he is full of light or full of darkness, and there is a lamp on a stand by which a household sees. The second depends on the first. A man whose interior eye has gone bad has nothing to put on the stand.

And there is a counterweight in this same sermon that has to be held with this verse or the verse will be misused. One chapter later: be careful that you don't do your charitable giving before men, to be seen by them, or else you have no reward from your Father who is in heaven. Same preacher, same crowd, same week. Chapter five says let men see; chapter six says do not do it to be seen. That is not a contradiction, and the difference is not the amount of visibility. It is the object of the sentence. In 5:16 what men see is your good works, and what they do about it is glorify your Father. In 6:1 what men see is you, and what you get is the applause you were angling for, which the Lord coldly calls your reward in full. The basket and the spotlight are two failures of the same kind: in both, the man is thinking about himself. The lamp on the stand is the only posture in which he is not.

Paul's clay vessels belong beside this verse more than any other cross-reference in the list. The treasure is the light; the pot is cheap on purpose. That is not an insult to the pot, it is the design — that the exceeding greatness of the power may be of God, and not from ourselves. And the last reference tells you what the stand turns out to be. In Revelation the lampstands are congregations. The Lord who told a hillside crowd to put the lamp where the house can see it walks among seven lampstands at the end of the book, and He is not admiring them. He is inspecting them.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

It should be said at the outset that Calvin does not take up Matthew 5:15 in the Institutes. His verse-by-verse handling of it belongs to his commentary on the harmony of the Gospels, which is not in this library, and nothing here will pretend otherwise. But the doctrine this verse stands on is worked out at length in the Institutes, and it comes at the text from three directions — each one landing on a different word in the sentence.

Start with the stand, and with the question of who the light is for. In the chapter on self-denial Calvin lays down a rule that is, in effect, the theology of a lampstand:

whatever we obtain from the Lord is granted on the condition of our employing it for the common good of the Church, and that, therefore, the legitimate use of all our gifts is a kind and liberal communication of them with others.

— Calvin, Institutes III.7, §5 (Beveridge, 1845)

Granted on a condition. That is the hinge of the whole matter, and it is why the covered lamp is not merely unhelpful but a breach of terms. What you were given was never given for private consumption, and the man holding it is not the owner. Calvin presses the same point harder a sentence later:

There cannot be a surer rule, nor a stronger exhortation to the observance of it, than when we are taught that all the endowments which we possess are divine deposits entrusted to us for the very purpose of being distributed for the good of our neighbour.

— Calvin, Institutes III.7, §5 (Beveridge, 1845)

A deposit is somebody else's money in your hands. And when Calvin reaches for a picture of how a deposit like that behaves, he reaches for the same kind of picture Christ did — an ordinary thing doing the obvious thing it was built to do:

No member has its function for itself, or applies it for its own private use, but transfers it to its fellow-members; nor does it derive any other advantage from it than that which it receives in common with the whole body.

— Calvin, Institutes III.7, §5 (Beveridge, 1845)

An eye that saw only for itself would not be a modest eye. It would be a defect. That is the exact weight of Christ's neither do men: not a rule being broken but a nature being contradicted.

Second, the other ditch. Any verse that says be seen is one bad step from vanity, and Calvin had already put the fence up two sections earlier in the same chapter, in the middle of describing what self-denial actually does to a man:

This is that self-denial which Christ so strongly enforces on his disciples from the very outset … which, as soon as it takes hold of the mind, leaves no place either, first, for pride, show, and ostentation; or, secondly, for avarice, lust, luxury, effeminacy, or other vices which are engendered by self love.

— Calvin, Institutes III.7, §2 (Beveridge, 1845)

Pride, show, and ostentation are named first, ahead of the appetites — which is the correct order for a man reading Matthew 5. And Calvin knows how cheaply the counterfeit passes: even where there is some appearance of virtue, it is vitiated by a depraved longing for applause. The same lamp can be hoisted for two entirely different reasons, and the room cannot tell them apart. God can. This is why the paragraph in Matthew does not end at visibility but at glorify your Father which is in heaven. The purpose clause is not decoration. It is the thing that keeps the command from becoming its own opposite.

Third, the placement itself — and here Calvin is at his most useful, because he is talking about exactly the sort of man who reads a verse like this and decides he can obey it alone:

although the power of God is not confined to external means, he has, however, confined us to his ordinary method of teaching, which method, when fanatics refuse to observe, they entangle themselves in many fatal snares.

— Calvin, Institutes IV.1, §5 (Beveridge, 1845)

Not confined to it; but we are. That is the doctrine of the lampstand stated as ecclesiology. God can do without the ordinary place. You cannot. And Calvin names the temptation with unusual precision:

Pride, or fastidiousness, or emulation, induces many to persuade themselves that they can profit sufficiently by reading and meditating in private, and thus to despise public meetings, and deem preaching superfluous.

— Calvin, Institutes IV.1, §5 (Beveridge, 1845)

Reading and meditating in private is a lamp burning under a basket. The flame is real. Nobody sees by it. And note the three roots Calvin puts under the choice — pride, fastidiousness, emulation. Not one of them is doubt, and not one is a hard providence. They are all forms of a man preferring his own company, which is very close to what a basket is.

4. Confessional Anchor

Verse 16 is a proof-text in the confession, hung under the doctrine of good works; verse 15 is not cited anywhere. That is as it should be, because verse 15 is not a doctrine, it is a picture — and the picture has two halves that the confession states plainly in the same chapter. The first half is that you did not light the lamp.

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 16, Paragraph 3

"Their ability to do good works is not at all of themselves, but wholly from the Spirit of Christ; and that they may be enabled thereunto, besides the graces they have already received, there is necessary an actual influence of the same Holy Spirit to work in them to will and to do of his good pleasure; yet they are not on this account to grow negligent, as if they were not bound to perform any duty unless upon a special motion of the Spirit; rather, they ought to be diligent in stirring up the grace of God that is in them."

Read Chapter 16, Of Good Works →

Read that paragraph with a lamp in your hand and it falls into two clean pieces, and they are the two pieces of this verse. Not at all of themselves, but wholly from the Spirit of Christ — the flame is borrowed, and no amount of effort will strike it out of a cold wick. And then the turn: yet they are not on this account to grow negligent. The confession refuses to let the first truth eat the second. You cannot light it; you are still answerable for where it sits. Diligent in stirring up the grace of God that is in them is precisely the labour of taking the basket off — not producing light, but clearing what is smothering it.

The second half is the end the whole thing is aimed at, and here the confession is unsparing:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 16, Paragraph 7

"Works done by unregenerate men, although for the matter of them they may be things which God commands, and of good use both to themselves and others; yet because they proceed not from a heart purified by faith, nor are done in a right manner according to the Word, nor to a right end, the glory of God, they are therefore sinful and cannot please God, nor make a man fit to receive grace from God; and yet their neglect of them is more sinful and displeasing to God."

Read Chapter 16, Of Good Works →

Three tests: the right root, the right manner, the right end. A work can pass the first two and fail the third, and failing the third is fatal to it. That is the confession's version of the difference between Matthew 5:16 and Matthew 6:1 — same act, same visibility, and everything turning on a right end, the glory of God. And do not skip the last clause, because it forecloses the obvious dodge. If a man reasons that since his motives are mixed he had better do nothing at all, the confession tells him his neglect is more sinful than the flawed work would have been. There is no exit through the basket.

Then there is the room the lamp is actually in. The catechism, in the questions added for the Christian household, says what all that are in the house is for:

Baptist Catechism · Question 115

Q. What has God appointed the Christian home to be?
A. "God has appointed the Christian home to be a place of worship, where parents and children together fear the Lord — praying, singing his praise, and feeding on his Word — so that the knowledge of God is kept alive and handed on from one generation to the next."

Work through the Catechism →

Kept alive and handed on. That is a lamp passed from wick to wick down a hallway, one generation at a time, and it is the smallest and most demanding application this verse has. The house is the first room the light is meant to serve, and it is the only room where a man cannot stage-manage what is seen.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the Sermon on the Mount memorize pack — carry it. It is a short sentence and an easy one to hold, and what it will do when you have it is interrupt you at the moment you are reaching for the tub.

The basket is already in your kitchen. Nobody in this verse is persecuted. No magistrate confiscates the lamp. The light goes out under a grain measure — an object so ordinary it has no story attached to it. That is how it happens to most men. Not a crisis of faith, not a public denial: a schedule, a job to protect, a reputation for being easy to work with, a conviction that religion is a private matter, a run of tired Sundays. Every one of those is a legitimate object being used for an illegitimate purpose. Ask which of yours is currently sitting over the flame, and be specific enough that the answer has a name.

Placement is the only thing you are being asked for. This is a relief and it is also the end of a favourite excuse. You are not being told to be more radiant. You are being told where to put yourself: in the room, at the height, in front of the people. Which room, on a Tuesday? The crew you actually work with. The table your children actually eat at. The church you are actually a member of, on the days it is inconvenient. A lamp does not choose its brightness. It gets carried to a place and set down.

Start with the house, because the house is the hard part. A man can be known for his convictions across a city and be a rumour in his own hallway. Christ's stated reach here is all that are in the house — the people who see you when the door is shut, when you are tired, when the day went badly and nobody is watching who could do anything for you. If the light does not reach them it has not yet reached anyone; it has only been performed. Your wife and your children are not the practice audience. They are the congregation.

You did not light it, so stop trying to relight it by force. When a man notices he has gone dim, his instinct is to resolve harder — more discipline, more intensity, a better system. But a wick that has been smothered is not restarted by willpower; it is restarted from another flame. Practically that means the ordinary means and nothing more exotic: the Word until it starts arguing with you again, preaching you have not sanded smooth, the Lord's table, and other Christians who are permitted to say hard things to you. Calvin's line stands: God is not confined to those means, but you are.

There are two ways to fail this verse and only one of them looks like cowardice. The basket is the obvious one. The other is the man who takes verse 16 as permission and starts arranging to be seen — a Christianity with a camera on it, good works that are somehow always documented. That man has obeyed the letter of this verse and inverted its purpose, and chapter six tells him what he has bought: his reward, in full, already paid. The test is not whether people see. It is who they think about afterwards.

The stand is not a safe place, and it was never advertised as one. Up on the bracket the lamp is in the draft, in reach of anyone in the room, and visible to whoever opens the door. That is the position, and the previous verse has already told you it is not negotiable and the beatitude before that told you what it costs. What you get in exchange is not safety. It is the thing verse 16 names and nothing else in the paragraph does: men see the work, and your Father gets the credit. A lamp under a basket keeps nothing safe anyway. It only goes out slower, in the dark, with nobody warmed by it.
