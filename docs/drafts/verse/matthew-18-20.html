Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 18:20

The verse everybody quotes about small crowds is not about small crowds. It begins with the word for, and everything turns on what it is giving a reason for.

For where two or three are gathered together in my name, there I am in the middle of them.

— World English Bible (public domain)

For where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them.

— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Read the sentence by itself and it sounds like consolation for a thin room. Read the word it starts with and it stops being that. For is not decoration; it is a hinge. Jesus is not opening a new subject here. He is giving the ground of something he has just finished saying — that what a congregation binds on earth is bound in heaven, and that two of them agreeing on earth will be answered by the Father. This sentence is the reason those things hold. Take the for away and you have a proverb about attendance. Leave it where Matthew put it and you have a warrant.

One more thing before the words. In both public-domain texts printed above, the men in the room are not gathering. They are gathered. Something has been done to them. They did not convene a meeting on their own initiative; they were assembled — and the one who assembled them is the one who then says he will be standing in the middle of them.

  • γάρ gar · "for" · Strong's G1063 · conjunction

    The lexicon calls it a causal and explanatory conjunction that introduces the reason or ground for the preceding statement. That is the whole job it does here. Verse 18 hands a congregation the authority to bind and loose. Verse 19 promises the Father's answer to two who agree. Verse 20 says why: because he is there. It is the smallest word in the sentence and it carries the load, which is precisely why it is the word that gets dropped when the verse is quoted on a poster.

  • δύο dyo · "two" · Strong's G1417 · numeral

    Our lexicon calls this the most basic numeral of pairing, witness, and division in the New Testament, appearing in legal as well as relational contexts. Both are live here. "Two or three" is not a minimum crowd size — it is a quotation. Four verses earlier Jesus told the offended brother to take one or two more along, "that at the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may be established," and that is itself a quotation of Deuteronomy 19:15, where a matter is established at the mouth of two or three witnesses and never at the mouth of one. The number is not chosen because it is small. It is chosen because it is the smallest number at which a thing can be settled rather than merely alleged.

  • συνάγω synagō · "gathered together" · Strong's G4863 · verb

    To gather, collect, or assemble — people, crops, or things. It is the word that gave the ancient world its name for the building where the congregation met: a synagogue is simply a gathering-place. Matthew is not describing an audience that showed up. He is describing an assembly, the same kind of thing the synagogue down the street was, now constituted by a different name and meeting under a different authority.

  • ὄνομα onoma · "name" · Strong's G3686 · noun

    In the ancient world a name was not merely a label but an expression of character and an exercise of authority; to do a thing "in the name of" someone is to do it with his authority, representing him, on his behalf. This is the condition in the verse, and it is the only condition. To be gathered in his name is not to have said his name out loud at the start. It is to be there on his warrant, doing his business, under his rule. Two men meeting in a church building to settle a grudge on their own terms have not met in his name, and no amount of the right vocabulary makes it otherwise.

  • εἰμί eimi · "am I" · Strong's G1510 · verb

    The fundamental verb of being — to be, to exist — in the first-person present. A man standing in Galilee says he will be present wherever two or three meet in his name, in every town, at every hour, all at once. No prophet in Israel ever spoke about himself that way. Matthew has been building to it since his first chapter, where the child is named Immanuel, "God with us," and he closes the Gospel on the same note: "Behold, I am with you always, even to the end of the age." Chapter 18 is the middle term of that bracket. The presence promised at the beginning and at the end is the presence promised here — in the room where the hard case is being decided.

  • μέσος mesos · "the midst" · Strong's G3319 · adjective

    Middle, midst, centre, among; in the phrase our translations render "in the midst," it locates someone among a group rather than beside it. Not above them and not outside the door listening. In the middle. It is the position of the one who presides — where the table is set, where a man stands when the matter is being weighed. Whatever else this verse promises, it does not promise a distant blessing on the proceedings. It puts Christ inside the circle.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 18:16 — But if he doesn't listen, take one or two more with you, that at the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may be established.

Matthew 18:17 — If he refuses to listen to them, tell it to the assembly. If he refuses to hear the assembly also, let him be to you as a Gentile or a tax collector.

Matthew 18:18 — Most certainly I tell you, whatever things you bind on earth will have been bound in heaven, and whatever things you release on earth will have been released in heaven.

Matthew 18:19 — Again, assuredly I tell you, that if two of you will agree on earth concerning anything that they will ask, it will be done for them by my Father who is in heaven.

Matthew 18:21 — Then Peter came and said to him, "Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? Until seven times?"

Matthew 18 is the church-order chapter of the Gospels. It is also, with Matthew 16, one of only two places in any Gospel where the word for "church" appears at all. Mark never uses it. Luke never uses it. John never uses it. Matthew uses it twice: once at Caesarea Philippi, where Christ says he will build it, and once here in verse 17, where he tells you what to do when a brother will not repent — tell it to the assembly. The King James says tell it unto the church. Same word, same institution, and this verse is standing three lines underneath it.

The discourse runs in order, and the order is the argument. It opens with the little ones and a warning against being the man who makes one of them stumble. It moves to the shepherd who leaves ninety-nine to go after one. Then to your brother who has sinned against you: go to him alone. If he will not hear you, take one or two more. If he still will not hear, tell it to the church. If he will not hear the church, treat him as an outsider. Only then come binding and loosing, the promise about two agreeing in prayer, and this verse holding all of it up. And the instant it is finished, Peter asks the question every man asks the moment discipline has been explained to him: how many times do I have to forgive? The answer is seventy times seven, and then a parable about a servant forgiven a debt he could never pay who would not forgive a debt he could have collected.

So the setting is not a sparsely attended prayer breakfast. It is the hardest thing a congregation is ever asked to do. Jesus tells a handful of ordinary men that when they act together, in his name, on the matter of an unrepentant brother, heaven is not merely watching. Heaven has already ruled, and he himself is standing in the room. That is what makes church discipline bearable and what makes it terrifying at the same time. A congregation does not hold this authority because it is large, or credentialed, or well regarded in the town. It holds it because he is there.

Which is also why the verse cannot be shrunk down to a pat on the back for low turnout. It is bigger than that reading, not smaller. What it grants to two or three, it grants because of who is with them. What it withholds from a great assembly, it withholds for exactly the same reason.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin reaches for this verse twice in the Institutes, and the two uses cut in opposite directions. A man should have both of them.

The first comes where he is defining what makes a church a church. He has just conceded that we cannot see with certainty who truly belongs to Christ — that knowledge is God's. So how do you recognise a church when you find one? Not by its size, its lineage, or its reputation:

Wherever we see the word of God sincerely preached and heard, wherever we see the sacraments administered according to the institution of Christ, there we cannot have any doubt that the Church of God has some existence, since his promise cannot fail, "Where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them."

Calvin, Institutes IV.1 — read the chapter

That is the comfort in the verse, and it is a hard-headed comfort rather than a sentimental one. It does not say the small gathering feels like a church, or counts as one for encouragement's sake. It says that where the Word is truly preached and the ordinances truly kept, the church of God is actually there, because the promise cannot fail. The evidence is objective. You can check it on a Sunday.

The second use runs the other way. In the chapter on councils, Calvin faces men who quote this very verse to prove that an assembly of bishops cannot err. He grants the verse its full weight, and then reads the condition:

If we consult Scripture on the authority of councils, there is no promise more remarkable than that which is contained in these words of our Saviour, "Where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them." But this is just as applicable to any particular meeting as to a universal council.

And yet the important part of the question does not lie here, but in the condition which is added—viz. that Christ will be in the midst of a council, provided it be assembled in his name.

At present I only reply in one word, that our Saviour's promise is made to those only who assemble in his name.

Calvin, Institutes IV.9 — read the chapter

Same sentence, opposite audience. To the two or three who really are his, it says: you are not too small for him. To the thousand who are not, it says: the number buys you nothing. Calvin goes on to deny outright that men are assembled in Christ's name when they add to the Word of God or take from it and settle matters at their own pleasure. He is not being clever. He is reading the qualifier the way the verse reads it — as a claim about whose word is running the room, which is a thing you cannot vote yourself into and cannot pronounce over a meeting after the fact.

Both readings are the same doctrine seen from two sides. The promise is real and it is conditional. Strip the condition and the verse becomes a blessing on any gathering that invokes it. Strip the promise and the local congregation is left with nothing but its own opinion.

4. Confessional Anchor

The framers of the 1689 read the verse this way too, and you can see it in their proof texts. Where the confession describes Christ commanding his people to walk together in local congregations, it cites Matthew 18:15–20. Where it grants each congregation the authority it needs for worship and discipline, it cites Matthew 18:17–18. They did not treat this as a devotional line. They treated it as a charter.

In the exercise of this power wherewith he is so entrusted, the Lord Jesus calls out of the world unto himself, through the ministry of his Word and by his Spirit, those that are given to him by his Father, that they may walk before him in all the ways of obedience which he prescribes to them in his Word. Those thus called he commands to walk together in particular societies or churches, for their mutual edification and for the due performance of that public worship which he requires of them in the world.

1689 LBCF, ch. 26, para. 5 — proof texts include Matthew 18:15–20

To each of these churches thus gathered, according to his mind declared in his Word, he has given all that power and authority which is in any way needful for their carrying on that order in worship and discipline which he has instituted for them to observe — with commands and rules for the due and right exerting and executing of that power.

1689 LBCF, ch. 26, para. 7 — proof texts include Matthew 18:17–18

Notice what the confession will not say. It does not say a believer may attach himself to a church if it suits his schedule. It says Christ commands those he calls to walk together in particular societies or churches. And the reason a particular society of ordinary people holds any authority at all is the reason this verse gives: he is in the middle of it. The catechism turns the same thing into a plain duty laid on the newly baptised man.

Q101. What is the duty of those who are rightly baptized?

It is the duty of those who are rightly baptized to give themselves up to some particular and orderly church of Jesus Christ, that they may walk in all the commandments and ordinances of the Lord blameless.

The Baptist Catechism, Q101

Some particular and orderly church. Not the church in the abstract, not the church you follow online, not the church of all true believers everywhere. One church, with a name, that meets at an address you can drive to, whose members can tell when you are missing.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in The Wheel memorize pack — carry it. Carry the whole sentence, starting with for. The habit of memorising the back half only is how the verse got misused in the first place.

Then take four things off it.

Go alone first. The chapter is explicit and the order is not optional: him and you, by yourselves. Most men do this backwards. They assemble the two or three before they have said a word to the man, and by the time they get to him it is no longer a conversation, it is an ambush he can smell coming. The witnesses are not a coalition you build to win. They are what you resort to after the private attempt has failed, and their job is to establish the matter, not to outnumber him.

The condition is on you, not on the room. "In my name" is the load-bearing phrase, and nobody else can satisfy it for you. You can be in the right building, in the right seat, at the right hour, and still be there in your own name — protecting your standing, running your grievance, gathering allies. The test is not whether Christ's name was spoken. It is whether his authority is what is actually deciding the matter.

Your small church is a real church. If the Word is preached honestly there and the ordinances are kept as Christ instituted them, Calvin's judgment applies and there is no doubt about it: the church of God exists at that address. Not a placeholder until something better comes along. Whatever number is on the count sheet, the promise attached to two or three is not a lowering of the standard. It is the standard.

And when that church acts, it is not a committee. This is the costly half. If Christ stands in the middle of the assembly, then its decision about an unrepentant brother is not an opinion you may take or leave, and its call on your own life is not advice. Men who want the comfort of this verse without its jurisdiction want the wrong half of it. He is in the midst as Lord or he is not in the midst at all.

Two men and an open Bible in a break room, gathered in his name, have him standing between them. A thousand men gathered in their own have a crowd. Nothing in the verse depends on the size of the room, and everything in it depends on whose name is over the door.
