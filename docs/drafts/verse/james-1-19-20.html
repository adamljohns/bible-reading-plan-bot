Verse Study · James

James 1:19-20

Every man quotes this as advice about listening. James wrote it as instruction on how to receive the word that saves a soul — and a warning about the one thing that makes a man unable to.

So, then, my beloved brothers, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger; for the anger of man doesn’t produce the righteousness of God.— World English Bible (public domain)

Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath: For the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

It gets quoted as manners. Listen more, talk less, keep your temper — good advice, printed on coffee mugs, and not what James is doing. The sentence is bolted down at both ends. So then hooks it back to what came before it. For hangs a reason underneath it. Pull either bolt and you are left with a fortune cookie that happens to be true.

Notice what the reason does not do. James tells you what a man's wrath fails to produce. He does not, in this verse, tell you what does produce it. That is not coyness; the answer is two verses down the page. But the restraint matters, because the argument here is deliberately negative. He is closing a door men keep walking through — the one marked my anger is getting something done.

And notice who is addressed. The command is third person singular — estō, "let him be" — issued about every man, one at a time. Not "let us all try." There is no crowd to stand in. James begins with agapētos, beloved, and adelphos, brother, so you know before the command lands that it is family talk and not a lecture from a stranger. Then he issues it to you by yourself.

Three commands

  • ταχύς tachys · "swift" · Strong's G5036

    Swift, fast, quick — the plain adjective of speed. It is the root behind the tachu of Revelation, Christ's "I am coming quickly." James uses it exactly once, and he spends it on the ear. Everything after it in the sentence is brakes. A man's factory settings run the other way: fast on the mouth, fast on the temper, unhurried only about listening. The verse is not asking you to slow down. It is asking you to move your speed somewhere else.

  • ἀκούω akouō · "to hear" · Strong's G191

    The ordinary verb for hearing — sound arriving at an ear — but in Scripture it never stops there. Hearing carries response inside it; the Greek is doing the work of the Hebrew shama, where to hear and to obey are one motion, not two. That is why "he that hath ears to hear, let him hear" is not a redundancy. So "swift to hear" is not a compliment paid to good listeners. It is a command to be quick to obey, and verse 22 will say so with no cover at all.

  • βραδύς bradys · "slow" (twice) · Strong's G1021

    Slow — of movement, of speech, of understanding, of any process at all. The New Testament uses it three times, and one of the others is a rebuke: on the Emmaus road, Jesus calls the disciples slow to believe what the prophets had spoken. Same adjective, opposite verdict. Slowness is not a virtue in itself. It is a virtue or a fault depending entirely on what you are being slow about, which is why James is careful to name the two things. He puts the brake on precisely the two a man is proudest of being fast at.

  • λαλέω laleō · "to speak" · Strong's G2980

    To speak, to talk, to say — often with the weight on the act of speaking rather than on its content, where legō (G3004) leans the other way. The distinction is not airtight in New Testament usage, but here it earns its keep. James is not forbidding you to say true things; the whole letter is a man saying true things bluntly. He is slowing the reflex to make noise. The word for it in chapter 1 verse 26 is bridle — the tongue does not get cut out, it gets a bit put in it.

  • ὀργή orgē · "wrath" · Strong's G3709

    Wrath, anger, indignation — and this is the settled, purposeful kind, not the sudden boil that its near neighbour thymos names. That changes who the command is talking to. "Slow to wrath" is not only aimed at the man who shouts. It is aimed just as squarely at the man who never raises his voice and has kept a ledger on his brother for eleven years. He is slow to thymos and quick to orgē, and he is the one James has in his sights. One more thing about this noun: it is the same word the New Testament uses for the wrath of God, which is holy, judicial and never wrong. The word is identical. The two things are not.

One reason

  • κατεργάζομαι katergazomai · "worketh" · Strong's G2716

    To accomplish, to produce, to work a thing all the way through to completion — the kata prefix adding thoroughness to plain ergazomai, labour. And James has already used this exact verb in this exact chapter: the trying of your faith worketh patience (1:3). Seventeen verses apart, the same word, once positive and once denied. Testing produces endurance. Wrath produces nothing. James has set two machines on the bench, told you which one runs, and named the one you keep reaching for anyway.

  • δικαιοσύνη dikaiosynē · "the righteousness" · Strong's G1343

    Righteousness, justice, right standing — the word carries both the character of being right and the legal status of being declared right, and Paul spends two letters on the second sense. Which does James mean here? Almost certainly the first: the conduct God counts righteous, the life He requires, what chapter 3 will call the fruit of righteousness sown in peace. He is not warning that your temper will cost you your justification. He is telling you it will not manufacture obedience — not in you, and not in the man you are shouting at.

  • ἀνήρ anēr · "of man" (v. 20) · Strong's G435

    Two different nouns for "man" sit one verse apart, and the Strong's-tagged text makes the switch plain. In verse 19, "every man" is anthrōpos — a human being of either sex, the general term, the word behind "Son of Man." In verse 20, "the wrath of man" is anēr — an adult male, and in the New Testament frequently a husband in particular. Whether James narrowed on purpose or simply varied his vocabulary, the noun on the page in verse 20 is the one for a grown man. Take it, at minimum, as a coincidence worth sitting still for: the command is issued to everybody, and the warning is worded with a man in view.

2. Where It Sits

James 1:18 — Of his own will he gave birth to us by the word of truth, that we should be a kind of first fruits of his creatures.

James 1:21 — Therefore, putting away all filthiness and overflowing of wickedness, receive with humility the implanted word, which is able to save your souls.

James writes to the twelve tribes in the Dispersion — believers scattered, under pressure, and behaving the way pressured people behave. The letter's running complaint is talk that never becomes anything: faith without works, partiality dressed as hospitality, quarrels, and a tongue nobody is governing. He is not writing a book about anger. He is writing a book about whether the word you have heard has actually gone in.

Five verses before ours he lays out the anatomy of temptation, and it is a birth. Desire conceives; it bears sin; sin, full grown, brings forth death. Then in verse 18 he swings the same verb around — apokyeō, to give birth — and uses it of God: of his own will he gave birth to us by the word of truth. Two deliveries, three verses apart, the same word, opposite outcomes. One line runs desire, then sin, then death. The other runs God's will, then the word of truth, then you.

That is what so then is standing on. Given that God brought you into existence a second time by means of a word, here is how you will handle words from now on. The triad is not free-floating wisdom. It is the posture of a man receiving the thing that made him.

And the sentence after ours makes it explicit. Put away the filth and the overflow of wickedness, James says, and receive with meekness the engrafted word, which is able to save your souls. Two of that verse's words are worth having. Prautēs, meekness, is not softness; classical Greek used it of a horse broken to the bit — full power, fully directed. Emphytos, engrafted or implanted, is agricultural: a graft set into living wood, not a leaflet handed across a counter. Then verse 22: be doers of the word and not hearers only. Then the mirror, and the perfect law of liberty, and finally verse 26, where the man who cannot bridle his tongue has a religion that is worth nothing.

Put in that frame, the last clause explains itself. An angry man cannot be taught, because anger is already a verdict. Every sentence that reaches him gets sorted into evidence for or against a case he has finished trying. He is not slow to hear because he is busy; he is slow to hear because he has no questions left. And if the aim of receiving the word is a righteous life, and wrath cannot produce one, then wrath is not even a rough road to the goal. It is a road going somewhere else.

James returns to it in chapter 3, where the tongue is a fire and the wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceful, gentle, reasonable, full of mercy and good fruits — and then he states our verse forward instead of backward: the fruit of righteousness is sown in peace by those who make peace (3:18). Righteousness has a soil. The soil is peace. You cannot grow it in the ground you have just scorched.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin's commentary on James is not in this library, so what follows is the Institutes, where he takes up both halves of this verse under other headings — and takes them up harder than most men preach them.

On being swift to hear

Calvin's entire doctrine of Scripture assumes the posture James is commanding. He closes the first book of the Institutes with it, aimed at men who reject what Scripture plainly says because it exceeds their capacity:

Our true wisdom is to embrace with meek docility, and without reservation, whatever the Holy Scriptures have delivered.

— Calvin, Institutes I.18

That is verse 19 and verse 21 in one line: teachable, and without reservation — which is to say, no clause held back for review. And when Calvin needs a name for that temper, he reaches for James by name. Arguing far away from our text, deep in the fourth book, over the mysteries of faith and the limits of ordinary reasoning, he writes:

in the mysteries of faith we do not consult ordinary understanding, but, with the calm teachableness and gentle spirit that James commends, receive the doctrine that has come from heaven.

— Calvin, Institutes IV.17

Note what he assumes without arguing for it: the man who is quick to talk back is not merely rude, he is unequipped. He will not get the doctrine, because the doctrine is received in a posture he refuses to take. And Calvin will not let "swift to hear" evaporate into private reading, either. Hearing has a place and a voice attached to it:

every one who listens with docility to the ministers whom God appoints, will know by the beneficial result, that for good reason God is pleased with this method of teaching, and for good reason has laid believers under this modest yoke.

— Calvin, Institutes IV.1

This modest yoke. Calvin knows exactly how a capable man feels about sitting still while another man explains something to him, and he calls it a yoke on purpose. It is also, he says, the method God is pleased with.

On the wrath of man

Calvin does not file anger under temperament. He files it under murder — in his exposition of the Decalogue, where his governing point is that the Lawgiver reads hearts and not merely hands:

But God, whose eye nothing escapes, and who regards not the outward appearance so much as purity of heart, under the prohibition of murder, adultery, and thefts includes wrath, hatred, lust, covetousness, and all other things of a similar nature. Being a spiritual Lawgiver, he speaks to the soul not less than the body. The murder which the soul commits is wrath and hatred; the theft, covetousness and avarice; and the adultery, lust.

— Calvin, Institutes II.8

Later in the same chapter, expounding the sixth commandment directly, he presses in until there is nowhere left to stand:

This commandment, therefore, prohibits the murder of the heart, and requires a sincere desire to preserve our brother's life. The hand, indeed, commits the murder, but the mind, under the influence of wrath and hatred, conceives it.

How can you be angry with your brother, without passionately longing to do him harm? If you must not be angry with him, neither must you hate him, hatred being nothing but inveterate anger.

— Calvin, Institutes II.8

Hatred being nothing but inveterate anger. That is Calvin arriving at James's noun from the other direction — the settled kind, the kind that has had time. He does not treat the grudge as a milder thing than the outburst. He treats it as the outburst that has moved in and unpacked.

And then the line that sits directly against verse 20, because it says what wrath does produce, having been told what it does not:

Where either wrath or hatred is, there is an inclination to do mischief.

— Calvin, Institutes II.8

Put the two sentences side by side and you have the whole doctrine. James: the wrath of man does not produce the righteousness of God. Calvin: where wrath is, there is an inclination to do mischief. Anger is not idle. It is productive. It is simply productive of the wrong thing, and the man carrying it is generally the last to be told.

4. Confessional Anchor

Neither the 1689 nor the Baptist Catechism lists James 1:19-20 among its proof-texts. They did not need to. Between them they cover exactly the two things this verse is about: how the Word gets into a man, and where a man's good works actually come from.

First, the hearing. The confession will not let "swift to hear" float free of an object and a means:

The grace of faith, whereby the elect are enabled to believe to the saving of their souls, is the work of the Spirit of Christ in their hearts; and is ordinarily wrought by the ministry of the Word

— 1689 LBCF, ch. 14, paragraph 1

The Catechism then itemizes what being swift to hear actually looks like on a given Sunday, and it is a longer list than most men are running:

That the Word may become effectual to salvation, we must attend to it with diligence, preparation, and prayer; receive it with faith and love, lay it up in our hearts, and practice it in our lives.

— The Baptist Catechism, Q95

Diligence, preparation, prayer, faith, love, storage, practice. Seven things, and only one of them happens while the preaching is going on. That is what James is asking for when he says swift.

Now the other half. The confession's chapter on good works opens with a sentence that could have been written with an angry man sitting in the front pew:

Good works are only such as God has commanded in his Holy Word, and not such as without the warrant thereof are devised by men out of blind zeal, or upon any pretense of good intentions.

— 1689 LBCF, ch. 16, paragraph 1

Blind zeal. Pretense of good intentions. That is a portrait of the man in verse 20, and note that he is not accused of doing nothing. He is doing a great deal, at speed, with reasons. The confession says it does not count — which is only James's verb, worketh not, stated as a rule.

Two paragraphs later comes the positive that James leaves out of verse 20 and supplies in verse 21:

Their ability to do good works is not at all of themselves, but wholly from the Spirit of Christ

— 1689 LBCF, ch. 16, paragraph 3

If wrath will not produce the righteousness of God, neither will a calmer, better-managed version of you. The confession puts the source outside the man entirely. Which is exactly why James's answer to a temper is not "try harder" but "receive the implanted word."

And one line from the Catechism on the sixth commandment, because Calvin was not being eccentric — this is simply how the Reformed read it:

The sixth commandment absolutely forbids the taking away of our own life, or the life of our neighbor unjustly, or whatever tends to it.

— The Baptist Catechism, Q74

Or whatever tends to it. Four words, and they take in the raised voice, the slammed door, the eleven-year ledger, and the silence you are using as a weapon.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the PROVEN Men memorize pack — carry it. Carry it because it is one of the few verses that will contradict you in real time. Most memorized Scripture arrives as comfort. This one arrives while you are mid-sentence.

Swift to hear means the Word first. The verse is about your Bible and your pastor before it is about your wife. Get the order right and the rest follows; get it backwards and you will work on your listening skills for years while remaining unteachable about the only thing that could change you. A man who talks back to Scripture all week is not going to suddenly turn attentive at the kitchen table.

Slow to speak is a governor, not a gag. You are not being told to have no answer. You are being told that the answer which arrives in the first two seconds is your answer, formed before the words were finished, and it is almost never the best one you have. The gap between hearing and replying is where thought happens. Most men have paved it over.

Anger makes a man deaf, and it feels like clarity. That is the trap. Wrath does not arrive announcing itself as bias; it arrives as certainty — as finally seeing the situation for what it is. But it is a verdict handed down before the testimony is in, and a man who has already ruled cannot hear. Slow to wrath is, in practice, slow to conclude.

The lie this verse kills. Every angry man believes his anger is accomplishing something. It gets the kids moving. It gets the crew straight. It gets the point across. James does not argue about whether it works — he tells you what it does not produce. Compliance, yes. Silence, usually. Fear, reliably. Righteousness, never. What your volume teaches your children is your volume, and they will use it on someone smaller than them.

This is not a ban on anger. Scripture does not hand you an anger-free Christianity. Paul writes be angry, and don't sin, and the very noun James uses is the noun the New Testament uses for the settled wrath of God against evil, which is holy and never once wrong. What James denies is not that anger exists, or that it is ever warranted. He denies its productivity. You can be angry, and be right, and still be building nothing.

Moses at the rock. He had the facts, the office, the provocation, and a congregation that had earned every word of it. Hear now, you rebels! Shall we bring water out of this rock for you? He struck it twice, and water came out abundantly, and the people drank. It worked. And he did not enter the land. The text names his failure as unbelief — a failure to sanctify God before Israel — not as temper, so do not make it say more than it does. But look at the shape of the scene: right man, right facts, real result, and a door closed behind him. The wrath of man got water out of a rock and still did not produce the righteousness of God.

The remedy is in verse 21, not in your jaw. James never tells you to try to be calm. He tells you to put away the filth and receive the implanted word with meekness. The cure for a temper is not a tighter grip on the temper — that is white-knuckling, and it lasts until you are tired, which is Thursday. The cure is the Word getting further in, week after week, under preaching, with diligence and preparation and prayer, until it is grafted into living wood rather than laid on the counter. Meekness is not the absence of force. It is the horse broken to the bit: everything you had, now going where it is steered.

What that looks like on a Tuesday. Three seconds before you answer, every time, including when you are right. The message you draft and do not send. A question asked before a statement is made. Letting your wife finish a sentence you have heard the beginning of before — because you have not, in fact, heard the end of it. Hearing your son's reason all the way out even when you are sure it is a lie; you will address the lie better in a minute than you will now. And once, this week, sit down and count the last three times your anger got you what you wanted, then ask honestly what it built.

None of it will hold on willpower. It holds because a man who has been given birth by the word of truth has a reason to want that word more than he wants the last word. That is why James put the new birth in front of the command instead of behind it. You are not being asked to become a quieter man. You are being asked to act like what you already are.
