Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:10

The last beatitude ends where the first one started — theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven. Jesus closes the list by naming the men the world runs off, and hands them the same deed he handed the beggars in verse 3.

Blessed are those who have been persecuted for righteousness’ sake, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven.— World English Bible (public domain)

Blessed are they which are persecuted for righteousness' sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Read the line for what it does not say. It does not say blessed are those who suffer. Suffering is not a qualification. The world is full of it, most of it is meaningless, and none of it is a beatitude on its own. The sentence says blessed are those persecuted for righteousness’ sake, and the whole weight of the line hangs on that small prepositional phrase. Take it out and you have a sentiment that would comfort anybody having a bad year. Leave it in and you have a test that most of what men call persecution will not pass.

Then notice the second thing, which is stranger. The other seven beatitudes name something a man is or something he does — poor in spirit, mourning, gentle, hungry for righteousness, merciful, pure in heart, making peace. This one names something done to him. He is the object of the verb. It is the only beatitude in the list that a man cannot go out and perform. Somebody else has to do it to him, and Jesus plainly assumes somebody will.

The tense is worth a moment too. The World English Bible and the NASB both read have been persecuted — a thing already done to a man, a condition he is standing in, not a danger he is bracing against. Other translations flatten it to the plain present. Either reading, the sermon is not speculating about a possibility. Two sentences later it stops describing a category of people and starts addressing the men sitting on the hillside: Blessed are you when people reproach you.

  • μακάριος makarios · “Blessed” · Strong’s G3107

    Not emotional happiness dependent on circumstances, but the deep well-being of those who are right with God. Greek writers had used the word of the gods, who lived free of human trouble, and of the dead, who were past caring.

    Which is exactly why the word lands so hard here. Makarios was vocabulary for people beyond the reach of harm, and Jesus fastens it to a man who is currently being hunted. It is not a description of how he feels. It is a verdict rendered over him by the only court that will still be sitting when the persecution is over. A man can be makarios on the worst afternoon of his life and know it by testimony rather than by sensation.

  • διώκω diōkō · “are persecuted” · Strong’s G1377

    The verb carries two senses that live side by side in the New Testament: to pursue with hostile intent — to persecute; and to pursue with earnest, positive intent — to press toward, to strive after. Paul was once the great diōkō-persecutor of the church; he became the great diōkō-pursuer of the prize.

    So the picture is not of a man who tripped over some misfortune. It is of a man being chased. Somebody has set his mind on him and is running him down. That is the difference between persecution and hard luck, and it is why the beatitude is not a consolation prize for a difficult life. It also means the same energy is on both sides of the field: the men doing the hunting are pursuing, and the man they are hunting was told to pursue righteousness, faith, love, and peace. One word, aimed two directions. What decides everything is what a man is running at.

  • ἕνεκα heneka · “for … sake” · Strong’s G1752

    A causal and purposive preposition — “for the sake of,” “on account of,” “because of.” It introduces the reason behind an action: what someone suffers or acts for.

    This is the hinge of the verse and the reason the beatitude cannot be claimed casually. Heneka does not describe the suffering; it names the cause of it. The same beating, the same firing, the same silent room can be blessed or simply deserved depending on the word that follows this preposition. Peter says the identical thing in plain terms — let none of you suffer as a murderer, or a thief, or an evil doer, or a meddler in other men’s matters. Persecution earns nothing. Persecution heneka dikaiosynēs is what the Lord blesses.

  • δικαιοσύνη dikaiosynē · “righteousness” · Strong’s G1343

    One of the central theological terms of the New Testament: righteousness, justice, right standing. It denotes both the character of being right and the legal status of being declared right.

    Both halves are in play. The status came first — a man does not manufacture the righteousness he is hated for; it was credited to him. But what draws the fire is the character, the visible life that follows the credit. And the sermon does not leave that life to the imagination. Verse 6 says a blessed man is starving for this righteousness. Verse 20 says it has to run deeper than the professionals’. Then the rest of chapter 5 spells it out line by line — anger, lust, marriage, oaths, retaliation, enemies. That is the righteousness in question. Not a set of opinions a man holds. A way of living he cannot hide.

  • αὐτός autos · “theirs” · Strong’s G846

    The most common word in the Greek New Testament — the ordinary pronoun, but in emphatic use it stresses identity and personal agency: himself, the same.

    Ordinary word, extraordinary placement. The men who own the kingdom are these men — the ones just described, not some better class of Christian standing behind them. Whatever the world has taken off them, the pronoun says the title did not transfer. It is theirs.

  • βασιλεία basileia · “the kingdom” · Strong’s G932

    Kingdom, reign, royal power — the central theme of Jesus’ preaching. It refers not to a geographic territory but to God’s active reign, his sovereign rule breaking into the present age. It is both present and future, and that tension defines Christian existence between the first and second comings of Christ.

    Watch the tense of the promise. Every other beatitude in the list pays out in the future: they shall be comforted, they shall inherit, they shall see God. This one and verse 3 pay out now. Theirs is. Present indicative. The man being run out of a job for his integrity is not waiting on a settlement. He already holds the deed to the only country that is not going to fall.

  • οὐρανός ouranos · “of heaven” · Strong’s G3772

    The abode of God — the heavens. Matthew’s standing idiom: Our Father in heaven.

    Heaven in this sermon is not mainly a destination. It is a jurisdiction, and more immediately a place where accounts are kept. Two verses on, Jesus tells the persecuted man that his reward there is great. The persecutor can reach a man’s wages, his reputation, and his body. He cannot reach the ledger.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 5:9 — Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called children of God.

Matthew 5:11 — “Blessed are you when people reproach you, persecute you, and say all kinds of evil against you falsely, for my sake.

This is the eighth and last of the beatitudes proper, and it closes a circle. Verse 3 blessed the poor in spirit, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven. Verse 10 blesses the persecuted with the identical words. Everything in between — mourning, meekness, hunger, mercy, purity, peacemaking — sits inside that bracket. The list opens with a man who has nothing and closes with a man who is losing what he has left, and both are handed the same kingdom. That is not accidental structure. It is the sermon telling you what kind of people the kingdom is actually populated with.

Then the pattern breaks. Verse 11 stops talking about a category and starts talking to the men on the hill: Blessed are you. And the reason for the hostility shifts with it — from for righteousness’ sake to for my sake. Those two phrases are laid over each other on purpose. In this sermon righteousness is not a free-standing ethical program a man could adopt without Christ. It is Christ’s own righteousness, and the life it produces, and men hate the one because they hate the other. Verse 12 then widens the frame to the whole Old Testament: that is how they persecuted the prophets who were before you. A persecuted believer is not an anomaly in the story. He is in the main line of it.

What follows is the part men skip. Having just told them they will be hunted, Jesus immediately says: you are the salt of the earth, you are the light of the world, a city on a hill cannot be hidden, let your light shine before men. The order is deliberate and it forecloses the obvious escape. The right response to verse 10 is not to lower your profile. It is to keep the light where people can see it and let the chips fall.

The rest of Scripture reads the verse the same way. Paul states it as a flat rule — all who desire to live godly in Christ Jesus will suffer persecution. Jesus grounds it in himself — a servant is not greater than his lord; if they persecuted me, they will also persecute you. Peter repeats the beatitude almost word for word, and adds the guardrail about suffering as an evildoer. And Luke shows what the doctrine looks like in the body: the apostles walked out of a flogging rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for Jesus’ name.

  • 1 Peter 3:14

    But even if you should suffer for righteousness’ sake, you are blessed. “Don’t fear what they fear, neither be troubled.”

  • 2 Timothy 3:12

    Yes, and all who desire to live godly in Christ Jesus will suffer persecution.

  • Acts 5:41

    They therefore departed from the presence of the council, rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for Jesus’ name.

  • Isaiah 53:8

    He was taken away by oppression and judgment; and as for his generation, who considered that he was cut off out of the land of the living and stricken for the disobedience of my people?

  • Psalm 34:19

    Many are the afflictions of the righteous, but the LORD delivers him out of them all.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin devotes a whole chapter of the Institutes to bearing the cross, and one section of it is a direct exposition of this verse — he cites Matthew 5:10 by name. It is the most useful thing in the Reformed tradition on this text because he refuses to treat persecution as an interruption of the Christian life. He treats it as the shape of it.

He starts by setting expectations, and he does not soften them:

Those whom the Lord has chosen and honoured with his intercourse must prepare for a hard, laborious, troubled life, a life full of many and various kinds of evils

Calvin, Institutes III.8

The reason is not that God is stingy with his people. It is that the Son went first, and the household follows the pattern of its head:

as he passed to celestial glory through a labyrinth of many woes, so we too are conducted thither through various tribulations.

Calvin, Institutes III.8

He says the same thing elsewhere in blunter form, and it is worth setting beside our verse because it explains why the kingdom and the suffering arrive in the same sentence rather than in that order:

as it behaved him first to suffer, and then to enter into his glory, so we also, through much tribulation, enter the kingdom of heaven.

Calvin, Institutes III.18

Then he comes to Matthew 5:10 itself. Read what he calls it:

There is singular consolation, moreover, when we are persecuted for righteousness’ sake. For our thought should then be, How high the honour which God bestows upon us in distinguishing us by the special badge of his soldiers.

Calvin, Institutes III.8

A badge. Not a wound to be endured quietly and mentioned to nobody — an insignia issued by a commanding officer, which is only ever given to men who were sent somewhere. Calvin has taken the one thing a man is most tempted to read as evidence that he got it wrong and reclassified it as evidence that he was enlisted.

Now, the scope. This is the question every man reaches for next: does this only count for missionaries and martyrs? Calvin answers it immediately, and his answer is wider than most men expect:

By suffering persecution for righteousness’ sake, I mean not only striving for the defence of the Gospel, but for the defence of righteousness in any way.

Calvin, Institutes III.8

Then he spells out what that covers, and it reaches the ordinary working life of an ordinary man:

Whether, therefore, in maintaining the truth of God against the lies of Satan, or defending the good and innocent against the injuries of the bad, we are obliged to incur the offence and hatred of the world, so as to endanger life, fortune, or honour, let us not grieve or decline so far to spend ourselves for God; let us not think ourselves wretched in those things in which he with his own lips has pronounced us blessed.

Calvin, Institutes III.8

Defending the good and innocent against the injuries of the bad. That is a shift floor and a school board and a family, not just a pulpit. And the closing clause is the argument in one line: do not think yourself wretched in the very thing Christ called blessed with his own mouth. Your read on your situation and his read on it cannot both stand. One of them is going to have to yield, and it is not going to be his.

Calvin then runs the exchange rate, item by item, and it is the most bracing paragraph in the chapter:

if driven from our homes we have a more welcome reception into the family of God; if vexed and despised, we are more firmly rooted in Christ; if stigmatised by disgrace and ignominy, we have a higher place in the kingdom of God; and if we are slain, entrance is thereby given us to eternal life.

Calvin, Institutes III.8

Every line trades something a man can lose for something he cannot. That is not a trick of perspective. It is the same accounting Jesus does two verses later when he tells the persecuted that their reward in heaven is great.

But Calvin will not let this turn into a performance of serenity, and here he is better than most of the men who quote him. He spends an entire section attacking the idea that a Christian under the cross should feel nothing:

The cheerfulness required, however, does not imply a total insensibility to pain.

Calvin, Institutes III.8

He calls the alternative an iron philosophy, notes that it was invented mostly by men who speculate more than they act, and points out that the Lord himself grieved and wept. Then he describes what fortitude actually looks like from the inside, which is not what the brochure says:

the believer in this displays his fortitude, that though fully sensible of the bitterness and labouring grievously, he still withstands and struggles boldly

Calvin, Institutes III.8

Fully sensible of the bitterness. The man in this beatitude is allowed to feel every ounce of it. He is not required to pretend the cost is small. He is required to keep standing.

One guardrail before we leave Calvin, because this verse has been abused by men who wanted a holy name for a fight they picked. Writing about those who tear away from the church over the sins of others, he grants that even good men fall into what he calls an inconsiderate zeal for righteousness, and he warns where it ends:

Let all the godly study to provide themselves with these weapons, lest, while they deem themselves strenuous and ardent defenders of righteousness, they revolt from the kingdom of heaven, which is the only kingdom of righteousness.

Calvin, Institutes IV.1

Read that against Matthew 5:10 and it cuts the right way. A man can be so certain he is being persecuted for righteousness that he walks himself out of the only kingdom where righteousness is found. The beatitude promises the kingdom to men the world drives out. It promises nothing to men who drive themselves out.

4. Confessional Anchor

Neither the confession nor the catechism has a chapter on persecution. What they have is the three things this verse assumes and never argues: that the righteousness in question is not the sufferer’s own, that the kingdom he is promised is genuinely coming, and that he will still be standing when it does.

Start with the righteousness, because a man under pressure will start auditing his own and lose his nerve. The catechism does not leave the source in doubt:

Justification is an act of God's free grace, in which he pardons all our sins, and accepts us as righteous in his sight, only for the righteousness of Christ imputed to us, and received by faith alone.

The Baptist Catechism, Question 36

Only for the righteousness of Christ imputed to us. Which settles the question a persecuted man asks himself at two in the morning: am I even good enough for this to count? The righteousness he is being hated for was credited to him before he did anything about it. The life it produced is real and it is what drew the fire, but it is not the basis of his standing and it never was. He does not have to be sure his motives were pure in order to be sure the verdict holds.

Next, the kingdom. The catechism’s handling of the second petition is the persecuted man’s prayer, whether or not he has noticed:

In the second petition, which is, Your kingdom come, we pray that Satan's kingdom may be destroyed, and that the kingdom of grace may be advanced, ourselves and others brought into it and kept in it, and that the kingdom of glory may be hastened.

The Baptist Catechism, Question 109

Three kingdoms in one sentence, and the man in Matthew 5:10 is standing at the seam of all of them. One kingdom is coming apart. One is advancing, and he is being kept in it — note that the catechism thinks keeping is something to pray for, not something to assume. And one is being hastened, which is where the whole account finally settles. Persecution is what it costs to stand at that seam and not step back over the line.

Then the keeping itself, which is the confession’s answer to the fear underneath all of this — not will it hurt but will I hold:

Many storms and floods may rise and beat against them, but they shall never be torn from the foundation and rock to which faith has fastened them.

1689 LBCF, ch. 17 — Of the Perseverance of the Saints

Read the grammar of that. A man is not commended for his grip. He is fastened, and the thing he is fastened to is not moving. The storms are granted, in the plural, and so are the floods. What is denied is the tearing loose.

And finally the day the ledger is read out. The confession says God set that day for two reasons, and the second one is aimed straight at the man in this verse:

As Christ would have us to be certainly persuaded that there shall be a day of judgment, both to deter all men from sin and for the greater consolation of the godly in their adversity, so will he have the day unknown to men

1689 LBCF, ch. 32 — Of the Last Judgment

For the greater consolation of the godly in their adversity. The judgment is not only a terror to be preached at the wicked. It is comfort issued to men who are being wronged now and cannot get it fixed. Every unanswered slander, every rigged meeting, every case that never got heard goes on a docket that will be called. A man who believes that can afford to stop defending himself so hard.

The verse, in the confession’s own words

For righteousness’ sake — a righteousness he did not manufacture: only for the righteousness of Christ imputed to us, and received by faith alone.

Theirs is the kingdom of heaven — the kingdom he prays for while it costs him: that the kingdom of glory may be hastened; the kingdom he is kept in; the kingdom he cannot be torn from, with a day appointed for the greater consolation of the godly in their adversity.

The Baptist Catechism  ·  LBCF 17  ·  LBCF 32  ·  Institutes III.8

5. For the Man Reading This

Check the preposition before you claim the beatitude. This is the first work and most men skip it. Peter names the disqualifiers plainly — let none of you suffer as a murderer, or a thief, or an evil doer, or a meddler in other men’s matters. That last one is the common case. If people are hard on you because you are abrasive, because you cannot let a thing go, because you said the true thing in the cruellest available way, or because you inserted yourself where you had no business, that is not this verse. That is consequence, and calling it persecution is a way of never repenting of it. Do the honest inventory first. It is short and it stings.

Righteousness is a life, not a set of takes. The sermon defines its own terms right after this line: anger, contempt, lust, faithfulness in marriage, plain speech, refusing retaliation, loving enemies. That is the righteousness men get hated for. Being known online for correct positions costs almost nothing and is frequently mistaken for the real thing. Being the man in the shop who will not pad the invoice, will not join the pile-on, and will not laugh at the joke — that is what gets a man quietly moved off the good crew.

Do not go looking for it. Calvin’s warning is not a footnote. A man who wants the badge badly enough will manufacture a fight to get one, and the beatitude does not cover self-inflicted exile. Nobody in this verse volunteered. They were pursued. Your job is to live the life and let the hostility find you, not to go out and provoke enough of it to feel biblical.

Expect the ordinary version. Most American persecution is not a prison. It is a room that goes quiet when you walk in. It is the promotion that goes to the other guy with no reason given. It is the family group chat where you are now the difficult one. It is being described accurately by people who make it sound damning. Calvin listed disgrace and ignominy right alongside prison and death, and he noted that for some temperaments the disgrace is the harder of the two. Do not disqualify your own suffering because it is not dramatic. Do not inflate it either.

Read the tense and stop waiting. Theirs is. Not theirs will be. Whatever this has already cost you — the job, the friendship, the standing in a room you used to own — you are not owed a settlement at some future date. You already hold the deed. That changes what you are willing to risk next week, because a man who has already been paid is much harder to buy.

You are allowed to hate it. Calvin spent a whole section demolishing the idea that a faithful man should feel nothing under the cross. The Lord grieved and wept; that settles it. You do not have to perform serenity you do not have. Grieve the thing, say so out loud to someone, and keep standing anyway. That is what fortitude is — fully sensible of the bitterness, still withstanding.

Keep the light where it can be seen. The very next paragraph of the sermon is salt and light and a city on a hill. The instinct after being hit is to get smaller, quieter, harder to find. Jesus put the command to be visible immediately after the promise about being persecuted, and the sequence is the whole point. Dimming out is the one response the text forecloses.

Do not defend yourself past the point of usefulness. There is a day appointed, and one of its stated purposes is your consolation. The record will be read. That frees you to make your case once, plainly, and then let it go — instead of burning a year of your life trying to force a verdict out of people who were never going to give you one.

Find the other men taking it. The apostles walked out of a flogging together, and they went rejoicing. Persecution in Scripture is almost never borne alone, and isolation is where it does its real damage. If you are the only one you know paying anything for this, that is a problem to solve this month, not a badge of honor.

In the Sermon on the Mount memorize pack — carry it. Say it the next time you are working out how to answer a question at work where the true answer is going to cost you something.

And keep the shape of the thing straight. Jesus is not telling you persecution is good. He is telling you that when the world runs a righteous man off, it cannot run him out of the kingdom, because he was never holding it on the world’s terms. The list of beatitudes opens with a beggar and closes with a fugitive, and both walk away with the same estate. The world reads that as a consolation for losers. Jesus says it is the deed.
