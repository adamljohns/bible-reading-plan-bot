Verse Study · Galatians

Galatians 2:20

Paul says he died, and then keeps talking. The whole Christian life is packed into the strangeness of that sentence.

I have been crucified with Christ, and it is no longer I that live, but Christ lives in me. That life which I now live in the flesh, I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself up for me.— World English Bible (public domain)

I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me: and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself for me.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The grammar is doing something odd on purpose. I have been crucified — a past event, done to him. Nevertheless I live — so he is not gone. Yet not I, but Christ lives in me — so the “I” that lives is not the “I” that died. And the life I now live in the flesh — so we are talking about ordinary embodied Tuesday life, not a mystical state.

Four clauses, each one qualifying the last. Paul is circling something he cannot say plainly in one go: that a man can be genuinely dead and genuinely alive at once, and that the living one is not a restored version of the dead one.

  • Χριστός Christos · “with Christ” · Strong’s G5547

    The Anointed One — the Greek translation of Hebrew Mashiach (Messiah). In the OT, kings, priests, and prophets were anointed with oil as a sign of God's commissioning.

    Christos — the Anointed One, the Messiah. Paul uses the title, not just the name, and it matters for what “crucified with” means. He is not saying he shares a personal experience with a man he admires. He is saying he was included in what the Messiah did on behalf of a people. Union, not imitation.

  • ζάω zaō · “I live” · Strong’s G2198

    To live, to be alive, to have vitality. Zaō covers physical life, spiritual life, and eternal life.

    Zao — to live, to have vitality. Paul uses it three times in this one verse, and each time the subject shifts: I live, Christ lives, the life I now live. That repetition is the argument. He will not resolve the tension by dropping either term.

  • σάρξ sarx · “the flesh” · Strong’s G4561

    Flesh — the physical body, human nature in its weakness, or the sinful disposition opposed to the Spirit. Sarx has a wide range in the NT: neutral (physical body), positive (the Word became flesh), and negative (the works of the flesh).

    Sarx here is neutral — the physical body, embodied existence, not the sinful disposition it means elsewhere in Paul. That distinction saves the verse from a serious misreading. This is not a claim to have escaped the body into something purer. The new life is lived in the flesh, in the same body, with the same hands and the same commute.

  • πίστις Pistis · “the faith” · Strong’s G4102

    The Greek noun pistis is one of the most important words in the New Testament, occurring approximately 243 times. It means faith, trust, reliance, or firm conviction — particularly the trust placed in God and in Jesus Christ.

    Pistis — trust, reliance, firm conviction. Note the mechanism: I live by faith. Not by feeling Christ’s presence, not by achieving a state. The living gets done by trusting, which means it is available on days when nothing is felt at all.

  • παραδίδωμι Paradidōmi · “gave” · Strong’s G3860

    The Greek verb paradidōmi means to hand over, deliver up, entrust, or betray — to pass something from one person's hands to another's. The prefix para- (alongside/over) + didōmi (to give) creates the sense of a deliberate handoff.

    Paradidomi is to hand over, deliver up — and it is the word for betrayal, used of Judas handing Jesus over. Paul takes that verb and puts Christ Himself as the subject: He handed Himself over. Nobody took Him; He delivered Himself the way a man hands over a possession. That is a deliberate reclaiming of the ugliest verb in the passion narrative.

  • συσταυρόω systauroō · “I am crucified” · Strong’s G4957

    The Greek verb systauroō means to crucify together with — to nail a man to the same cross as another. It is syn (with) plus stauroō (to crucify).

    Systauroo is to be crucified together with — the compound doing the work Paul cannot say in one clause. He is not describing an experience he shares with a man he admires. He was included in what the Messiah did on behalf of a people. Union, not imitation.

The two words that carry the weight

Loved me… gave himself for me. Singular. Paul has spent two chapters on universals — justification, law, the gospel to the Gentiles — and lands the paragraph on the first-person singular.

That shift is not sentimentality; it is the point of application. A man can hold the doctrine of the atonement entirely and never once put himself inside it. Paul does, in the last five words, and it is the only place the verse becomes usable.

2. Where It Sits

This is not devotional writing. It is the last line of a rebuke delivered to another apostle in public.

Peter had come to Antioch and eaten with Gentile believers. Then men came from James, and Peter withdrew and separated himself, fearing the circumcision party — and the other Jews joined him, even Barnabas. Paul says he opposed Peter to his face, because he stood condemned.

Verses 15 through 21 are Paul’s argument in that confrontation: we are justified by faith in Christ and not by works of the law. Verse 20 is the climax of that argument, and verse 21 is the closing hammer — if righteousness comes by the law, then Christ died for nothing.

So the context is a table. The question is whether a man’s standing depends on his observance, and Paul’s answer is that the man whose standing depended on observance is dead. There is no one left to be scored.

That setting also explains the intensity of the first person. Paul is not composing a meditation. He is telling Peter what happened to Paul.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

This verse is the classic Reformed proof of union with Christ — the doctrine that a believer is not merely forgiven by Christ but joined to Him, so that what happened to Christ counts as having happened to the believer.

That doctrine does specific work. It explains how justification can be both entirely outside you and genuinely yours: not a legal fiction, because you were actually included. And it explains sanctification without turning it into self-improvement, since the life being lived is not a better version of the old one.

The tradition was also careful about what the verse does not say. It does not describe the erasure of the person. Paul is still Paul; he still writes letters, argues with Peter, has a temperament. Not I, but Christ is not annihilation of the self but the deposing of the self as the one in charge.

4. Confessional Anchor

By this faith a Christian believes to be true whatsoever is revealed in the Word, for the authority of God himself; and also apprehends an excellency therein above all other writings, and all things in the world.

1689 LBCF, ch. 14 — Of Saving Faith

The confession cites this verse under faith, which is a telling place to file it. Paul’s own account of how the new life operates is I live by faith in the Son of God — so the confession is reading the verse the way Paul wrote it, as a description of the mechanism rather than of an experience.

Apprehends an excellency therein above all other writings, and all things in the world is worth sitting with. Faith in this account is not a blind leap but a seeing — the recognition of a worth that is really there. Which is what makes the last clause of Paul’s verse possible. A man who has seen that does not need to be talked into saying He loved me and gave Himself for me.

5. For the Man Reading This

The practical value of this verse is that it takes a particular argument away from you.

There is a version of self-hatred that presents as humility — the running commentary about what a failure you are, how little you have changed, how unworthy. It feels like honesty and it is actually a claim: that the “I” in question is still the one that matters, still the one being scored. Paul’s answer is that the man being scored was executed. You are not permitted to keep prosecuting a corpse.

And there is the opposite error, which this verse also closes. Christ lives in me is not a licence to stop showing up, as though your ordinary effort were now beside the point. Paul says the life is lived in the flesh — same body, same job, same marriage. The new life does not float above Tuesday; it is what Tuesday is now made of.

So the day-to-day form is this. When you fail, you do not restart the self-improvement project, because the project’s owner died. You go back to the mechanism Paul names: I live by faith. Trust, again, that the one who loved you and handed Himself over is still the one living in you — and then go do the next ordinary thing in the same flesh you woke up in.
