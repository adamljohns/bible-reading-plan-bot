Verse Study · James

James 1:21-22

Three orders in a row, and they come in a fixed sequence: strip, receive, do. Skip the third and James says you have not merely stalled — you have started lying to yourself, and you are the only one taken in.

Therefore, putting away all filthiness and overflowing of wickedness, receive with humility the implanted word, which is able to save your souls. But be doers of the word, and not only hearers, deluding your own selves.— World English Bible (public domain)

Wherefore lay apart all filthiness and superfluity of naughtiness, and receive with meekness the engrafted word, which is able to save your souls. But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Two verses, and every clause in them is load-bearing. Strip something off. Take something in. Then act on what you took in. James does not offer these as three good ideas a man may sample. He stacks them, and the order is not reversible. A man who has not put the filth down has no clear hands to receive with. A man who has received and not acted has, by James's own reckoning, received nothing he can prove.

The words below are given in their dictionary forms. The inflected Greek line is not part of this desk's text store, so it is not reproduced here; what is reproduced is the Strong's-tagged vocabulary behind the King James rendering, which is what the tagging in this repo actually supports.

Start with the hinge word the English almost hides. "Wherefore" translates dio (Strong's G1352), a conjunction that draws a conclusion out of what came before it. It is a slightly harder link than the more common oun: not "and so," but "for that reason, therefore." Verse 21 is not a new subject. It is the payment due on verse 18.

  • ἀποτίθημι apotithēmi · "lay apart" · Strong's G659

    To put away, lay aside, take off, set down. The picture is a garment: something you have been wearing comes off and goes on the floor. It is the same verb Paul uses for putting off the old man in Ephesians 4:22, and the one behind Peter's "laying aside all malice" in 1 Peter 2:1 — where Peter pairs it with kakia, the same malice James names a few words later. Note what it is not. It is not fighting the filth, or managing it, or budgeting for it. It is taking it off, the way a man strips out of clothes he has been working in all day. Decisive, and done from the outside in.

  • ῥυπαρία rhyparia · "filthiness" · Strong's G4507

    Filth, squalor, moral grime — from rhyparos, dirty or wretched. It appears once in the New Testament, here. This is not the language of legal guilt but of accumulated dirt: what settles on a man who has been living carelessly, the way grease settles on a kitchen wall nobody wipes. The word pairs with the garment verb on purpose. Grime is what you take off. It is not part of you.

  • περισσεία perisseia · "superfluity" · Strong's G4050 and κακία kakia · "naughtiness" · Strong's G2549

    Perisseia is abundance, surplus, what runs over the top — Paul's word for grace abounding beyond sin in Romans 5. James turns it around and sets it in front of kakia: malice, ill-will, the settled disposition of a heart bent toward harm. Not abstract badness — the word usually names the malice that damages other people, which is why Peter clusters it with guile, hypocrisies, envies, and evil speakings in 1 Peter 2:1, under the same "lay aside" verb James uses here. So the phrase is not "put away a bit of naughtiness." It is: there is more malice in you than you have accounted for, and it is running over the sides.

  • δέχομαι dechomai · "receive" · Strong's G1209

    To receive, accept, welcome. Greek has a harder verb for taking — lambanō can mean to seize — and James does not use it. Dechomai is the word for taking in a guest: deliberate, willing reception of what is offered. It is the verb Luke uses of the Bereans, who received the word with all readiness of mind and then went and checked it. This is the first thing the verse asks a man to do rather than stop doing, and it is not passive. A guest at the door is not received by anyone who merely notices him.

  • πραΰτης prautēs · "meekness" · Strong's G4240

    The English word has gone soft; the Greek has not. Prautēs is strength under control — classical Greek used it of a broken horse: full power, fully answering the rein. Moses is called the meekest man on earth in Numbers 12:3, and Moses was not mild. Meekness here is the posture of a man with force enough to argue with the text who does not. He takes the correction with his hands down. An angry man cannot be taught, which is the point James made two verses earlier.

  • ἔμφυτος emphytos · "engrafted" · Strong's G1721

    Innate, implanted, engrafted. The image is agricultural, and the two great English renderings pull on different parts of it: "implanted" hears a seed put down in soil, the King James "engrafted" hears a living branch bound into a living stock until the two grow as one. Either way the word is not describing information delivered to a man. It is describing something set inside him that is alive there and expected to grow. Which is why the next verse can measure a man by his conduct. You do not test a graft by asking whether the tree remembers it.

  • σῴζω sōzō · "save" · Strong's G4982 · ψυχή psychē · "souls" · Strong's G5590

    Sōzō runs from rescue out of danger, through physical healing, to full salvation from sin; psychē is life, the self, the inner man, the part of you that outlives the body. Put together, the claim is enormous and it is made about the word, not about the reader's effort: the implanted word is able — dunatai, it has the power — to save your souls. James has just said in verse 18 that God gave us birth by the word of truth. The word that started the life is the word that finishes it.

  • ποιητής poiētēs · "doers" · Strong's G4163

    A maker, from poieō, to do or make. In ordinary Greek it can mean a poet, and Paul uses it that way once, quoting the Greek poets on Mars Hill; in the tagged King James it is "doer" five times and "poets" once. James wants the noun, not the verb, and the difference matters. He does not say do the word occasionally. He says be a doer of it. That is a job description, not an activity.

  • ἀκροατής akroatēs · "hearers" · Strong's G202

    A hearer — and in the Greek world, a respectable thing to be. An akroatēs was an auditor at a lecture: you attended, you followed the argument, you may have enjoyed it, and nobody expected the lecture to alter your household. That is the force of the word in James's hands. He is not attacking men who sleep through the sermon. He is attacking men who follow it closely, agree with it, and go home unchanged.

  • παραλογίζομαι paralogizomai · "deceiving your own selves" · Strong's G3884

    The sharpest word in the two verses. From para (beside) and logizomai (to reckon, to enter in an account): to misreckon, to reason beside the mark. It is a bookkeeping word. The tagged King James uses it twice — Colossians 2:4, where somebody else beguiles you with enticing words, and here, where the verb turns inward. Same cheat, but now the man is both the salesman and the mark. This is not an honest mistake. It is a cooked ledger, and the point of a cooked ledger is that the man reading it feels perfectly solvent. Hearing without doing does not feel like disobedience. That is why James has to name it.

2. Where It Sits

James 1:19-20 — So, then, my beloved brothers, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger; for the anger of man doesn't produce the righteousness of God.

James 1:23-24 — For if anyone is a hearer of the word and not a doer, he is like a man looking at his natural face in a mirror; for he sees himself, and goes away, and immediately forgets what kind of man he was.

James 1 opens in the middle of trouble — trials, the asking for wisdom, the double-minded man who is unstable in all his ways (1:8, dipsuchos, Strong's G1374, literally two-souled). Then it climbs to verse 18: of his own will God gave us birth by the word of truth. Everything after that is response, not initiative. God planted; now receive what was planted. The seed was not the reader's idea.

Immediately before our verses stands the instruction most men can quote and few obey: swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger. Verse 20 gives the reason — the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God. Then dio: therefore. The meekness of verse 21 is not a new virtue dropped in from nowhere; it is what "slow to anger" looks like when a man sits down under the word. He has already been told his temper produces nothing God wants. Now he is told what to do with the hands his temper was occupying.

Immediately after comes the mirror. A hearer-only studies his own face, walks off, and forgets what he saw. The joke in it is precise: he did look. He got real information about himself and it evaporated at the door. Verse 25 completes the figure — the man who looks into the perfect law of liberty and continues in it, not a forgetful hearer but a doer of the work (Strong's G2041), is blessed in his doing. James calls the law of God a law of liberty (Strong's G1657) in the same breath in which he commands obedience to it. He sees no tension there at all.

Widen the frame and a spine shows through the chapter: three different Greek words for deception, all aimed at the same man. Verse 16, planaō (Strong's G4105) — do not be led astray. Verse 22, paralogizomai — do not cook your own books. Verse 26, apataō (Strong's G538) — the man who thinks himself religious and does not bridle his tongue deceives his own heart, and his religion is worthless. Three verbs, one target, eleven verses. James is not worried his readers will be argued out of the faith by outsiders. He is worried they will talk themselves into a version of it that costs nothing. Chapter 2 then says it outright: faith without works is dead. Verse 22 is where that argument is born.

  • Luke 8:15

  • Jeremiah 31:33

    "But this is the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel after those days," says the LORD: I will put my law in their inward parts, and in their heart will I write it; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people:

  • Romans 2:13

    For it isn't the hearers of the law who are righteous before God, but the doers of the law will be justified

  • Luke 6:46

    "Why do you call me, 'Lord, Lord,' and don't do the things which I say?

  • Ezekiel 33:32

    Behold, you are to them as a very lovely song of one who has a pleasant voice, and can play well on an instrument; for they hear your words, but they don't do them.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin — the word is a seed, and a seed does its own work

Everything in verse 21 depends on what kind of thing the word is. If it is data, receiving it means filing it. Calvin, arguing that true faith cannot be finally extinguished in a believer, reaches for the image James uses:

In this way, it appears that the word, which is an incorruptible seed, produces fruit similar to itself. Its germ never withers away utterly and perishes.

Calvin, Institutes III.2 — Of Faith

Fruit similar to itself. That is the logic of the engrafted word in one phrase — a graft does not produce what the host tree produced before; it produces after its own kind. So James moves from "receive the implanted word" to "be doers of the word" in a single breath without changing subjects. The doing is not an add-on the reader supplies. It is what the seed does when it is genuinely in the ground.

Calvin — the man who is fooled is the one doing the fooling

Verse 22 says the hearer-only deceives himself, and the natural question is how. Nobody sets out to run a con on his own soul. Calvin, describing men who believe for a while and then fall away, answers with unsettling precision:

Such, we doubt not, eagerly receive the word with a kind of relish, and have some feeling of its divine power, so as not only to impose upon men by a false semblance of faith, but even to impose upon themselves. They imagine that the reverence which they give to the word is genuine piety, because they have no idea of any impiety but that which consists in open and avowed contempt.

Calvin, Institutes III.2 — Of Faith

The last clause is the diagnosis. The man's whole definition of ungodliness is open contempt — the scoffer, the man who walks out. Measured against that, his own warm regard for the word looks like the opposite of impiety, and the books balance. He never has to lie about a fact. He only has to keep the wrong ledger. Calvin knows why the fraud goes undetected for years:

The human heart has so many recesses for vanity, so many lurking places for falsehood, is so shrouded by fraud and hypocrisy, that it often deceives itself.

Calvin, Institutes III.2 — Of Faith

Which is why James does not ask a man to examine his sincerity. Sincerity is precisely the thing the recesses can counterfeit. He asks about conduct, because conduct cannot be sincere and absent at the same time.

Calvin — doctrine is not an affair of the tongue

Writing on the life of the Christian man, Calvin takes up those who can talk about the gospel fluently and cannot be identified by it:

Doctrine is not an affair of the tongue, but of the life; is not apprehended by the intellect and memory merely, like other branches of learning; but is received only when it possesses the whole soul, and finds its seat and habitation in the inmost recesses of the heart. … it must be transfused into the breast, and pass into the conduct, and so transform us into itself, as not to prove unfruitful.

Calvin, Institutes III.6 — The Life of a Christian Man

"Is received only when." Calvin uses receive the way James uses dechomai, and defines it by where it ends up rather than by how it felt going in. Doctrine that stops at the intellect and memory has not been received badly or partially. It has not been received. That is a harder sentence than most sermons on this verse are willing to say.

Calvin — and this does not make works the ground

One hazard needs naming before the application: read carelessly, "be doers" becomes "earn it." Calvin handles James at length in his treatment of justification and refuses both errors in one stroke:

The error of our opponents lies chiefly in this, that they think James is defining the mode of justification, whereas his only object is to destroy the depraved security of those who vainly pretended faith as an excuse for their contempt of good works. Therefore, let them twist the words of James as they may, they will never extract out of them more than the two propositions: That an empty phantom of faith does not justify, and that the believer, not contented with such an imagination, manifests his justification by good works.

Calvin, Institutes III.17 — Of Justification and Works

Depraved security. That is the disease James is treating, and be exact about who catches it. Not the man who doubts — the man who is at ease, settled, certain of a faith that has never once cost him a decision. James is not raising the price of salvation. He is taking a counterfeit receipt out of a man's hand.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession takes verse 21's claim about the word literally, and puts it where it belongs — under the doctrine of faith rather than the doctrine of effort.

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 14, Of Saving Faith

The grace of faith, whereby the elect are enabled to believe to the saving of their souls, is the work of the Spirit of Christ in their hearts; and is ordinarily wrought by the ministry of the Word; by which also, and by the administration of baptism and the Lord's Supper, prayer, and other means appointed of God, it is increased and strengthened.

1689 LBCF, ch. 14, para. 1
Read Chapter 14, Of Saving Faith →

"To the saving of their souls." The confession is standing on this verse, in this vocabulary. And it settles the question James's imperative raises: if the word is able to save, who is doing the saving? The Spirit of Christ, working ordinarily through the ministry of the Word. The graft is not self-set. But look at what the same chapter says the resulting faith actually does with the text it believes:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 14, para. 2

yielding obedience to the commands, trembling at the threats, and embracing the promises of God for this life, and that which is to come

1689 LBCF, ch. 14, para. 2
Read Chapter 14, Of Saving Faith →

That is a description of faith, not of works added to faith. Faith is being defined here partly by the fact that it obeys commands. A "faith" that embraces the promises and skips the commands is not a weak instance of this thing; it is a different thing. Which is James's point, restated by English Baptists who knew what a public confession was going to cost them.

The confession is equally careful on the other side. Chapter 16 says that good works, done in obedience to God's commandments, are the fruits and evidences of a true and lively faith — fruits and evidences, not roots and causes. Same chapter, a few lines on: when we have done all we can, we have done but our duty, and are unprofitable servants. Nobody is buying anything here.

The catechism then supplies the mechanics — how the word gets from the air into a life.

The Baptist Catechism · Q94 — How is the Word made effectual to salvation?

The Spirit of God makes the reading, but especially the preaching, of the Word an effectual means of convincing and converting sinners, and of building them up in holiness and comfort through faith to salvation.

The Baptist Catechism, Q94
Q95 — How is the Word to be read and heard, that it may become effectual to salvation?

That the Word may become effectual to salvation, we must attend to it with diligence, preparation, and prayer; receive it with faith and love, lay it up in our hearts, and practice it in our lives.

The Baptist Catechism, Q95
Work through the Catechism →

Read Q95 slowly and you are reading James 1:21-22 in the catechism's own vocabulary, in order. Attend with diligence, preparation, and prayer — that is the meekness, the posture of a man who came ready to be corrected. Receive it with faith and love — that is dechomai. Lay it up in our hearts — that is the implanted word. And practice it in our lives — that is verse 22, and it is the last clause for a reason. Everything ahead of it is preparation for it. The catechism's own proof-text list for that answer ends at James 1:25.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is week four of the PROVEN Men memorize pack — twelve weeks, one verse or short range a week. Chamber it before you need it. Week seven of the same pack is James 1:19-20, the two verses immediately before this one. That is not an accident of the list. A man who has not learned to be slow to anger will not receive anything with meekness, and a man who receives nothing has nothing to do.

Take something off before you try to put something on. The order in verse 21 is not decorative. Apotithēmi comes first — the garment verb. Most men attempt this in reverse: add a reading plan on top of an unchanged week and wonder why nothing takes. Name one thing this week that is grime rather than sin you would defend out loud — a feed, a habit of speech, a running resentment you have decided is justified. Take it off. You are not fighting it. You are setting it down.

Then check the overflow. James does not say "some malice." He says the surplus of it, the part running over the sides. Ask the man you live with, or the woman who married you, where your ill-will spills. You will not find it by introspection; the recesses Calvin described are good at their job. It shows up in tone, in what you are quick to assume about people, in who you enjoy hearing bad news about.

Measure by conduct, not by feeling. This is the specific and costly force of the self-deceit. You cannot audit yourself by asking whether the sermon moved you, because being moved is exactly what the hearer-only has plenty of. Ask instead: what did I do differently after the last thing that convicted me? If the honest answer is nothing, the ledger is cooked, and the fact that you feel fine about it is the symptom rather than the defense.

Give the word one command a week and obey it before Sunday. Not a study goal — an assignment. One sentence of Scripture you know applies to you, executed while it is still uncomfortable. Apologize to the son you were sharp with. Make the call you have been avoiding. Pay the thing back. It is small on purpose: a doer is a job description, and men grow into job descriptions by doing the small parts of them on Tuesday, not by resolving to do the large parts someday.

Do not let the mirror close the loop. The man in verse 23 got accurate information about himself. That is what makes him a warning instead of a fool. Everything worked except the door. So when something in the text lands on you, write it down before you stand up. The forgetting is not a memory problem. It is a design feature of a heart that would rather not.

And when this cuts, hold onto where the sentence puts the power. It is the word that is able to save your souls, not your compliance with it. God gave you birth by the word of truth before you had done a thing about it, and the graft he set in you produces fruit similar to itself. You are not doing the word in order to be saved. You are doing it because something living was put in you, and living things grow whether or not the man carrying them feels particularly holy on a given morning. Obey it anyway. That is what a doer is.
