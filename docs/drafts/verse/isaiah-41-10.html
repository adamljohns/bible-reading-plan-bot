Verse Study · Isaiah

Isaiah 41:10

Two commands and five promises. Notice which of them God asks you to supply.

Don’t you be afraid, for I am with you. Don’t be dismayed, for I am your God. I will strengthen you. Yes, I will help you. Yes, I will uphold you with the right hand of my righteousness.— World English Bible (public domain)

Fear thou not; for I am with thee: be not dismayed; for I am thy God: I will strengthen thee; yea, I will help thee; yea, I will uphold thee with the right hand of my righteousness.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Count the verbs and the whole verse reorganizes itself. Fear not. Be not dismayed. Those are the two things asked of you, and they are both negatives — stop doing something. Then: I am with you. I am your God. I will strengthen. I will help. I will uphold. Five affirmatives, every one of them His.

That ratio is the argument. God does not tell a frightened man to generate courage. He tells him to stop being afraid, and then supplies five reasons that have nothing to do with the man’s resources. The imperatives are only obeyable because of what follows them.

  • יָרֵא Yare · “Fear” · Strong’s H3372

    The Hebrew verb yare means to fear, be afraid, revere, or stand in awe. It occurs over 300 times in the Old Testament and carries a dual meaning: natural fear or dread in the face of danger, and reverential awe before God's holiness and majesty.

    Yare covers both natural dread in the face of danger and reverent awe before God. The same verb runs in both directions, which is part of Scripture’s answer to fear: the man who fears God rightly has less room left over for fearing anything else. Here it is plain dread, and the command is not to feel nothing but to stop letting the dread govern.

  • שָׁעָה Shaah · “dismayed” · Strong’s H8159

    When Abel's offering was accepted, God "had regard (shaah) for Abel and his offering" (Genesis 4:4). When Cain's was not: "for Cain and his offering he had no regard (shaah)." This divine gaze — given or withheld — is not arbitrary but reflects the condition of the heart behind the offering.

    The verb behind be not dismayed is shaah, and its ordinary sense is to gaze or look intently. It is the word for God’s regard when He looked with favour on Abel’s offering. In this form it carries the sense of looking around anxiously — the head swiveling, scanning for the threat. That is a precise picture of what fear actually does to a man. It is not only an emotion; it is a posture, eyes darting. And the antidote in the verse is a fixed point: I am your God.

  • אָמַץ Amats · “I will strengthen” · Strong’s H553

    The Hebrew verb amats means to be strong, to prevail, to make firm, or to be courageous. It is used both of physical strength and moral-spiritual courage, frequently in divine commissioning speeches where God commands His servants to take courage in the face of overwhelming challenge.

    Amats is to be strong, to prevail, to make firm. It is the word used in divine commissioning speeches — what God says to Joshua before the Jordan. Note the person of the verb: I will strengthen you. Not “be strong,” which is what we usually hear. The strengthening is something done to you.

  • עָזַר azar · “thee; yea, I will help” · Strong’s H5826

    Azar (עָזַר) means to help, to give aid, to come to one's assistance. It is the root of several significant names: Ezra (עֶזְרָא — 'help'), Eleazar (אֶלְעָזָר — 'God has helped'), and Lazarus (the Greek form of Eleazar).

    Azar is to help, to come to someone’s aid. It is the root behind names Israel gave its children — Ezra, “help”; Eleazar, “God has helped.” Families put this word in their sons' names because the help had already come and they wanted it on the record permanently.

  • תָּמַךְ Tamak · “thee; yea, I will uphold” · Strong’s H8551

    The Hebrew tamak describes the action of holding something or someone up — sustaining, supporting, or maintaining. It is used of God upholding His servants (Psalm 41:12; 63:8), of wisdom being held fast (Proverbs 3:18; 4:6), and of hands that grip and sustain.

    Tamak is to hold up, to sustain, to grasp and keep hold of. It is used of God upholding His servants and of a man holding fast to wisdom. There is a grip in it. This is not a hand hovering nearby in case you fall; it is a hand already closed around you.

  • יָמִין Yamin · “thee with the right hand” · Strong’s H3225

    The Hebrew noun yamin means right hand, right side, or south (since when facing east, south is on the right). It occurs approximately 139 times in the Old Testament.

    The yamin, the right hand, is the hand of power and of skill — the working hand, the sword hand. To be upheld by God’s right hand is not to be steadied by a spare hand He had free. It is to be held by the hand He does everything else with.

The strange phrase at the end

The right hand of my righteousness. Not the right hand of My power, which is what the sentence seems to want. Tsedek is righteousness, justice, what is right and vindicated. So the hand holding you is specifically God’s just hand — which matters, because a frightened man’s deepest worry is usually not whether God is strong but whether He is obliged. Power alone could let you go and lose nothing. Righteousness is a commitment. The grip is a matter of God keeping His own word.

2. Where It Sits

Isaiah 41 is a courtroom. God summons the nations to trial — let them bring their case, let them produce their idols and see whether those can declare what is coming. Verse 7 has the carpenter encouraging the goldsmith, one craftsman telling another the idol is coming along nicely, and then fastening it with nails so it will not topple over.

Then verse 8: But you, Israel, my servant, Jacob whom I have chosen, the offspring of Abraham my friend. Everything shifts. Against the manufactured god that has to be nailed down comes the God who chose a people and carries them.

Our verse lands there. It is spoken to a people facing empires, and the contrast the chapter has just drawn is the whole point: one god has to be held up by its makers, the other holds up His.

Read it that way and the promises stop being general encouragement. They are the specific difference between a god you must maintain and a God who maintains you.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed reading has always tied verses like this to providence rather than to temperament. Fear is not conquered by feeling braver; it is undercut by a doctrine — that nothing reaching you has escaped God’s government, and that His commitment to His people is not contingent on their performance under pressure.

The tradition also refused to read these promises as a guarantee of comfortable outcomes. Isaiah’s own audience went into exile. I am with you was true through the deportation, not instead of it. Any use of this verse that promises the circumstance will resolve is promising something Isaiah’s first hearers did not receive.

4. Confessional Anchor

God the good Creator of all things, in his infinite power and wisdom, does uphold, direct, dispose, and govern all creatures and things, from the greatest even to the least, by his most wise and holy providence.

1689 LBCF, ch. 5 — Of Divine Providence

The confession’s first verb is Isaiah’s last one. Uphold. What the prophet promises to one frightened people, the confession states as the standing condition of everything that exists.

Many storms and floods may rise and beat against them, but they shall never be torn from the foundation and rock to which faith has fastened them.

1689 LBCF, ch. 17 — Of the Perseverance of the Saints

And this is the honest version of the promise. Not that the storms are cancelled — the confession grants the storms in the same sentence — but that the grip holds. Which is exactly the shape of Isaiah 41:10: the danger is real enough to warrant the command fear not, and the hand is stronger than the danger.

5. For the Man Reading This

Take the two commands as commands about attention, not about feeling. Shaah is the anxious scanning, the head turning, and you can actually obey a command about where you are looking in a way you cannot obey a command about how you feel. Fear tells you to keep sweeping the horizon for the next threat. The verse gives you one fixed thing to look at instead.

Then notice you are not asked to produce anything. Read the promises again and find the one that requires something from you. There isn’t one. Every verb in the back half has God as its subject. Men in trouble exhaust themselves trying to manufacture the strength the verse says will be supplied — and the exhaustion is itself a form of unbelief, because it assumes the supply line is your responsibility.

And carry the last phrase into the worst nights. When it does not feel like He is holding you, the grip is not a feeling; it is a matter of His righteousness, and that is not fluctuating tonight. The hand is closed. You are not the one holding on.
