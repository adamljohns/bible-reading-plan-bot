Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:16

The first command in the light passage, and the first time in Matthew's Gospel that Jesus calls God your Father. Everything in the sentence is arranged so that the last word is not about you.

Even so, let your light shine before men; that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father who is in heaven.— World English Bible (public domain)

Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Three sentences, three moods. Verse 14 is a statement of fact: ye are the light of the world. Verse 15 is an observation about how people behave: neither do men light a candle, and put it under a bushel. Verse 16 is the first thing in the paragraph a man is actually told to do. The Lord has spent two verses establishing what you are and what a lamp is for, and only now does He give the order. That sequence is not decoration. He will not command the shining until He has told you the light is already yours.

And when the order comes, it is smaller than most sermons make it. He does not say be brighter. He does not say go and shine. He says let — get out of the way of a thing that is already burning. The light is not manufactured on the spot by an act of will; it is released by the removal of whatever was covering it, which is exactly what the previous verse was about.

Then comes the clause that governs the whole verse, and it is worth reading slowly, because it has two steps in it and men routinely stop after the first. That they may see your good works — that is step one, and it is not the goal. And glorify your Father which is in heaven — that is the goal, and it is not something you do. Being seen is a means. The Father's honor is the end. A man who obeys the first half and never reaches the second has not half-obeyed this verse; he has obeyed Matthew 6:2 by mistake.

Notice, too, what men are told to look at. Not your light — nobody looks at light, you look by it. Not you. Your good works. The light is invisible in itself and known only by what it makes visible, which is the most useful thing this verse says about the Christian life. You are not the exhibit. The lamp is not on the stand to be admired; it is on the stand so the room can see something else.

And track the little word your. It occurs three times: your light, your good works, your Father. The first two are handed to you and the third takes them out of your hands. It is your light — but you did not light it. They are your works — but the glory for them is booked to somebody else's account. By the end of the sentence the possessive has changed direction entirely: the last thing called yours is not a possession at all, it is a relation, and it is the only one of the three you could never have arranged.

  • οὕτως houtōs · "so" / "even so" · Strong's G3779

    The Greek adverb houtos means 'thus,' 'so,' 'in this way,' or 'in the same manner.' It connects the manner of divine action to its proclamation.

    Manner, not degree — and the difference is the whole opening of the verse. Modern ears hear the KJV's let your light so shine as an intensifier, as though the Lord were asking for more wattage. He is not. The word points backwards at the picture He has just drawn. Shine like that: like a small clay lamp put where the household can see by it. The newer renderings make the backward reach explicit — even so, in the same way — and the translations differ mainly on where the little word attaches, not on what it does. Whatever else obedience to this verse involves, it looks like a lamp on a stand and not like a bonfire.

  • λάμπω lampō · "shine" · Strong's G2989

    The Greek verb lampō means to shine — to beam, to give light, to radiate brilliance, whether of a lamp, a face, or a light God Himself commands. It is a primary verb. KJV renders it shine, shined, did shine, and giveth light. The noun kin is phōs (G5457), light itself.

    The same verb stood in verse 15, where it was a plain description of what a properly placed lamp does — it giveth light unto all that are in the house. Here it becomes the command. That is the whole pastoral logic of the paragraph: first you are told what the shining is, so that when you are told to do it you already know it is not an exotic requirement but the ordinary behaviour of what you have been made. The verb also runs elsewhere in ways worth knowing. It is used of Christ's face at the transfiguration, and of the God who commanded light to shine out of darkness and then shone in our hearts. Both of those are prior to this command. The order in Scripture is never shine, and then God will look on you kindly. It is: God shone, therefore let it shine.

  • φῶς phōs · "light" · Strong's G5457

    Light — physical illumination and its profound spiritual symbolism. Phōs represents truth, goodness, divine presence, and revelation. It stands opposite skotos (darkness), which represents sin, ignorance, and evil.

    It is the same noun Jesus takes for Himself two chapters later in John — I am the light of the world — and the same one He hands to the disciples in verse 14. That doubling is not a slip and it is not flattery. Nothing here is generated by the disciple. He is lit, placed, and then told to stop obstructing. Which is why the possessive in your light is entirely safe: a man can own a lamp without having invented fire.

  • ἔμπροσθεν emprosthen · "before" · Strong's G1715

    The Greek preposition and adverb emprosthen means 'before' or 'in front of' — indicating spatial position ahead of or in the presence of someone. It can indicate physical position (standing before someone), temporal precedence (before in time), or priority/supremacy.

    In front of, out in the open, where you can be looked at. This is the word Christ uses later in the same Gospel for confessing Him before men and being confessed before the Father — the two audiences named with the same preposition, one now and one later. It is also, word for word, the preposition standing in Matthew 6:1: do not your alms before men, to be seen of them. Same sermon, same word, opposite verdict. So the difference between the two commands cannot possibly be how public you are. It is somewhere else entirely, and the rest of the verse says where.

  • ἄνθρωπος anthrōpos · "men" · Strong's G444

    A human being, mankind generically — not specifically male (that is anēr). Anthrōpos is the general term for humanity.

    Not the brethren. Not the congregation. People at large — the ordinary run of humanity, most of whom in that crowd had no interest in the speaker's movement and some of whom would later help kill Him. The audience for your works is not the church. Verse 15 said the light reaches all that are in the house; verse 16 opens the door and puts the neighbours in view. If the only people who have ever seen your obedience are people who already agree with you, this verse has not yet had its way with you.

  • καλός kalos · "good" · Strong's G2570

    The Greek adjective for intrinsic goodness — something that is genuinely excellent, beautiful in character, or noble in quality. Unlike agathos (G18, good in a moral/beneficent sense), kalos carries more aesthetic and evaluative force: it is the good that is also lovely, the excellence that commends itself to perception.

    This is the more interesting of the two Greek words for good, and it is the one Christ picked. Kalos is goodness that shows — the kind a bystander can recognise without a theological briefing. It is the word behind the good shepherd and the good fight. Which sets a real standard on the works in question: they have to be the sort of thing that reads as good to a man who does not share your convictions. Not showy. Legible. A quiet integrity that an unbeliever can see the shape of, and cannot easily explain away.

  • ἔργον ergon · "works" · Strong's G2041

    Denotes any work, deed, action, or undertaking — from manual labor to moral conduct to divine activity. It covers God's works (creation, miracles, redemption), human works (moral deeds, works of the law), and Christ's works (signs that reveal His identity).

    Deeds. Things done, with hands and hours, not dispositions felt. The word is the same one at the centre of the New Testament's long argument about faith and works, and it is worth being plain: nothing in this verse suggests these works purchase anything. Paul says we are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared before that we would walk in them — prepared beforehand, which means the works are downstream of the making, not upstream of it. Christ is not here recruiting men to earn a Father. He is telling men who have one what to do with their hands.

  • εἴδω eidō · "they may see" · Strong's G1492

    A primary verb used in two tenses with distinct meanings: the aorist eidon means 'I saw' (physical or visionary sight), and the perfect oida means 'I know' (knowledge gained through observation). This dual meaning reveals the Greek connection between seeing and knowing — what you have seen, you know.

    The verb of eyes, and — in Greek as in life — of the knowledge eyes produce. What men do with your works is first of all just look at them. That is a low bar and a merciful one. You are not asked to explain yourself into their good opinion or to win an argument. You are asked to do things in the open that can be seen. The seeing is theirs; the doing is yours. And the ancient link between seeing and knowing is the hinge the verse turns on, because what they come to know from the looking is not primarily about you.

  • δοξάζω doxazō · "glorify" · Strong's G1392

    Doxazo (δοξάζω) is the verb form of doxa (glory/honor) — to ascribe glory, to honor, to magnify, to cause someone's greatness to be recognized. It captures active participation in the display of God's glory: we don't merely observe glory, we proclaim, reflect, and amplify it.

    Here is the point at which the sentence leaves your hands. The subject of this verb is they, not you. You cannot glorify God on another man's behalf; you can only put in front of him something that makes God worth glorifying, and then the verb belongs to him. Matthew twice records the thing actually happening — crowds who saw what Christ did and glorified God — and both times it is a response, unrehearsed, to something they watched. Which sets a limit on your responsibility that is a genuine relief. You are answerable for the works. You are not answerable for the verdict.

  • πατήρ patēr · "Father" · Strong's G3962

    Patēr appears 413 times in the NT and carries the full range of paternal meaning. Most profoundly, it is Jesus's primary designation for God — and the designation with which he taught his disciples to address God in prayer.

    This is the first time in Matthew's Gospel that God is called Father. Go back and check: the word has appeared before, but only of Herod's father, of Abraham, of Zebedee mending nets in a boat. Four chapters and fifteen verses of genealogy, birth, exile, baptism, wilderness, calling, healing and beatitudes, and God is King, Lord, God — never Father. Then, in a sentence about ordinary people watching ordinary obedience, the name arrives, and once it arrives it does not leave: seventeen times in the three chapters of this sermon. The Sermon on the Mount is the place where the disciples are taught the name, and this verse is the door they come in by.

Put the sentence back together and it runs downhill in one direction. God lights the lamp. You are the lamp, and your one job is placement. Men see, because that is what men do with what is in front of them. And what they end up doing is not admiring the lamp but honouring the One who lit it — by a name, moreover, that they have just heard used for the first time in this Gospel, in your hearing, about your God.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 5:14 — You are the light of the world. A city located on a hill can’t be hidden.

Matthew 5:15 — Neither do you light a lamp, and put it under a measuring basket, but on a stand; and it shines to all who are in the house.

Matthew 5:17 — “Don’t think that I came to destroy the law or the prophets. I didn’t come to destroy, but to fulfill.

The paragraph is four verses long and this is its last, which means the purpose clause here reaches back over all of it. Salt in verse 13, light and a city in verse 14, a lamp and a stand in verse 15 — and then verse 16 tells you what the whole cluster was for. Not influence. Not cultural leverage. Not even the good of the men who see, though that is real. Glorify your Father which is in heaven. Strip that clause off and the passage becomes a motivational text about Christian visibility, which is roughly what it has become in most hands.

Remember also what has just finished. The Beatitudes end on being reviled and persecuted and slandered for Christ's sake, and any sane man hearing that begins looking for cover. Salt, city, lamp, stand — the entire cluster is the Lord's answer to that instinct, and He closes every exit. You are already in the meat. You are already on the hill. The basket you were reaching for does not save the flame, it kills it. And now, finally, He tells you what all that exposure is for, which is the only thing that makes it bearable. A man will not stand in the open for the sake of being in the open. He will stand there if the standing puts honour on his Father's name.

Then verse 17 turns to the law, and everything after it makes the law heavier rather than lighter — anger set under murder, the eye set under adultery, love pushed out past the enemy line, and the whole run capped with unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, there is no way you will enter into the Kingdom of Heaven. That stretch is the content of the good works. Whatever letting your light shine has been made to mean in a hundred sermons, in Matthew it means the righteousness described in the rest of this sermon, done in front of people who are watching.

The counterweight sits one chapter later, from the same mouth in the same week, and it looks at first like a flat contradiction. Chapter five: let men see. Chapter six: be careful that you don't do your charitable giving before men, to be seen by them. The words are nearly identical. Even the preposition is the same. The difference is not the amount of visibility, and it is not the size of the audience — it is the object of the sentence and the destination of the credit. In 5:16 what men see is your good works, and what they do is glorify your Father. In 6:1 what men see is you, and what you get is applause, which the Lord coldly calls your reward, paid in full, receipt issued. Two failures bracket this verse, then: the basket of verse 15, which hides the works, and the trumpet of chapter six, which broadcasts the man. Both are failures of the same organ. In both the fellow is thinking about himself.

There is a thread here that repays following. Matthew 5:16 is the first your Father in the Gospel, and the phrase then recurs at intervals through the sermon, always at the point where motive is being decided: that you may be children of your Father who is in heaven; your Father in heaven is perfect; no reward from your Father who is in heaven; your Father who sees in secret; Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy. The last of these is the Lord's Prayer, and it is the same request as the purpose clause of this verse turned around and pointed upward. Verse 16 is what hallowing the name looks like on a Tuesday, with your hands.

Matthew also shows the thing happening. Twice the crowds watch something done and the narrative reports the result in exactly the vocabulary of this verse: they saw, and they glorified God. Neither time is anyone converted on the spot; neither time is there a sermon attached. People watched, and the honour went where it belonged. That is the whole ambition of Matthew 5:16, and it is smaller and more achievable than the ambitions usually preached from it.

The apostles took the verse and worked it in both directions. Peter's version is nearly a paraphrase and adds the hard part — that the glorifying may be delayed past your lifetime and may follow a stretch in which the same people call you a criminal. Paul's version runs the other way and names the inverse: the name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you. That is Matthew 5:16 with the sign flipped, and it is the honest warning attached to any doctrine of visible obedience. If men can be brought to honour God by watching a Christian, they can be brought to despise Him the same way. The lamp on the stand is in public either way.

Titus is the one most easily overlooked and the most useful to a working man. Paul is writing to slaves about not pilfering, and he says the point of their honesty is that they may adorn the doctrine of God — make the teaching look like what it is. That is Matthew 5:16 applied to a job nobody chose and nobody admired. The doctrine does not need decorating in the sense of improvement. It needs to be seen worn by somebody, in a place where it costs something, by a man with no platform at all.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin does not take up Matthew 5:16 in the Institutes. His verse-by-verse handling of it belongs to the commentary on the harmony of the Gospels, which is not in this library, and nothing here will pretend otherwise. But the four things this verse holds together — that the works are real, that they are evidence and not payment, that the honour goes to God, and that the God in question is a Father — are exactly the four things the Institutes works out at length. Take them in that order.

First, whether a Christian may look at his own obedience at all. Calvin has spent chapters insisting that nothing a man does can found his standing before God, and he knows the objection coming: then are works simply to be ignored? No. Once the foundation is laid elsewhere, works become legible as something other than merit:

For if when we call to mind the gifts which God has bestowed upon us, they are like rays of the divine countenance, by which we are enabled to behold the highest light of his goodness; much more is this the case with the gift of good works, which shows that we have received the Spirit of adoption.

— Calvin, Institutes III.14, §18 (Beveridge, 1845)

Read that with Matthew 5:16 open. Calvin calls good works a gift, which settles the ownership question the verse leaves implicit, and he says the thing they show is the Spirit of adoption — sonship. Which is precisely the word this verse ends on. The works do not earn the Father. They evidence Him. And Calvin is careful to say which direction the reasoning runs:

When believers therefore feel their faith strengthened by a consciousness of integrity, and entertain sentiments of exultation, it is just because the fruits of their calling convince them that the Lord has admitted them to a place among his children.

— Calvin, Institutes III.14, §19 (Beveridge, 1845)

The fruits convince them of the calling; they do not create it. A lamp does not become a lamp by shining. It shines because it is one, and the shining is how the household finds out.

Second, the ditch on the other side, which any verse containing the phrase before men runs along. Calvin is unsparing about the man who does good in order to be looked at, and he reaches for the same chapter of Matthew that sits one page after this verse:

So far, therefore, is God from being delighted with these hunters after popular applause with their swollen breasts, that he declares they have received their reward in this world

— Calvin, Institutes III.7, §2 (Beveridge, 1845)

Hunters after applause. Not men who were accidentally seen — men who went out looking for it, which is a different activity conducted with the same hands. And Calvin's remedy is not less visibility but a redirected aim, stated a few lines earlier in the same section:

Hence follows the other principle, that we are not to seek our own, but the Lord’s will, and act with a view to promote his glory.

— Calvin, Institutes III.7, §2 (Beveridge, 1845)

That is the purpose clause of Matthew 5:16 stated as a rule for the whole of life. The lamp is not asked to be dimmer. It is asked to burn toward a different end.

Third, the end itself. Calvin's fullest treatment of what it means for God to be glorified by men comes where you would expect it, in his exposition of the first petition of the Lord's Prayer — which is, as noted above, this verse's request turned upward:

The sum of the whole is, It must be our desire that God may receive the honour which is his due: that men may never think or speak of him without the greatest reverence.

— Calvin, Institutes III.20, §41 (Beveridge, 1845)

The honour which is his due. Not honour we generate on His behalf, and not honour He lacks — a debt already owed by everyone, which most of the world declines to pay. And Calvin then says something that puts a Christian's ordinary conduct into the middle of the transaction:

Then since God manifests himself to us partly by his word, and partly by his works, he is not sanctified unless in regard to both of these we ascribe to him what is due, and thus embrace whatever has proceeded from him, giving no less praise to his justice than to his mercy.

— Calvin, Institutes III.20, §41 (Beveridge, 1845)

God shows Himself by word and by works. Christ has just told a hillside crowd that a set of those works will be done by them, in public, and that the seeing will land as honour on the Father. Neither Calvin nor the verse allows the alternative every generation prefers: a Christianity that is talked about but never watched.

Fourth, the name at the end of the sentence, which is the part a man is most likely to read past. Calvin will not let anyone use the word Father casually, because he knows what it costs to be able to say it:

Who would have the presumption to arrogate to himself the honour of a son of God were we not gratuitously adopted as his sons in Christ?

— Calvin, Institutes III.20, §36 (Beveridge, 1845)

Gratuitously adopted — given, not achieved. Which means that the last clause of Matthew 5:16 is not a reward held out at the end of a life of good works but the standing you are working from on the first day. And Calvin adds the note that keeps the word from going soft, on the phrase that follows it in the Lord's Prayer:

In short, under the name of Father is set before us that God, who hath appeared to us in his own image, that we may invoke him with sure faith; the familiar name of Father being given not only to inspire confidence, but also to curb our minds, and prevent them from going astray after doubtful or fictitious gods.

— Calvin, Institutes III.20, §40 (Beveridge, 1845)

To inspire confidence and to curb. Both. Christ does not say glorify God, which men can do at a safe distance; He says your Father, which is warmer and also a great deal harder to walk away from. A man can be indifferent to a deity. Sons behave differently in public, because the name is on them.

4. Confessional Anchor

This verse is one of the ten proof-texts hung under the second paragraph of the confession's chapter on good works, and that paragraph is worth the whole section, because it lists what good works are for — six purposes in one sentence, of which Matthew 5:16 supplies the last.

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 16, Paragraph 2

"These good works, done in obedience to God's commandments, are the fruits and evidences of a true and lively faith; and by them believers manifest their thankfulness, strengthen their assurance, edify their brethren, adorn the profession of the gospel, stop the mouths of the adversaries, and glorify God; whose workmanship they are, created in Christ Jesus thereunto, that having their fruit unto holiness they may have the end eternal life."

Read Chapter 16, Of Good Works →

Count the list: thankfulness, assurance, the brethren, the profession, the adversaries, and God. It moves outward from the man's own heart to his church to the watching world and finally past all of them. Two items in the middle are this verse's territory exactly — adorn the profession of the gospel and stop the mouths of the adversaries. The first is Titus. The second is Peter, and it is bracing: not that opponents are argued into silence, but that a life gives them nothing to hold. And then the sentence turns, in its second half, and takes the credit back before anyone can pocket it: whose workmanship they are, created in Christ Jesus thereunto. Even the works listed as ours are called His workmanship in the same breath. The confession does in one clause what the verse does with the word your.

Two paragraphs later the confession forecloses the misuse, and it does so in language no honest man enjoys reading:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 16, Paragraph 5

"We cannot by our best works merit pardon of sin or eternal life at the hand of God, by reason of the great disproportion that is between them and the glory to come, and the infinite distance that is between us and God, whom by them we can neither profit nor satisfy for the debt of our former sins; but when we have done all we can, we have done but our duty, and are unprofitable servants; and because as they are good they proceed from his Spirit, and as they are wrought by us they are defiled and mixed with so much weakness and imperfection, that they cannot endure the severity of God's punishment."

Read Chapter 16, Of Good Works →

Defiled and mixed. That is the state of the best thing you did this year, and if the paragraph stopped there a man would have no reason to get out of bed. It does not stop there:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 16, Paragraph 6

"Yet notwithstanding the persons of believers being accepted through Christ, their good works also are accepted in him; not as though they were in this life wholly unblameable and unreprovable in God's sight, but that he, looking upon them in his Son, is pleased to accept and reward that which is sincere, although accompanied with many weaknesses and imperfections."

Read Chapter 16, Of Good Works →

The person is accepted first, and then the work is accepted in him. That order is not negotiable and it is the difference between this verse being a comfort and being a lash. God is not grading your light against a standard it will fail. He is looking upon them in his Son, and pleased to accept what is sincere while it is still mixed with everything you know is wrong with it. A man who has that straight can obey Matthew 5:16 without pretending, which is the only way it can be obeyed at all.

Then the catechism, on the request this verse exists to answer:

Baptist Catechism · Question 108

Q. What do we pray for in the first petition?
A. "In the first petition, which is, Hallowed be your name, we pray that God would enable us and others to glorify him in all that by which he makes himself known, and that he would dispose all things to his own glory."

Work through the Catechism →

Note the two halves and their order: enable us and others to glorify him, then dispose all things to his own glory. Us first, others second — the same two parties as this verse, in the same sequence, and the enabling belongs to God at both ends. You pray for the ability to do the works and for the neighbour's ability to see what they mean. Neither is in your gift.

And underneath the whole thing, the doctrine that makes the last word of the verse true:

Baptist Catechism · Question 37

Q. What is adoption?
A. "Adoption is an act of God's free grace, by which we are received into the number and have a right to all the privileges of the sons of God."

Work through the Catechism →

An act of God's free grace. Not an arrangement you entered by good conduct and can lose by bad. Which is why your Father can stand at the end of a command about works without the command collapsing into wage-earning. The sonship is settled before the sentence starts. The works are what sons do, in front of people, with the family name on them.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the Sermon on the Mount memorize pack — carry it. It is one sentence and it will fit in your head by the end of a drive, and what it does once it is in there is ask a single question at the end of anything you have done well: who did that just point at?

They are supposed to see the works, not the man. This is the test and it is not subtle. After somebody watches you, what are they left thinking about? If the answer is him — how disciplined he is, how much he gives, how he handled that — the verse has not been obeyed even though every visible box is ticked. If the answer is a question about God, it has. You cannot fully control which happens, but you can tell a great deal by noticing which one you were hoping for while you did it.

Do the work where unbelievers are. The word is men — humanity at large, not the congregation. Most Christian men have arranged their lives so that nearly all of their observed obedience happens in front of other Christians, where it is safe and where it is graded generously. The verse points somewhere else: the crew, the shop, the neighbours, the man across the counter, the family who does not go to church and watches you anyway. If your best work is only ever seen at church, you have built a lamp cupboard with excellent lighting.

Make them the kind of good a stranger can read. Kalos is the good that shows. A pagan cannot see your private prayers or your convictions about the atonement, and he is not meant to. He can see whether you keep your word when it costs you, whether you are the same man at hour eleven as at hour one, whether your wife is easy in your company, whether you did the hidden part of the job properly. Those are legible without a translator. Start there, because that is what is actually on offer to be seen.

The trumpet is the other basket. One chapter later the Lord describes a man who does the same good works, in the same public, and loses everything by it, because he arranged the audience. In our era that arrangement is easier than it has ever been and it does not require a trumpet — a photograph will do. The rule of thumb is simple and it stings: if the good work needed documenting before it counted to you, it has already been paid out. Do a great deal that nobody records. It keeps the visible part honest.

You are not on the hook for the result. That they may see and glorify — both verbs belong to them. Some men will watch a decent Christian life and honour God for it. Some will watch the same life and hate it, and Peter warns that the honouring can be delayed a very long time. Paul names the worst case, that God's name gets blasphemed on account of a professing man, and it is the case you should fear about your own conduct rather than resent about other people's. But the responsibility line is clear: you are answerable for the works, not for the verdict. Do not measure your obedience by how many people were impressed.

Get the last clause settled before you attempt the first. The verse ends on your Father, and the whole thing runs backwards if a man is trying to acquire that Father by shining. He cannot. Adoption is free grace, the confession says the works themselves are God's workmanship, and Calvin calls them a gift that shows the Spirit of adoption has already come. So the order for a man who feels the weight of this verse and knows his own record is not: shine harder, and hope. It is: settle the sonship, then go to work. A son who knows he is a son can be seen doing good without needing anything back from the people watching — which is, precisely, the freedom this verse requires and the thing it is impossible to fake for very long.

And take the small scale seriously. The two times Matthew reports this actually happening, nobody preached. People watched something and the credit went upward. That is the whole ambition. Not a movement, not a reputation, not a platform — a life lived at the ordinary height, in front of the ordinary people, that leaves somebody thinking better of God than they did on Monday. It is a modest target and almost nobody hits it, because hitting it requires wanting God's name to be large more than you want your own to be well thought of. That is the entire difficulty of this verse, and no amount of effort applied at any other point will touch it.
