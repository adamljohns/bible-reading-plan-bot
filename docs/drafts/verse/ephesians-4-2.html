Verse Study · Ephesians

Ephesians 4:2

In the next breath Paul asks the church to keep its unity. This verse is the price he names first.

with all lowliness and humility, with patience, bearing with one another in love;— World English Bible (public domain)

With all lowliness and meekness, with longsuffering, forbearing one another in love;— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Read the verse in either translation above and notice what is missing. There is no command in it. There is no verb of its own in it. It opens with with, and it never arrives anywhere; it is a string of phrases hanging off the imperative in the previous line — walk worthily of the calling with which you were called. Verse 1 gives the order. Verse 2 tells you what the order is made of.

That grammar is the whole doctrine. These four dispositions are not a devotional garnish on the Christian life, the soft part you get to after the serious part. They are what a worthy walk consists of. A man can hold every line of Ephesians 1–3 — election, redemption, the riches of grace, the mystery made known — and still walk unworthily of all of it, and Paul tells you exactly where the failure would show: in how he handles the people who irritate him.

Paul had built this sentence before. Colossians 3:12–13 runs the same four words in the same order — lowliness of mind, meekness, longsuffering, forbearing one another — and then adds the clause Ephesians leaves implicit: even as Christ forgave you, so also do ye. The vocabulary is not incidental. It is a set piece Paul carried from letter to letter, because the problem it addresses does not change.

  • ταπεινοφροσύνη tapeinophrosynē · lowliness · Strong's G5012

    A compound: tapeinos, low, plus phrēn, the mind. Lowliness of mind. In the Greek and Roman world this was not a virtue and was never meant as a compliment — it was the word for a slave's outlook, the temper of a man with no standing to lose. Paul commands it of free men. The same noun stands in Philippians 2:3, where it is defined by what it produces: let each esteem other better than themselves, and in 1 Peter 5:5, where you are told to put it on like clothing. It is not a low opinion of yourself. A man can hold a low opinion of himself and still be measuring. It is a mind that has quit ranking the room.

  • πραότης praotēs · meekness · Strong's G4236

    Our lexicon gives it in five words: strength under control, not weakness. The New Testament backs that reading hard. It is fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5:23, so it is grown and not manufactured. And in 2 Corinthians 10:1 Paul appeals to his readers by the meekness and gentleness of Christ — the same noun — in the opening line of the most combative stretch he ever wrote. Meekness is not the absence of force. It is force with a bit in its mouth, and only a man who actually has some can display it.

  • μακροθυμία makrothumia · longsuffering · Strong's G3115

    Makros, long, plus thumos, passion or temper. Long-tempered — the exact opposite of the thing we call short-tempered, and the KJV's longsuffering is a better rendering than patience because it keeps the suffering in. This is the word Romans 2:4 uses of God toward sinners, and 2 Peter 3:15 tells you to count that longsuffering as salvation. It is worth distinguishing from hupomone (G5281), the endurance that holds up under pressure and circumstance. Circumstances do not argue back. Makrothumia is the patience aimed at people, which is why it is harder.

  • ἀνέχομαι anechomai · forbearing · Strong's G430

    To hold up under, to bear with, to put up with. It occurs some fifteen times in the New Testament and it is morally neutral about its object: the same verb is used in 2 Timothy 4:3 of people who will not endure sound doctrine. What is being borne has to come from the context. Here the context supplies it in the next two words. What you are holding up under is each other.

  • ἀλλήλων allēlōn · one another · Strong's G240

    The reciprocal pronoun, and the backbone of more than a hundred New Testament commands. Reciprocal is the point, and it is the part men skip. The verse does not appoint you the forbearing one and everybody else the difficulty. You are also the weight. Somebody in your congregation is obeying this verse right now with you as the assignment.

  • ἀγάπη agapē · love · Strong's G26

    The love that is a settled gift and duty rather than a mood. It governs the whole line: in love. Forbearance can be practiced without it, and when it is, it has a different name. A man who puts up with his brother while privately filing the offense has not obeyed this verse; he has written the brother off and called it maturity. The KJV's charity suffereth long in 1 Corinthians 13:4 is the same pairing — the love and the longsuffering are one act, not two.

One more word carries more than its size. The verse says with all lowliness — pas, the ordinary Greek word for the whole of a thing, with nothing held back. Paul does not ask for a working amount. He closes the door on the reserve clause every man keeps: humble, sure, up to the point where I am plainly in the right and he is plainly not.

2. Where It Sits

Ephesians 4:1 — I therefore, the prisoner in the Lord, beg you to walk worthily of the calling with which you were called,

Ephesians 4:3 — being eager to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace.

Ephesians breaks cleanly in half at chapter 4. The first three chapters are God's work reported: chosen before the foundation of the world, made alive when we were dead, saved by grace through faith, given access to the Father. Not one imperative in the lot worth speaking of. Then 4:1 turns the whole thing on its edge with a single word — therefore — and from there to the end of the letter it is walking, standing, submitting, working, fighting. Verse 2 is the first thing on that list. Before marriage, before children, before employers, before the armor and the sword, Paul puts four words about not being difficult to live with.

And he is not writing from a study. He calls himself the prisoner in the Lord in verse 1, as he did in 3:1. The man asking a comfortable congregation to be longsuffering is under guard while he asks it.

The calling he wants walked worthily was described two chapters back, and it explains why he chose these particular words. Chapter 2 says the Gentiles were far off, strangers to the covenants, without hope and without God. Then Christ broke down the middle wall of partition, killed the hostility in his own flesh, and made of the two one new man. So the church in Ephesus was a room containing two groups of people who had spent their entire history on opposite sides of a wall that God had just removed. The unity of verse 3 is not a mood. It is an engineering problem.

Which is why verse 3 says keep and not make. Verses 4 through 6 pile up the sevenfold one — one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all. The unity is a fact about the church before it is a duty laid on it. Nobody is asked to manufacture it. They are asked not to break it, and verse 2 lists the four things that keep a man's hands off it.

It does not stay theoretical for long. By verse 16 Paul has the body growing up into its head through that which every joint supplies, each part working in measure. That does not happen among men who cannot stand each other. The maturity of a church and the patience of its members are the same subject in this chapter, taken at two different distances.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin's commentary on Ephesians is not in this library, so what follows is the Institutes — where he takes up the exact material of this verse, under a heading that tells you what he thinks it costs. He files it under self-denial.

On why lowliness is so hard

Calvin does not treat pride as an occasional lapse. He treats it as the direction a man falls when nothing is holding him up, and he begins by naming the two texts Paul is drawing on here — Romans 12:10 and Philippians 2:3 — as commands the natural mind is simply not equipped to obey:

For so blindly do we all rush in the direction of self-love, that every one thinks he has a good reason for exalting himself and despising all others in comparison. If God has bestowed on us something not to be repented of, trusting to it, we immediately become elated, and not only swell, but almost burst with pride.

— Calvin, Institutes III.7

Then he describes the accounting a proud man keeps, and it is uncomfortably exact:

The vices with which we abound we both carefully conceal from others, and flatteringly represent to ourselves as minute and trivial, nay, sometimes hug them as virtues. When the same qualities which we admire in ourselves are seen in others, even though they should be superior, we, in order that we may not be forced to yield to them, maliciously lower and carp at them; in like manner, in the case of vices, not contented with severe and keen animadversion, we studiously exaggerate them.

— Calvin, Institutes III.7

Two ledgers, kept in the same chest, by the same clerk. Your faults are rounded down and his are rounded up, and the arithmetic feels like discernment the entire time it is running. Lowliness of mind is what it looks like when a man closes the second ledger.

On meekness that survives being provoked

Calvin knows the cheap version of this verse, and he refuses to be impressed by it:

Many bear about with them some measure of mildness so long as all things go smoothly and lovingly with them, but how few are there who, when stung and irritated, preserve the same tenor of moderation?

— Calvin, Institutes III.7

That is the test the verse was written for. A man's mildness in easy company proves nothing about his meekness, because nothing has yet asked him for any. The relevant data comes in only after he is stung.

On forbearing without flattering

The obvious objection to forbearing one another is that it sounds like a policy of pretending. Calvin answers it in the same chapter, and the qualification is as sharp as the command:

Then we are told to overlook their faults, not, indeed, to encourage by flattering them, but not because of them to insult those whom we ought to regard with honour and good will.

— Calvin, Institutes III.7

He makes the same distinction elsewhere, expounding Solomon's line that love covers a multitude of sins, and there he says outright what forbearance is for:

Those who hate are incessantly biting, carping at, upbraiding, lacerating each other, making every thing a fault; but those who love mutually conceal each other's faults, wink at many, forgive many: not that the one approves the vices of the other, but tolerates and cures by admonishing, rather than exasperates by assailing.

— Calvin, Institutes III.4, on Proverbs 10:12

Tolerates and cures by admonishing, rather than exasperates by assailing. That sentence is the difference between forbearance and cowardice. Both men decline to attack. Only one of them is still working on the problem.

On why you owe it to a man who has not earned it

And when Calvin has to say where the obligation comes from — because the brother in question is frequently not worth the trouble — he does not appeal to the man's merits at all:

we are not to look to what men in themselves deserve, but to attend to the image of God, which exists in all, and to which we owe all honour and love. But in those who are of the household of faith, the same rule is to be more carefully observed, inasmuch as that image is renewed and restored in them by the Spirit of Christ.

— Calvin, Institutes III.7

Note the escalation. The image of God in any man is reason enough. In a brother, the image is being actively repaired by the Spirit — so the man annoying you on a Sunday morning is a job site, and the contractor is God. That is a hard thing to keep sneering at.

Augustine, on what happens when discipline forgets this verse

Calvin quotes Augustine at length in his chapter on church discipline, against men who leave a church the moment its elders fail to correct something fast enough. Augustine's rule ties discipline directly to the two verses on either side of ours — bearing mutually with each other, and the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace:

Every pious reason and mode of ecclesiastical discipline ought always to have regard to the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. This the apostle commands us to keep by bearing mutually with each other. If it is not kept, the medicine of discipline begins to be not only superfluous, but even pernicious, and therefore ceases to be medicine.

— Augustine, quoted in Calvin, Institutes IV.12

Medicine that stops being medicine is poison. Correction offered by a man who has not first been longsuffering with the person he is correcting does not heal anything, and Augustine is willing to say it does harm. Calvin, a few lines earlier in the same passage, warns from Matthew 18 that discipline without moderation degenerates into destruction — correctors becoming executioners.

One more line, from Calvin's treatment of the communion of saints, where he is expounding Ephesians 4:4 — the verse two lines down from ours:

For if they are truly persuaded that God is the common Father of them all, and Christ their common head, they cannot but be united together in brotherly love, and mutually impart their blessings to each other.

— Calvin, Institutes IV.1

Cannot but be. Calvin runs the logic the direction Paul runs it. The oneness is the given; the brotherly love is what necessarily follows in men who actually believe it. Which means chronic impatience with the brethren is not first a personality problem. It is a persuasion problem.

4. Confessional Anchor

The 1689 cites this verse exactly once, and it picks the least abstract moment in the entire chapter on the church to do it — the moment a member has a grievance against another member and is deciding whether to keep showing up.

No church members, upon any offense taken by them, having performed their duty required of them toward the person they are offended at, ought to disturb any church order, or absent themselves from the assemblies of the church or the administration of any ordinances upon the account of such offense at any of their fellow members; but they are to wait upon Christ in the further proceeding of the church.

— 1689 LBCF, ch. 26, paragraph 13 — proof-texts Matthew 18:15–17 and Ephesians 4:2–3

Read what the framers assumed. They assumed the offense is real. They assumed you have already gone and done your duty toward the man. And they still say: do not disturb the order of the church, and do not stop attending. The one thing you are given to do is wait upon Christ while the church works. That is forbearing one another written as church polity, and it is aimed at the most common exit in congregational life — the quiet drift, the family that just stops coming, the offense that never gets named because leaving is easier than bearing.

The next chapter says why the obligation binds at all. It is not a rule about attendance. It is a fact about union:

And being united to one another in love, they have communion in each other's gifts and graces, and are bound to the performance of such duties, public and private, as in an orderly way conduce to their mutual good, both in the inward and outward man.

— 1689 LBCF, ch. 27, paragraph 1 — Of the Communion of Saints

And underneath all of it, the pattern. The Baptist Catechism spends one question on Christ's humiliation, and every clause of it is lowliness of mind at a cost no member of any church will ever be asked to match:

Christ's humiliation consisted in his being born, and that in a low condition, made under the law, undergoing the miseries of this life, the wrath of God, and the cursed death of the cross; in being buried, and continuing under the power of death for a time.

— The Baptist Catechism, Question 30

That is the argument Philippians 2 makes explicitly, and Ephesians 4:2 makes by assumption. You are asked for lowliness of mind by a Lord who was born in a low condition and stayed there through the grave. The command is not steep. It is downhill from where he went.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the PROVEN Men memorize pack — carry it. Carrying it does something specific. Most of these four words are needed in the ninety seconds before you say the thing you will have to apologize for, and ninety seconds is not enough time to go look a verse up. It has to already be in you.

The people this verse is about are not hypothetical. You can name them. There is a man at your church, or in your house, whose voice puts an edge on you before he has finished a sentence. Ephesians 4:2 is not general advice about being pleasant. It is about him, specifically, and Paul knew you would have one.

Forbearance is not silence, and it is not agreement. Calvin's line settles this: tolerate and cure by admonishing, rather than exasperate by assailing. A man who says nothing and seethes is not obeying this verse; he has just chosen a slower way to fight. If the thing needs to be said, say it — with lowliness, meekness, and longsuffering attached, and in love, which means you say it to his face and to nobody else.

Watch the exit. The 1689 names it because it is the ordinary path: real offense, honest attempt, nothing resolved fast enough, and then the slow fade — a Sunday missed, then two, then a new church where nobody knows you well enough to disappoint you. The confession will not let you call that peacemaking. It calls it disturbing the order of the church, and it puts you back in the pew to wait on Christ.

Longsuffering has a length, and you are not the one who sets it. The word is makros — long. Not until you have made your point. Not until he apologizes. Romans 2:4 uses this same patience of God toward you, and 2 Peter 3:15 tells you what it bought. You are being asked to extend a measure of what you are currently living on.

And here is the part worth sitting with: you are also the load. Someone is obeying this verse with you in mind — bearing your tone, your certainty, the thing you always bring up. That is not a guilt trip. It is the reciprocal pronoun doing its work. A man who really believes he is somebody else's assignment finds the whole verse considerably easier, because he is no longer the only adult in the room, keeping score.
