Verse Study · Romans

Romans 1:16

A negative confession from a man who had every reason to make one. Paul says what he is not, and the whole letter follows from it.

For I am not ashamed of the Good News of Christ, for it is the power of God for salvation for everyone who believes; for the Jew first, and also for the Greek.— World English Bible (public domain)

For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ: for it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth; to the Jew first, and also to the Greek.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

I am not ashamed. It is a strange way to open the argument of Romans, and the strangeness is the point. Nobody announces that he is unashamed of something obviously admirable. You say it about a thing the room finds embarrassing.

And the room did. Paul is writing to the capital of the empire about a Jewish provincial who was executed by Roman authority in the most degrading way the state had available. Every instinct of that culture read the message as shameful — foolishness to Greeks, a stumbling block to Jews, as he says elsewhere. So the sentence is not bravado. It is a man naming the specific temptation and refusing it out loud.

  • ἐπαισχύνομαι Epaischunomai · “I am” · Strong’s G1870

    The Greek verb epaischunomai (ἐπαισχύνομαι) means to be ashamed of or to feel shame about. It appears 11 times in the New Testament and is theologically decisive: Paul and other writers invoke it to address the temptation to be ashamed of the Gospel in a culture that viewed crucifixion as ultimate shame.

    Epaischunomai is to be ashamed of, to feel shame about something — and note the object. You are not ashamed of yourself here; you are ashamed of a thing associated with you. That is the precise pressure. The temptation is rarely to renounce Christ. It is to be quietly embarrassed by Him in company where He costs you something.

  • εὐαγγέλιον euangelion · “of the gospel” · Strong’s G2098

    Good news, glad tidings. From eu (good) + angelos (messenger).

    Euangelion is good news — and it was not originally a religious word. In the Roman world it announced imperial victories and the emperor’s birthday: a herald arriving in a city with news of a battle won and a new lord. Paul takes the empire’s own vocabulary for state announcements and applies it to a crucified man. That is not a metaphor he stumbled into. It is a claim about who actually won.

  • δύναμις dynamis · “the power” · Strong’s G1411

    The Greek dynamis means power, might, ability, or miraculous power. It is the root of the English words 'dynamic' and 'dynamite'.

    Dynamis — power, might, the word behind “dynamite.” Paul does not say the gospel explains God’s power or points to it. It is the power of God for salvation. The message is not a description of the rescue; it is the instrument of it. That is why preaching matters and why Paul is not embarrassed: the thing he is carrying is not a philosophy about God’s power but the operative form of it.

  • σωτηρία sōtēria · “salvation” · Strong’s G4991

    The NT's primary word for salvation, 46 times. It encompasses rescue from danger, preservation, and — theologically — God's complete work delivering humanity from sin, death, and judgment.

    Soteria covers rescue, preservation, deliverance — like the Hebrew yeshuah, both present and future. Worth holding, because “salvation” has narrowed in English to mean a transaction about the afterlife. In Paul it is the whole rescue of a person, begun now.

  • πιστεύω pisteuō · “that believeth” · Strong’s G4100

    To believe, to trust, to have confidence in, to entrust oneself to. Pisteuō is the verb form of pistis (faith).

    Pisteuo is not merely to accept a proposition but to entrust oneself. And the scope Paul attaches is total: everyone who believes. No qualifier of ethnicity, class, or record. That universality is what will occupy the next ten chapters.

“To the Jew first, and also to the Greek”

The order is historical, not preferential — the gospel came through Israel and went to the nations. But the and also is the explosive half. Paul is writing to a congregation with both in it, and a great deal of Romans exists to work out how they belong at the same table. The verse that opens the letter’s argument already contains the problem the letter will spend sixteen chapters solving.

2. Where It Sits

The verse sits at the seam between Paul’s introduction and his argument. He has spent fifteen verses on greetings and on his long-frustrated desire to visit Rome — he has purposed to come many times and been prevented, and he is eager to preach the gospel there too.

Then verse 16: for I am not ashamed. The for connects it to that eagerness. Why is he so determined to bring this message to the imperial capital of all places? Because he is not ashamed of it, and because it is power.

And verse 17 completes the thought: in it is revealed God’s righteousness, from faith to faith, as it is written, the just shall live by faith. That quotation from Habakkuk is the thesis of the entire letter. Everything from 1:18 through chapter 11 is the exposition of it.

So these two verses are the hinge. Behind them, a travel plan. Ahead of them, the most sustained argument about God’s righteousness ever written.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Verse 17 is the verse that broke Luther open. He had understood “the righteousness of God” as the standard by which God condemns, and he said he hated the phrase — until he came to see it as the righteousness God gives. The Reformation’s whole doctrine of justification is downstream of that reading.

Which puts our verse in its proper light. Paul’s freedom from shame is not temperament. It is the consequence of what he says the gospel is: God’s power, delivering a righteousness that is received rather than achieved. A man who thinks he is recommending a moral program will always be somewhat embarrassed by it, because the program is always partly failing. A man who is announcing a victory has nothing to apologize for.

4. Confessional Anchor

Those whom God effectually calls, he also freely justifies; not by infusing righteousness into them, but by pardoning their sins, and by accounting and accepting their persons as righteous; not for anything wrought in them, or done by them, but for Christ’s sake alone.

1689 LBCF, ch. 11 — Of Justification

This is verse 17 in confessional language — a righteousness reckoned rather than produced. And it explains why verse 16 can be said without qualification.

If salvation ran on infused righteousness, the gospel’s credibility would rest on how its adherents were turning out, and any honest man would be embarrassed some of the time. The confession puts the credibility somewhere else: for Christ’s sake alone. That is a message you can carry into a hostile room without checking your own record first.

5. For the Man Reading This

Locate your actual shame, because it is rarely theological. Almost no Christian in the West is tempted to deny the resurrection at gunpoint. The temptation is smaller and constant: the conversation where you could say why, and change the subject. The email signature you thought better of. The dinner where you let a remark pass because pushing back would have cost you a reputation for being reasonable.

Paul’s counter is not “be braver.” It is a claim about what the message is. If the gospel is advice, then sharing it is imposing your preferences on people, and embarrassment is appropriate. If it is power for salvation, then withholding it is not tact.

So the practical question is not whether you feel bold. It is whether you believe the sentence. A man who genuinely thinks he is carrying the power of God does not need to be talked into mentioning it — and a man who is merely carrying an opinion should probably keep it to himself. Paul settled which one it was, in writing, before he started arguing.
