Verse Study · Hebrews

Hebrews 10:24-25

One sentence, one command — and it is not the one this verse usually gets preached for. The order is: study the man next to you, then do not quit the room.

Let us consider how to provoke one another to love and good works, not forsaking our own assembling together, as the custom of some is, but exhorting one another; and so much the more, as you see the Day approaching.— World English Bible (public domain)

And let us consider one another to provoke unto love and to good works: Not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together, as the manner of some is; but exhorting one another: and so much the more, as ye see the day approaching.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Most men have met this verse once, as the proof text for showing up. That is in here. But it is the negative half of a subordinate clause, hanging off a main verb that says something harder. The one command in the sentence is let us consider, and its object is not the service, the schedule, or the building. Its object is one another. You are told to study the people you sit with closely enough to know what would actually move them toward love — and then to apply pressure. Attendance is what the sentence assumes. It is not what the sentence asks for.

In Greek this is one sentence, and it is the third of three let us clauses stacked back to back: let us draw near (v. 22), let us hold fast (v. 23), let us consider (v. 24). Faith, hope, and love, in that order, all standing on the “therefore” of verse 19. Three participles then hang off the third verb — not forsaking, exhorting, seeing. So the architecture is simple: one order, three conditions on how it gets carried out.

  • κατανοέω katanoeō · “let us consider” · Strong’s G2657

    To look down into a thing and take full notice of it — kata (down, thoroughly) fused to noeō (to perceive). Not a glance. It is the verb of a man who stops and inspects, and it takes work. A man can sit in the same room for twenty years and never once do this verb. He can also do it as a critic, which is easier and useless. Hebrews wants a third thing: study aimed at the other man’s good, not at your assessment of him.

  • παροξυσμός paroxysmos · “provoke” · Strong’s G3948

    Para (alongside) plus oxys (sharp): a provoking, a stirring up, an irritating stimulus. English gets the medical word paroxysm from it. The other place it appears in the New Testament is the sharp dispute that split Paul and Barnabas over John Mark (Acts 15:39) — a genuine rupture. That is the register of the word. The writer of Hebrews takes a term for friction and points it at love and good works. The KJV’s “provoke” is the honest rendering; the softer modern “encourage” loses the edge the word is carrying.

  • ἐπισυναγωγή episunagōgē · “the assembling” · Strong’s G1997

    An intensive compound of sunagōgē, with the epi- prefix pushing toward a complete or final gathering. It names the act of gathering, not the room and not the hour on the calendar. It occurs twice in the New Testament: here, and in 2 Thessalonians 2:1, where it is the gathering of God’s people to Christ at His return. The same noun covers Sunday morning and the last day. That is not a coincidence the writer is unaware of.

  • ἐγκαταλείπω egkataleipō · “forsaking” · Strong’s G1459

    To leave behind, abandon, desert somebody in his hour of need. It is the word in the cry from the cross — why hast thou forsaken me — and the word in God’s promise never to do it to His own (Hebrews 13:5). The writer had gentler options. He could have said neglecting, or missing. He said deserting, and he said it about people, because that is what the absence actually is. The man who stops coming has not skipped an event. He has left a post.

  • ἔθος ethos · “as the manner of some is” · Strong’s G1485

    A custom or habit — a practice established by repeated use. This is the diagnosis, and it is merciless in its accuracy. Nobody resolves to abandon the church. It becomes a manner. The same word describes Jesus going to the Mount of Olives “as his custom was” (Luke 22:39). Habit is neutral machinery. It runs in whatever direction you last pointed it, and it does not need your permission to keep running.

  • παρακαλέω parakaleō · “exhorting” · Strong’s G3870

    To call alongside. It shares its root with paraklētos, the Comforter. The word spans the whole pastoral range — comfort the grieving, urge the wavering, press the hard-hearted, appeal to the man about to quit. Which of those you owe the person in front of you depends entirely on the person, and there is no way to know that from the back row on your way to the parking lot.

  • ἡμέρα hēmera · “the Day” · Strong’s G2250 · ἐγγίζω eggizō · “approaching” · Strong’s G1448

    Hēmera is the ordinary word for a day; here it stands with no adjective at all — just the Day, the one the prophets meant, when God settles accounts. Eggizō means to draw near, and it carries both distance and time: it is the verb behind “the kingdom of God has come near.” Note who is moving. The Day is approaching you. You are not approaching it. And the writer draws the opposite conclusion from the one men usually draw under pressure — as the thing closes in, gather more, not less.

Two smaller things repay attention. The love (agapē, G26) here is not a mood to be worked up; it is the settled thing God commended at the cross, now owed sideways between men. And the works are kalos (G2570) erga (G2041) — not merely correct deeds but fine ones, good in a way that is visible and commends itself to anybody watching. Hebrews is not asking you to nudge your brother toward technical compliance. It is asking you to provoke him into something worth seeing.

2. Where It Sits

Hebrews 10:19 is the hinge of the whole letter. Ten chapters have prosecuted one case: that Jesus is a better priest, with a better sacrifice, in a better sanctuary, under a better covenant. Then verse 19 turns the corner — “Having therefore, brothers, boldness to enter into the holy place by the blood of Jesus.” Everything that follows is what a man does with boldness he did not earn.

What follows is three moves. Draw near (v. 22) is vertical. Hold fast (v. 23) is durative — it is about staying put over time. Consider one another (v. 24) is horizontal, and it is not a lesser appendix to the first two. It is where the first two get their maintenance. The letter does not treat the gathered church as an optional support for private faith. It treats the gathering as the place private faith is kept from rotting.

Read what comes immediately after and the stakes get plain. Verse 26: “For if we sin willfully after we have received the knowledge of the truth, there remains no more a sacrifice for sins” — running down to verse 31, “It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God.” The paragraph about not skipping the assembly is bolted directly onto the severest warning in the letter. That is not an accident of chapter divisions. The people the writer is worried about are drifting, and drifting men leave the room first. Absence is not the disease. It is the first symptom anybody else can see.

He had already said the same thing earlier, with the same verb: “but exhort one another day by day, so long as it is called ‘today’; lest any one of you be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin” (Hebrews 3:13). Note what the stated defense against a hardening heart is not. It is not more private discipline. It is other people, out loud, daily.

And these were not comfortable people with a scheduling conflict. Verses 32-34 remember “the former days” when they endured a great struggle with sufferings, were publicly exposed to reproach, and had their property plundered. Meeting cost them something. That is exactly the condition under which the “manner of some” sets in — not laziness, but a slow, reasonable-sounding calculation that the gathering is not worth what it costs this week.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin’s commentary on Hebrews is not in this library, so it is not quoted here. His Institutes are, and Book IV opens on exactly this ground — the church as the ordinary means God uses to keep His people. Calvin’s first move is to refuse the idea that a man can have the Father without the household:

What God has thus joined, let not man put asunder … to those to whom he is a Father, the Church must also be a mother.

— Calvin, Institutes IV.1 (Beveridge, 1845)

Then he names the exact man Hebrews is warning, and does it without flattery. The people who quit the assembly in Calvin’s day did not think of themselves as quitters. They thought of themselves as advanced:

Pride, or fastidiousness, or emulation, induces many to persuade themselves that they can profit sufficiently by reading and meditating in private, and thus to despise public meetings, and deem preaching superfluous.

But since as much as in them lies they loose or burst the sacred bond of unity, none of them escapes the just punishment of this impious divorce, but become fascinated with pestiferous errors, and the foulest delusions.

— Calvin, Institutes IV.1 (Beveridge, 1845)

That is a five-hundred-year-old description of a man with a study Bible, a podcast queue, and no congregation. Calvin’s claim is not that such a man is lazy. It is that he is unprotected, and that the errors will find him. He puts the weight on God’s own estimate of the thing:

For such is the value which the Lord sets on the communion of his Church, that all who contumaciously alienate themselves from any Christian society, in which the true ministry of his word and sacraments is maintained, he regards as deserters of religion.

— Calvin, Institutes IV.1 (Beveridge, 1845)

He is careful, though, and the care matters. Calvin is not defending a church that has gone wrong; he is defending the church where the Word is truly preached and the ordinances rightly administered, however unimpressive the people in it. In the same chapter he cuts off the most common modern exit — leaving because the congregation is full of sinners:

But if the holy prophets felt no obligation to withdraw from the Church on account of the very numerous and heinous crimes, not of one or two individuals, but almost of the whole people, we arrogate too much to ourselves, if we presume forthwith to withdraw from the communion of the Church, because the lives of all accord not with our judgment, or even with the Christian profession.

— Calvin, Institutes IV.1 (Beveridge, 1845)

And he draws the line where Hebrews draws it — at the moment the thought first occurs, not at the moment a man finally walks:

How perilous, then, nay, how fatal the temptation, when we even entertain a thought of separating ourselves from that assembly in which are beheld the signs and badges which the Lord has deemed sufficient to characterise his Church!

— Calvin, Institutes IV.1 (Beveridge, 1845)

4. Confessional Anchor

The 1689 divines cite this text in three different places, and where they cite it tells you how they read it. It shows up first under worship — not under membership, and not under obedience in general:

Neither prayer nor any other part of religious worship is now, under the gospel, tied unto or made more acceptable by any place in which it is performed, or toward which it is directed; but God is to be worshiped everywhere in spirit and in truth — as in private families daily, and in secret each one by himself, so more solemnly in the public assemblies, which are not carelessly nor wilfully to be neglected or forsaken, when God by his Word or providence calls thereunto.

— 1689 LBCF, ch. 22, Of Religious Worship

Read the two adverbs. Carelessly covers the man who drifted. Wilfully covers the man who decided. The confession refuses to let either one off, and it grants the private and family exercises their full weight in the same breath — which is what makes the sentence honest. Nobody is claiming Sunday is the only place God is worshiped. The claim is that the solemn public assembly is a distinct thing that the other two do not replace.

Saints by profession are bound to maintain a holy fellowship and communion in the worship of God, and in performing such other spiritual services as tend to their mutual edification; as also in relieving each other in outward things, according to their several abilities and necessities.

— 1689 LBCF, ch. 27, Of the Communion of Saints

“Relieving each other in outward things” is the confession’s gloss on good works. It means money, tools, time, a truck on a Saturday. The provoking Hebrews commands is not a matter of finding the right words. It has a bill attached.

Then the chapter on the church closes off the specific exit that swallows most men who leave — the offense taken at a brother:

No church members, upon any offense taken by them, having performed their duty required of them toward the person they are offended at, ought to disturb any church order, or absent themselves from the assemblies of the church or the administration of any ordinances upon the account of such offense at any of their fellow members; but they are to wait upon Christ in the further proceeding of the church.

— 1689 LBCF, ch. 26, Of the Church

Note the order the divines put it in. You go to the man first — that is the duty required. Then, having done it, you stay. Absence is not permitted as a form of protest even when the offense is real. And the catechism turns all of this into a plain duty a man can either be doing or not be doing:

It is the duty of those who are rightly baptized to give themselves up to some particular and orderly church of Jesus Christ, that they may walk in all the commandments and ordinances of the Lord blameless.

— The Baptist Catechism, Q. 101

Some particular church. Not the church in general, not the church as an idea you approve of. A named congregation with an address, elders who know you, and a roll you are on. That is the only kind of church in which Hebrews 10:24 can actually be obeyed, because you cannot consider people you have not committed to standing next to.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is the Fellowship spoke of The Wheel memorize pack, paired with Matthew 18:20 — carry it. Christ is the hub; obedience is the rim; Word, Prayer, Fellowship, and Witness are the spokes. The reason this one is a spoke and not a decoration is that a wheel with three spokes still turns for a while. It just does not hold up under load.

Consider is homework, and it has a pass/fail. Name three men in your congregation. For each one, name what he is actually carrying right now and what would move him toward love and good works this month. If you cannot answer, you are not disobeying some vague call to community — you are failing the main verb of the sentence. Attendance without katanoeō is a body in a chair.

Provoking costs you. A paroxysmos is friction. Sending a text that says “praying for you” costs nothing and provokes nothing. Asking a man a question he would rather not answer, and then staying in the room while he decides whether to resent you for it — that is the word. Do it badly and you will hurt somebody. Do it never and you have simply declined the command.

The habit is the enemy, not the decision. The text does not say “as some have decided.” It says as the manner of some is. Two Sundays becomes a stretch, a stretch becomes a season, and a season becomes a man who says he is still a Christian and means it. Watch the machinery, not your intentions. Intentions are not the thing that got anybody out of church.

Stay when the room disappoints you. The confession is deliberate here, and it is not sentimental: go to the man, do your duty toward him, and then keep sitting down in the same building. Leaving over an offense is the one exit the 1689 explicitly nails shut. Not because your grievance is imaginary — because sulking has never once been listed among the means of grace.

And there is the last clause, which is comfort and pressure at the same time. So much the more, as ye see the day approaching. Every argument the world makes for pulling back — it is getting worse out there, people are unreliable, I am tired — Hebrews takes and reverses. The Day is not receding, and it is not waiting on you to be ready. It is closing distance. So the answer to the pressure is more of what you were already commanded: gather, consider, provoke, exhort. The men who make it to that Day will not be the ones who held out alone. There is no such category in this book.
