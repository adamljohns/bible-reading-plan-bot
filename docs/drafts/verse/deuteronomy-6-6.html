Verse Study · Deuteronomy

Deuteronomy 6:6

The words have to land in the father before they can be sharpened on the children. Verse 7 is famous. Verse 6 is the one most houses skip.

These words, which I command you today, shall be on your heart;— World English Bible (public domain)

And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart:— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Moses has just given the Shema. Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one. You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, soul, and might. Then, before any talk of children, doors, or phylacteries, he says this: these words shall be in your heart.

The grammar is not a suggestion. Shall be. The words are not a poster for the house. They are a resident of the man.

  • דָּבָר dabar · “these words” · Strong’s H1697

    From the root H1696 (to speak), dabar is a common and significant Hebrew word. It encompasses a wide range of meanings, including a spoken word, a matter or affair, a thing, a command, or a promise.

    Dabar is speech that has become a matter — a word, a command, a thing. “These words” in context are not inspirational fragments. They are the commandments, statutes, and judgments of 6:1, concentrated in the confession of 6:4–5. What has to be in the heart is the whole claim: this God, this love, this exclusive allegiance.

  • צָוָה Tsavah · “which I command” · Strong’s H6680

    Tsavah means to command, order, charge, or appoint. It appears about 495 times.

    The words do not enter the heart as a hobby. They are commanded. Moses is not offering Israel a spirituality. He is placing them under a charge that has to be internalized, not merely archived.

  • לֵבָב lebab · “in thine heart” · Strong’s H3824

    The Hebrew noun lebab is the longer twin of leb (H3820). It still means heart — the inner man of love, memory, and will.

    As on Psalm 119:11, Hebrew “heart” is not the seat of feelings as opposed to the head. It is the inner man — mind, will, affection — the place decisions are actually made. So this is not “feel warmly about the Bible.” It is: these words must get down into the part of you that chooses before you have time to perform.

    Verse 7 will talk about teaching children. Verse 6 will not let you outsource that to a curriculum you do not yourself carry. A father whose heart is empty cannot whet a blade he does not hold.

2. Where It Sits

Deuteronomy 6 is the chapter after the Ten Words, and it is aimed at a people about to inherit houses they did not build (6:10–12). The danger is not ignorance in the wilderness. The danger is fullness in the land.

So the sequence is deliberate. One LORD (v. 4). Whole-self love (v. 5). Words in the heart (v. 6). Then — only then — diligent teaching of the children in the ordinary hours (v. 7). Bind them. Write them. And when the son asks, tell him the exodus story (vv. 20–25).

If you start at verse 7 you get a parenting technique. If you start at verse 6 you get a man who has to be converted before he can catechize.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed instinct on this verse is the same instinct as Psalm 119:11: Scripture is not only the church’s public authority. It is the believer’s inward supply. That is why the tradition built family worship instead of leaving the text to clergy.

I am not hanging a verbatim Father-quote on this page this turn; the Henry fetch for this verse did not return a clean public-domain edition I could check word for word. The argument of the chapter does not need one. Moses already said it: the words are commanded today, and the first destination is the man’s own heart.

4. Confessional Anchor

The Holy Scripture is the only sufficient, certain, and infallible rule of all saving knowledge, faith, and obedience.

1689 LBCF, ch. 1 — Of the Holy Scriptures

Obedience is the clause that ties the confession to Deuteronomy 6:6. A rule that never gets into the heart cannot govern the house. Sufficiency matters here too: you do not need a second secret word for your children if the commanded words are actually in you.

5. For the Man Reading This

Do not start with a family plan. Start with whether the words are in you.

Pick the Shema, or the verse you keep telling your sons they should know, and put it in your own mouth until it is in your heart. If you cannot say it without looking, you are not ready to shanan it in verse 7. You are decorating.

The test is not whether the house has Bibles. The test is whether, when you sit down and when you walk, the words that come out of you are the words that were commanded — or the words that happen to be in you instead.

A father whose heart is empty cannot whet a blade he does not hold. That is not a parenting tip. It is the order of the chapter: the commanded words first become a resident of the man, and only then become speech in the house.

If the words are only a poster, verse 7 will be theater. If they are in the heart, verse 7 has something to sharpen. Moses will not let you skip the first destination and subcontract the second.

The danger named later in the chapter is fullness — houses you did not build, wells you did not dig. A full house with an empty heart is exactly the man this verse is aimed at. He can buy curriculum. He cannot buy a heart that already carries the words.

So start here, before you start there. The Shema is not a slogan for the boys. It is a charge on you this day. Shall be in thine heart. Then, and only then, teach them diligently.

These words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart. The first destination is the man’s own heart. The second destination, in verse 7, is the children’s ears. Skip the first and the second is decoration.

A silent house is not the only failure. A talking house whose father does not carry the words is the same failure with more noise. Verse 6 is the lock. Verse 7 is the whetstone. Keep them in that order.

In the Family Captain memorize pack — carry it.
