Verse Study · Luke

Luke 11:28

A woman in the crowd blesses the mother who bore him. Jesus does not deny her a thing — he moves the line, and the new line runs straight through every man who hears a sermon on Sunday and cannot find it by Tuesday.

But he said, “On the contrary, blessed are those who hear the word of God, and keep it.”— World English Bible (public domain)

But he said, Yea rather, blessed are they that hear the word of God, and keep it.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The sentence is a correction, and most readers hear it as a rebuke. It is not one. Jesus is not telling the woman she is wrong about his mother. He is telling her she has stopped one rank short of the thing worth envying, and that the rank she is standing on cannot be reached by anybody else in the crowd while the one he names is open to all of them.

Everything turns on the little word at the front. The King James has him say Yea rather; the World English Bible has On the contrary. The two renderings are pulling in slightly different directions and both are honest, because the Greek particle underneath does both jobs at once — it concedes and it raises. Not no, and not quite yes. Closer to: true enough, and now let me tell you what actually counts.

What follows is one blessing with two conditions, and the second one is where the sentence bites.

  • ἔπω epō · said · Strong's G2036

    The verb that carries the past tense of legō, and the workhorse that introduces nearly every recorded statement of Jesus in the Gospels. It is worth pausing on how ordinary it is. The whole exchange is a woman shouting from a crowd and a man answering her out loud. Christianity does not rest on an atmosphere or an experience. It rests on things that were said, in the open, in front of witnesses who could have contradicted them.

  • μακάριος makarios · blessed · Strong's G3107

    Not cheerfulness, and not luck. This is the word that opens the Beatitudes, and in Greek before Jesus got hold of it, it described gods who lived above human trouble and the dead who were finally beyond it. He keeps the height and changes the address. In his mouth the blessed are the poor in spirit, the mourning, the persecuted — and here, the man who listens and then guards what he heard. The woman used the same idea one verse earlier about a womb. Jesus does not lower her word. He relocates it.

  • ἀκούω akouō · hear · Strong's G191

    The ordinary verb for hearing — sound striking the ear — but in Scripture it never stays merely physical. It carries the freight of the Hebrew shama, where hearing and obeying are not two acts but one, which is why Hear, O Israel is a command and not an announcement. Jesus keeps saying he that hath ears to hear, let him hear, which would be a strange thing to say to people who were plainly not deaf. Hearing, in this sense, is something a man can fail to do at full volume.

  • λόγος logos · the word · Strong's G3056

    The word, the message, the account. John's prologue will eventually load this term with everything it can bear — the Word who was with God and was God — and every other use in the New Testament stands in that shadow. Here it is concrete and unglamorous: what God has actually said. Not an impression of God, not a sense of the divine, not what a man concluded about God on his own. A message with content, which is the only kind of thing that can be kept or lost.

  • θεός theos · of God · Strong's G2316

    The genitive is doing quiet work. It is not a word about God, or a word men assembled concerning him. It is his. That single syllable is the reason the sentence has any authority at all: strip the possessive and Jesus is recommending attentive listening to religious material, which nobody needs a Savior to recommend.

  • φυλάσσω phylassō · keep · Strong's G5442

    The hinge of the verse, and the word most likely to be read past. Phylassō is not the soft English keep that means hold on to as a souvenir. It is kin to phylax, a guard — to keep by guarding, to watch over a thing so that it is not lost, stolen, or quietly ignored. Luke has already used it seven verses earlier, of a strong man who keepeth his palace and whose goods are therefore in peace. That is a sentry, not a collector. Jesus is describing a man who has posted a watch over what he heard so that it cannot walk out of the house.

Put the last two together and the shape of the blessing is clear. Hearing is where it starts and it is not where it ends. A man can fill his ears every week and leave the gate wide open behind him. The blessing is not on the ear. It is on the guard.

2. Where It Sits

Luke has been building to this for most of the chapter, and the setting is not a sermon — it is a fight. Jesus has cast out a demon and been accused of doing it by the prince of demons. He answers the charge, and then tells two short stories that hang directly over verse 28.

Luke 11:21 — “When the strong man, fully armed, guards his own dwelling, his goods are safe.”

Luke 11:25–26 — “When he returns, he finds it swept and put in order. Then he goes, and takes seven other spirits more evil than himself, and they enter in and dwell there. The last state of that man becomes worse than the first.”

That is the picture the woman interrupts: a house cleaned out and left empty. Not a house full of filth — a tidy one, swept and put in order, and worse off at the end than it was at the start, because nobody moved in and nobody stood watch. Verse 28 is the answer to that empty house. What fills a man is the word of God; what holds it is the guard he posts over it. Luke put the same verb in both places, seven verses apart, and it is not an accident.

Luke 11:27 — It came to pass, as he said these things, a certain woman out of the multitude lifted up her voice, and said to him, “Blessed is the womb that bore you, and the breasts which nursed you!”

Her instinct is a good one. A man says something like this and you want to know who raised him. But her blessing lands on a fact of biology, and no one else in that crowd could ever qualify for it. Jesus takes the blessing off a body and puts it on a response — and the moment he does, the door opens to everybody standing there, including her.

Luke has already told us that this is not a demotion for Mary. In the first chapter of the same Gospel, Elizabeth pronounces her blessed, and pronounces the reason:

Blessed is she who believed, for there will be a fulfillment of the things which have been spoken to her from the Lord!

Luke 1:45, World English Bible

Blessed because she believed. Not because she carried him — because she took God at his word. And Mary's own answer to the angel is Luke 11:28 in a single line, spoken before the child was conceived:

Behold, the servant of the Lord; let it be done to me according to your word.

Luke 1:38, World English Bible

So Jesus is not correcting his mother's honor. He is telling the woman in the crowd that the thing she admires in Mary is available to her, and that it was never the womb. It was the hearing and the keeping.

This is standing habit in Luke, not a one-off. Earlier, told his family is outside asking for him, Jesus answers:

My mother and my brothers are these who hear the word of God, and do it.

Luke 8:21, World English Bible

And in the parable of the sower, the good soil is defined by exactly the pair we have here — heard, and held:

That in the good ground, these are such as in an honest and good heart, having heard the word, hold it tightly, and produces fruit with patience.

Luke 8:15, World English Bible

Then look at what comes next in chapter 11. The crowd swells and immediately asks for a sign. Jesus refuses, and points at two sets of outsiders who will stand up in the judgment and condemn the insiders: the Queen of the South, who traveled from the ends of the earth to hear wisdom, and the men of Nineveh, who repented at nothing but a sermon from a reluctant prophet. No miracle, no spectacle. A word, heard, and acted on. The generation standing in front of Jesus had a greater word than either of them and wanted fireworks instead.

Verse 28 sits between an empty swept house and a crowd shopping for signs. Both are pictures of religion with nothing kept in it.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

A limit worth naming first: Calvin's commentary on Luke is not part of this site's library, so nothing below is drawn from it. What follows is from the Institutes, which is.

Calvin — on where sound doctrine starts

Calvin locates the beginning of the Christian life at exactly the point Jesus does. Not in achievement, not in insight, but in a readiness to be told something:

For what is the beginning of true doctrine but prompt alacrity to hear the Word of God?

Calvin, Institutes I.7 — The Testimony of the Spirit

Prompt alacrity is a strong phrase for what most men bring to a sermon. It is not endurance and it is not attendance. It is leaning forward. And Calvin's point in that chapter is that this readiness is itself a gift — the Spirit who spoke the word must speak within, or the ear stays shut with the sound still going into it.

Calvin — on hearing that never becomes keeping

The other half of the verse gets Calvin's sharpest paragraph anywhere in the Institutes. He is writing about men who have the vocabulary of the faith and nothing else, and he refuses to grant them the benefit of the doubt:

Doctrine is not an affair of the tongue, but of the life; is not apprehended by the intellect and memory merely, like other branches of learning; but is received only when it possesses the whole soul, and finds its seat and habitation in the inmost recesses of the heart.

Calvin, Institutes III.6 — The Life of a Christian Man

Read that against Luke 11:28 and the fit is exact. Hearing puts a thing in the intellect and the memory. Keeping is what happens when it takes up residence. Calvin will not let the first stand in for the second:

To doctrine in which our religion is contained we have given the first place, since by it our salvation commences; but it must be transfused into the breast, and pass into the conduct, and so transform us into itself, as not to prove unfruitful.

Calvin, Institutes III.6 — The Life of a Christian Man

Notice he does not devalue doctrine to make room for obedience. Doctrine gets the first place. It simply is not allowed to stop where it landed. And then the line that closes off the usual escape:

For you cannot divide the matter with God, undertaking part of what his word enjoins, and omitting part at pleasure.

Calvin, Institutes III.6 — The Life of a Christian Man

That is what most of us actually do with the word we hear. We do not reject it. We take delivery of it and then decide which parts to sign for. Calvin's answer is that there is no such transaction available; God is not negotiating terms.

Calvin — on the mistake in verse 27

Calvin never comments on Luke 11:27 in the Institutes, but the impulse behind it — devotion aimed at Mary rather than at Christ — is one he treats at length in the chapter on prayer. His objection there is precise and worth stating in plain prose rather than borrowed sentences: he is not attacking anyone for honoring Mary, he is attacking prayers that ask her to command her Son to grant a request. The complaint is about who is being asked, and on whose authority. It is the same nerve Jesus touches in verse 28. Honor is not the problem. Mislocated blessing is. (Institutes III.20, Of Prayer →)

The apostolic echo

James says the same thing without the parable, and adds the diagnosis:

But be doers of the word, and not only hearers, deluding your own selves.

James 1:22, World English Bible

The word is deluding. The hearer-only is not merely underperforming; he is under a false impression about himself, and the sermon is what supplies it. James's image for him is a man who checks his face in a mirror, walks off, and forgets what he looks like. He got real information about a real problem, and it evaporated on the way to the door.

Jesus had already put the same warning in the form of a builder's question:

“Why do you call me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ and don’t do the things which I say?

Luke 6:46, World English Bible

Both houses in that parable heard the same words. Only one of them was still standing when the water came up.

4. Confessional Anchor

The Baptist Catechism takes Luke 11:28 and turns it into a procedure. It asks the question in two halves — what the Word does, and what we are to do with it — and it hangs Luke 8:15 and James 1:25 under the second, which are the two texts we have been standing on all the way through.

The Baptist Catechism · Question 94 — How is the Word made effectual to salvation?

"The Spirit of God makes the reading, but especially the preaching, of the Word an effectual means of convincing and converting sinners, and of building them up in holiness and comfort through faith to salvation."

Work through the Catechism →
The Baptist Catechism · Question 95 — How is the Word to be read and heard, that it may become effectual to salvation?

"That the Word may become effectual to salvation, we must attend to it with diligence, preparation, and prayer; receive it with faith and love, lay it up in our hearts, and practice it in our lives."

Work through the Catechism →

Question 94 keeps the agency where Jesus keeps it — the Spirit makes it effectual, not the listener's technique. Question 95 then hands the listener something to actually do, and the verbs are worth counting: attend, prepare, pray, receive, lay up, practice. Six of them, and only the last happens outside the ear. That is the anatomy of keep it. Diligence and preparation and prayer come before the hearing; laying it up and practicing it come after. The sermon is the middle of the operation, not the whole of it.

The 1689 says the same thing about faith itself. Saving faith is not a single posture toward a single kind of sentence; it responds differently to different parts of the Word, and obedience is one of its ordinary motions, not an optional upgrade:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 14, Paragraph 2 — Of Saving Faith

"By this faith a Christian believes to be true whatsoever is revealed in the Word, for the authority of God himself; … and also acts differently upon that which each particular passage thereof contains; yielding obedience to the commands, trembling at the threats, and embracing the promises of God for this life, and that which is to come."

Read Chapter 14, Of Saving Faith →

Yielding obedience to the commands. On this account a man who hears a command and files it has not exercised a weak faith; he has, at that point, not exercised faith at all. The word did not do in him what faith does with a word.

And before anyone turns this into a program of willpower, the confession's first chapter shuts that door too. The same Word that must be kept cannot even be understood savingly without the Spirit:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 1, Paragraph 6 — Of the Holy Scriptures

"Nevertheless, we acknowledge the inward illumination of the Spirit of God to be necessary for the saving understanding of such things as are revealed in the Word."

Read Chapter 1, Of the Holy Scriptures →

So the guard Jesus describes is posted, not self-appointed. That is a relief and a rebuke at the same time. It means you are not being asked to manufacture obedience out of nothing — and it also means the ordinary response to a word you cannot keep is to ask for help keeping it, not to lower the standard until it fits.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the The Word Hand memorize pack — carry it. It sits on the little finger, under Hearing, as the companion to Romans 10:17. That placement is worth more than it looks. The little finger is the weakest grip on the hand, and hearing is the weakest hold a man has on Scripture — the easiest to acquire and the fastest to lose. The pack is built so that you feel that. Luke 11:28 is the verse that admits it out loud: hearing is where the word arrives, and by itself it will not stay.

Three things follow, and none of them are comfortable.

Stop grading the sermon by how it felt. The standard measure of a Sunday — that was a good word — evaluates the delivery and the emotional weather. Jesus proposes a different instrument. What did you guard this week? A man can leave a service moved, genuinely, and be one of Calvin's men whose religion has never traveled past the tongue. The honest test is not what you felt on the way out. It is what was still standing on Thursday.

Post an actual guard. Phylassō is a duty, and duties have times and places. This is where it stops being abstract: write the one line down before you leave the seat. Say it to your wife at dinner. Put the one command you heard on the calendar with a day attached. That is not a productivity trick — it is the difference between a man who owns a thing and a man who was briefly in the room with it. The strong man in verse 21 has his goods in peace for one reason. He is armed and he is standing there.

Do not leave the house swept and empty. The most common failure among serious men is not filth. It is a cleaned-out life with nothing installed. You quit the drinking, cut the feed, killed the habit — good, and by itself the setup for verses 25 and 26. The vacuum is the danger. Something moves into an empty house, and it is never better than what left. Keeping the word is what occupies the ground you took.

And the counterweight, because this verse is easily turned into a whip. Calvin, in the same chapter where he refuses to let doctrine live on the tongue, refuses just as firmly to make perfection the entry requirement — he will not deny the name Christian to a man who has not arrived, since on those terms nobody would be left in the church. Aim at it, he says. Run at it as a goal. But the blessing in Luke 11:28 is pronounced on hearers who keep, not on hearers who have finished keeping. If you heard something this week and you are still holding on to it on a Tuesday afternoon when nobody is watching and it costs you something — that is the man Jesus called blessed. Not the one who kept it perfectly. The one who did not put it down.
