Verse Study · John

John 14:21

The room is full of men who love Jesus, and one of them will deny him before sunrise. So he tells them how love is actually identified.

One who has my commandments, and keeps them, that person is one who loves me. One who loves me will be loved by my Father, and I will love him, and will reveal myself to him.”— World English Bible (public domain)

He that hath my commandments, and keepeth them, he it is that loveth me: and he that loveth me shall be loved of my Father, and I will love him, and will manifest myself to him.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The sentence runs in two directions at once. The first half is a test — here is how you tell who loves me. The second half is a promise — here is what happens to the man who passes it. Both halves are worth slowing down over, because most men misread the first and never get to the second.

Start with the direction of the argument. Jesus does not say what you expect him to say. Six verses earlier he said it the ordinary way: “If you love me, keep my commandments” (v. 15) — love first, obedience follows. Here he reverses the sentence and runs it backwards: the one who has the commandments and keeps them, that is the one who loves me. Obedience is not merely what love produces. It is what love is recognized by. And that reversal matters enormously to a man trying to take his own spiritual temperature, because his affections are the one thing he cannot inspect directly and his conduct is the one thing he can.

Then count the loves. There are three in a single verse, and they are not equal partners. The man loves Christ. The Father loves the man. Christ loves the man. It reads like a circle, but it is a chain, and the man is not the first link — everything he offers here is answer, not initiative. John will say it flat in his first letter: we love because he first loved us.

  • ἔχω echō · has / hath · Strong's G2192

    A primary verb: to have, to hold, to possess. It appears about 708 times in the New Testament, and beyond simple possession it carries state of being and relationship. John's Gospel and letters lean on it hard to name what a believer actually holds — “Whoever has the Son has life; whoever does not have the Son of God does not have life” (1 John 5:12).

    Note that Jesus uses two verbs where one would have done. Has and keeps. He separates them because they come apart so easily. A man can have the commandments the way he has an owner's manual in the glovebox — genuinely in his possession, never once opened. Having them is real, and having them is not enough. But the order is not accidental either: you cannot keep a charge you were never given.

  • ἐντολή entolē · commandments · Strong's G1785

    Divine order and instruction — the word behind “If you love me, keep my commandments” (John 14:15) and behind the question about the greatest commandment in Matthew 22.

    The weight here is not on the noun but on the possessive attached to it. My commandments. He is standing in a room of Jewish men who had spent their entire lives calling these things God's commandments, and he claims them as his own without a word of explanation. That is a christological claim before it is an ethical one. He is not appending footnotes to Moses; he is speaking in the voice that gave Moses the law. And the noun is plural and concrete — not a mood of general devotion, but particular things said, which can be written down, remembered, and checked.

  • τηρέω tēreō · keeps / keepeth · Strong's G5083

    To keep watch over, to guard, to observe, to hold fast. The word covers both protective guarding and faithful observance, and it implies vigilance — active, intentional holding on to what matters.

    This is a sentry's word, not a filing clerk's. And it runs in both directions inside this same night. The disciples are told to keep his commandments; three chapters later Jesus prays, “Holy Father, keep them in your name” (John 17:11), using the same verb. The man standing guard over the commandment is himself under guard. Neither watch is the reason for the other, and dropping either one out of the picture wrecks it.

  • ἀγαπάω agapaō · loves / loveth · Strong's G25

    To love — not as a passing feeling, but as a settled will to seek another's good. In the New Testament this verb names the Father's giving of the Son, the Son's self-giving for his people, and the commanded love of God, neighbor, and wife. It is the verbal counterpart of the noun ἀγάπη (agapē), and it can mark either divine initiative or the believer's answering obedience.

    All three loves in this verse are the same verb. That is deliberate, and it is generous — the kind of love asked of a man is named with the word used of God's own. But same word is not same rank. His is the fountain; yours is the runoff. Which is exactly why the test in the first half of the verse cannot become a wage in the second.

  • ἐμφανίζω emphanizō · will reveal / will manifest · Strong's G1718

    To make visible, to manifest, to make known. It also carries a legal and official sense — to report, to lay information before authorities, which is how Luke uses it twice in Acts 23 of the plot against Paul. It appears only about ten times in the whole New Testament.

    Two of those ten are right here: Jesus promises it in verse 21, and Judas — not Iscariot — throws it straight back at him in verse 22. Something is going to be made visible. Not to everyone. That is the shock, and the next verse shows a man in the room feeling it.

Two smaller things repay attention. The pronoun behind “that person” is the emphatic demonstrative ἐκεῖνος (ekeinos), “that one” — John's word for singling out a specific figure rather than gesturing at a category. Jesus is not describing a type of person. He is pointing. And what Christ promises to disclose is not a set of facts about himself but the reflexive ἐμαυτοῦ (emautou) — myself. The reward for keeping his word is not better information. It is him.

2. Where It Sits

It is the last night. Judas Iscariot has already gone out into the dark. Jesus has washed their feet, named a betrayer at his own table, and told Peter — Peter, of all of them — that he will deny him before the rooster crows. These men are about to lose the center of their lives and they know it. John 14 opens with Jesus telling them not to let their hearts be troubled, which is not a thing you say to men who are calm.

Chapter 14 is built out of their questions. Thomas asks how they can know the way. Philip asks to be shown the Father. And immediately after our verse, a third man asks the question our verse provokes. In between, Jesus answers the grief underneath all three questions — not with consolation but with presence:

John 14:16–17 — I will pray to the Father, and he will give you another Counselor, that he may be with you forever — the Spirit of truth, whom the world can't receive; for it doesn't see him, neither knows him. You know him, for he lives with you, and will be in you.

John 14:18 — I will not leave you orphans. I will come to you.

So verse 21 does not sit in a discussion of ethics. It sits inside a promise about presence. The whole paragraph argues one thing: the mode of my presence is changing; the presence is not stopping. And then it names who will experience the new mode. Not the men with the strongest feelings about him. The men who keep his word.

Notice too what has already been said about the world in verse 17 — the Spirit is one whom the world can't receive. The line dividing insiders from outsiders was drawn four verses before our verse ever applies it. That is what makes the next question so honest:

John 14:22 — Judas (not Iscariot) said to him, “Lord, what has happened that you are about to reveal yourself to us, and not to the world?”

Judas is not being difficult. He is being a first-century Jew with an entirely reasonable expectation: when Messiah shows himself, he shows himself — publicly, to Israel, to the nations, in a way no one can miss. Why the private door? And Jesus does not argue the premise. He restates the condition, and then raises the promise higher than it already was:

John 14:23 — Jesus answered him, “If a man loves me, he will keep my word. My Father will love him, and we will come to him, and make our home with him.

John 14:24 — He who doesn't love me doesn't keep my words. The word which you hear isn't mine, but the Father's who sent me.

A manifestation was already more than the disciples expected. Verse 23 turns it into a residence. And verse 24 shuts the back door: the man who does not keep the words does not love him, whatever else he may sincerely feel. John never lets this go. Decades later he is still writing the same sentence in different ink:

  • John 15:10

    If you keep my commandments, you will remain in my love; even as I have kept my Father's commandments, and remain in his love.

  • 1 John 2:3–4

    This is how we know that we know him: if we keep his commandments. One who says, “I know him,” and doesn't keep his commandments, is a liar, and the truth isn't in him.

  • 1 John 2:5

    But whoever keeps his word, God's love has most certainly been perfected in him. This is how we know that we are in him:

  • 1 John 3:24

    He who keeps his commandments remains in him, and he in him. By this we know that he remains in us, by the Spirit which he gave us.

  • 1 John 5:3

    For this is loving God, that we keep his commandments. His commandments are not grievous.

That last clause is the one to hold onto. Not grievous. The apostle who insists most fiercely that love is measured by obedience is the same one who insists the measuring is not a burden.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin — doctrine is not an affair of the tongue

Calvin never wrote a line about John 14:21 that is held in this repository, but he wrote the substance of it at the opening of his treatment of the Christian life, and he wrote it with a bluntness the verse deserves. His target is the man whose Christianity is entirely verbal:

Doctrine is not an affair of the tongue, but of the life; is not apprehended by the intellect and memory merely, like other branches of learning; but is received only when it possesses the whole soul, and finds its seat and habitation in the inmost recesses of the heart.

Calvin, Institutes III.6 — Of the Life of the Christian Man

That is John 14:21 in one sentence, arriving from the other side. Jesus says the keeping identifies the love; Calvin says the doctrine is not even received until it has gone past the tongue. He will not allow the name to stand in for the thing:

None have intercourse with Christ but those who have acquired the true knowledge of him from the Gospel.

Calvin, Institutes III.6 — Of the Life of the Christian Man

And he names the purpose of the whole business in a way that keeps obedience from ever becoming the price of sonship:

the object of regeneration is to bring the life of believers into concord and harmony with the righteousness of God, and so confirm the adoption by which they have been received as sons.

Calvin, Institutes III.6 — Of the Life of the Christian Man

Confirm the adoption — not purchase it. The obedience in John 14:21 confirms a status already granted. Read it any other way and the verse turns into a treadmill.

Calvin — how Christ makes himself known

The promise at the end of the verse is the part men most want and most often chase in the wrong place. I will manifest myself to him. The instinct is to look for that manifestation somewhere off the page — an experience, an impression, a voice. Calvin puts it back where God put it:

it is always by his word that he manifests himself to those whom he designs to draw to himself

Calvin, Institutes III.2 — Of Faith

It concerns us to know not only what he is in himself, but also in what character he is pleased to manifest himself to us.

Calvin, Institutes III.2 — Of Faith

This is not a demotion of the promise. It is a location for it. The same word a man keeps is the word through which Christ shows himself — which means obedience and disclosure are not two separate transactions with a reward posted between them. They happen in the same place. And the internal work that makes the word land is the Spirit's, not the reader's:

Hence the Spirit performs the part of a seal, sealing upon our hearts the very promises, the certainty of which was previously impressed upon our minds.

Calvin, Institutes III.2 — Of Faith

Which brings us back to Judas's question, and Calvin does not soften the answer:

God does not bestow it upon all promiscuously, but, by special privilege, imparts it to whom he will.

Calvin, Institutes III.2 — Of Faith

The world hears the same sermon and goes home unmoved. That is not a failure of volume. It is the difference between hearing a word and having it sealed on you, and no man arranges that for himself.

Calvin — where the love is supposed to go

One more, because the verse can be read as though the whole Christian life were a private conversation between a man and Christ. Calvin, working through the Ten Commandments, will not let the first table stay sealed off from the second:

First, our mind must be completely filled with love to God, and then this love must forthwith flow out toward our neighbour.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 — Of the Moral Law

And that is not an outside imposition on John 14. The commandment Jesus had given a few minutes earlier, in that same room, was “that you love one another” (John 13:34). So when he says my commandments here, the nearest antecedent is the one about how these men are to treat each other. The love that proves itself lands on somebody.

4. Confessional Anchor

No paragraph of the 1689 Confession cites John 14:21 as its proof-text, so the anchor here is by doctrine rather than by footnote. Two doctrines are in play, and they answer the two ways a man can get this verse wrong.

The first error is to hear my commandments as a softer standard than the law — as though Jesus had reduced the requirement to something manageable. The confession says the opposite:

The moral law forever binds all, as well justified persons as others, to the obedience thereof, and that not only in regard to the matter contained in it, but also in respect of the authority of God the Creator, who gave it. Neither does Christ in the gospel in any way dissolve this obligation, but much strengthen it.

1689 LBCF, ch. 19, para. 5 — Of the Law of God

The catechism puts the same thing in one line, and it is worth memorizing alongside the verse:

The duty which God requires of man is obedience to his revealed will.

The Baptist Catechism, Q44 — What is the duty which God requires of man?

The second error is the opposite one: to hear the verse as a bargain, obedience traded for the Father's love. The confession heads that off by explaining where the obedience comes from in the first place:

Neither are the forementioned uses of the law contrary to the grace of the gospel, but do sweetly comply with it — the Spirit of Christ subduing and enabling the will of man to do that freely and cheerfully which the will of God, revealed in the law, requires to be done.

1689 LBCF, ch. 19, para. 7 — Of the Law of God

Freely and cheerfully. That is the same note John struck — his commandments are not grievous. A man who keeps them under duress, hoping to be loved as a result, has not yet understood what has already happened to him.

Then there is the practical use of this verse, which the confession makes explicit. The chapter on assurance describes exactly the man John 14:21 describes, and its proof-texts are the sibling verses in John's first letter:

yet such as truly believe in the Lord Jesus, and love him in sincerity, endeavoring to walk before him in all good conscience, may in this life be certainly assured that they are in the state of grace, and may rejoice in the hope of the glory of God.

1689 LBCF, ch. 18, para. 1 — Of the Assurance of Grace and Salvation

It rests also upon the inward evidence of those graces of the Spirit to which promises are made, and on the testimony of the Spirit of adoption, witnessing with our spirits that we are the children of God; and, as a fruit of this assurance, the heart is kept both humble and holy.

1689 LBCF, ch. 18, para. 2 — Of the Assurance of Grace and Salvation

Note the phrase inward evidence. This is what Christ's manifesting himself ordinarily looks like on the ground — not a vision, but a growing certainty resting on the blood and righteousness of Christ and confirmed by graces you can actually observe in yourself. The catechism lists them:

The benefits which in this life do accompany or flow from justification, adoption, and sanctification, are assurance of God's love, peace of conscience, joy in the Holy Spirit, increase of grace, and perseverance in it to the end.

The Baptist Catechism, Q39 — What are the benefits which in this life accompany or flow from justification, adoption, and sanctification?

And because a verse like this one can be turned into a whip, the confession says out loud what happens when a believer's sense of all that goes dark:

True believers may have the assurance of their salvation in various ways shaken, diminished, and interrupted — by negligence in preserving it; by falling into some special sin which wounds the conscience and grieves the Spirit; by some sudden or vehement temptation; or by God's withdrawing the light of his countenance and suffering even those who fear him to walk in darkness and to have no light.

1689 LBCF, ch. 18, para. 4 — Of the Assurance of Grace and Salvation

The manifestation can dim without the sonship moving an inch. Peter is the proof, and he was standing in the room when the promise was made.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the The Wheel memorize pack — carry it. The Wheel puts Christ at the hub and obedience on one of the spokes, and this is the verse that keeps that spoke from snapping in either direction: it will not let obedience be optional, and it will not let obedience be the purchase price. Carry it and you have a ready answer to both of the lies you are most likely to tell yourself.

Your love is auditable, and you already know how to audit it. Stop asking whether you feel warmly toward Christ. That question has never once produced a reliable answer at four in the afternoon on a hard Tuesday. Ask the question Jesus asks: what did you do with what he said. That one has an answer, and you can get it in about ninety seconds of honest reflection.

Has comes before keeps. A sentry cannot guard a post he was never briefed on. Most of the disobedience in Christian men is not defiance — it is ignorance that has been kept comfortable, because a command you have never actually read is a command you never have to refuse. If you cannot name five things Christ commanded, the problem is not your willpower. Go get the commandments. Reading the Gospels with a pen is not an advanced discipline.

The manifestation is not something you generate. Men chase the last clause of this verse into strange places. Calvin's answer is the plain one: it is by his word that he manifests himself. So the man who wants Christ made visible to him should sit under the word expecting exactly that — in the preaching he hears Sunday, in the chapter he reads Thursday. Not as a technique. As the appointed place where Christ has said he shows up.

You are not the first link in the chain. The Father's love is not the wage paid at the end of a week of obedience. Look at the order in the discourse: the Spirit is promised in verse 16 before the obedience is described in verse 21, and the residence is promised in verse 23 to a man who is already loved. If you are keeping commandments in order to be loved, you have the verse upside down and it will grind you into the ground. Turn it over. You keep because you were loved first, and the keeping tells you the love got through.

And when you fail the test. You will. Peter did, that same night, in public, three times. The confession is honest that assurance gets shaken by negligence, by a particular sin, by a temptation that came in hard — and it is equally honest that the man is not thereby cut off. Calvin's word to a Christian who is not where he wants to be is the sanest thing on this page: our labour is not lost when to-day is better than yesterday. That is not a low standard. It is a survivable one.

One concrete thing, since the verse is about concrete things. Name the single command of Christ that you currently know and are not keeping. Not the general project of becoming holier — the specific one, the one that came to mind before you finished reading the sentence. That is the one. Keep it this week. Nothing about your feelings has to change first.
