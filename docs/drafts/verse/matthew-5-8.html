Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:8

Eleven words in English, and the second half of them is impossible. Scripture says flatly that no man has seen God, and that no man can. Then Jesus tells a hillside full of ordinary men that some of them will.

Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.— World English Bible (public domain)

Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Two halves, and a man's whole reading of the verse depends on which half he takes as the hard one. Most men read the first half as the difficulty — pure in heart, an impossible standard — and the second half as the reward, vague and religious and far off. That gets it backwards. The first half is a description Scripture applies to actual people in this life. The second half is the one that ought to stop you, because the Bible says elsewhere, in plain words and more than once, that it cannot happen.

Hold both. Whatever else this beatitude does, it puts a man in front of a promise that the rest of the Bible calls fatal, and it does it without softening either end.

  • μακάριος makarios · “Blessed” · Strong’s G3107

    The Greek word for blessedness — not emotional happiness dependent on circumstances, but the deep well-being of those who are right with God. The Beatitudes open with makarios.

    Sixth of eight. By now the repetition is doing its own work: the word is a verdict handed down, not a mood worked up, and it is pronounced over men who do not yet have what they are promised. That is the pattern in every line. God says what a man is before He gives him what He has sworn to give.

  • καθαρός katharos · “pure” · Strong’s G2513

    The Greek adjective katharos means clean, pure, or undefiled — free from dirt or contamination whether physical, ceremonial, or moral. It is the root of the English word ‘catharsis’ (purification).

    The word runs across three registers at once, and the Gospel of Matthew works all three. It is the word for a scoured dish. It is the word for a man ceremonially fit to come near the sanctuary. And it is the word for a man who is not rotten inside. Jesus later hangs an entire indictment on the collision between the first sense and the third: you clean the outside of the cup and of the platter, but within they are full of extortion and unrighteousness. The Pharisees were not accused of doing nothing. They were accused of scrubbing the wrong surface.

    So the beatitude is not asking for a man who has never been dirty. It is asking about which side of the cup he has been working on.

  • καρδία kardia · “heart” · Strong’s G2588

    The inner person — the centre of the will.

    This is where English readers get led astray, because we have made the heart the organ of feeling and set it opposite the head. Scripture does not divide a man that way. The heart in the Bible is the command post: what he thinks, what he decides, what he loves, and what he does when nobody is watching, all issuing from one place. Jesus says as much in this same Gospel — out of the heart come evil thoughts, murders, adulteries, sexual sins, thefts, false testimony, and blasphemies — a list that mixes appetites and calculations and words without apology, because they all come up the same shaft.

    Which means pure in heart is not a statement about a man's feelings being tender. It is a statement about the source being clean, and therefore about everything downstream of it.

  • ὅτι hoti · “for” · Strong’s G3754

    One of the most common Greek conjunctions. It either introduces the content of a statement (“that”) or gives the reason for it (“because,” “for,” “since”). Context decides which.

    Small word, load-bearing. The blessedness announced in the first half is not free-floating; hoti ties it to a stated ground. These men are called blessed because of what is coming to them. Take the ground away and the beatitude becomes advice about inner tidiness. Leave it in and the beatitude is an announcement about a destination, made to men who are still on the road.

  • ὀπτάνομαι optanomai · “shall see” · Strong’s G3700

    To appear, to be seen, to show oneself. A form related to horao (to see); in the New Testament it belongs almost entirely to the vocabulary of the post-resurrection appearances of Jesus Christ — the specialized wording of the Easter witness.

    That is not a small thing to find sitting in a beatitude. This family of words is what the New Testament reaches for when it reports that the risen Christ was seen — the wording behind the roll call in 1 Corinthians 15 and the forty days of Acts 1. It is not the vocabulary of visions and impressions. It is the vocabulary of eyewitness testimony, of men who could be cross-examined about what was in front of them.

    And the tense is future, in every translation printed above. Not they see. Not they may glimpse. They shall see — a thing that has not happened yet, stated as settled.

  • θεός theos · “God” · Strong’s G2316

    The standard Greek word for God. In classical use it covered any deity; the New Testament transforms it through its Hebrew inheritance — theos is the one true God of Israel, Creator, Sustainer, and Redeemer, revealed definitively in Jesus Christ.

    Notice what the promise is not. It is not they shall see heaven, or they shall see their reward, or they shall see justice done. Every one of those would be easier to say and easier to believe. The object of the verb is God Himself. The beatitude promises a Person, and it promises Him directly, without a screen.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 5:7 — Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy.

Matthew 5:9 — Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called children of God.

The beatitude sits in the second half of the eight, among the four that face outward: merciful, pure, peacemaking, persecuted. The first four described a man being emptied — bankrupt, mourning, meek, starving for a righteousness he does not own. This one describes what he is like once he has been fed. And its neighbours are both about how a man stands toward other people. Purity of heart is set down in company, not in a monastery.

Then look at where the Sermon goes. Twelve verses on, Jesus states the thesis of the whole discourse: unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, there is no way you will enter into the Kingdom of Heaven. That is a strange demand, because on the visible measures nobody exceeded the Pharisees. They fasted more, gave more, and kept more rules than the men listening. So the excess Jesus requires cannot be a matter of adding. It has to be a matter of location — and what follows shows exactly where He means.

You shall not murder becomes: everyone who is angry with his brother is in danger of the judgment. You shall not commit adultery becomes: everyone who gazes at a woman to lust after her has committed adultery with her already in his heart. Jesus is not raising the bar. He is moving it — from the hand to the source. Verse 8 announces that program before He runs it. The Beatitude names the man; the antitheses show you the standard He is being measured by.

Chapter 6 turns the same axis a second time and lands it on religion. Do not do your charitable giving to be seen by men. Do not pray in the corners of the streets that they may be seen by men. Instead: shut the door, and your Father who sees in secret will reward you openly. There are two audiences available to a man and he must pick one. The men who spend their lives being seen have already collected. The men content to be seen by God alone are the ones told, back in verse 8, that they will one day do the seeing themselves.

Behind all of it stands a psalm the men on that hillside knew by heart. Psalm 24 is a temple liturgy — a question asked at the gate and an answer given by the gatekeeper.

  • Psalm 24:3

    Who may ascend to the LORD’s hill? Who may stand in his holy place?

  • Psalm 24:4

    He who has clean hands and a pure heart; who has not lifted up his soul to falsehood, and has not sworn deceitfully.

  • Psalm 51:10

    Create in me a clean heart, O God. Renew a right spirit within me.

  • Psalm 73:1

    Surely God is good to Israel, to those who are pure in heart.

  • Hebrews 12:14

    Follow after peace with all men, and the sanctification without which no man will see the Lord,

Jesus takes the entrance requirement for a building and makes it the entrance requirement for God. That is the move. The psalm asked who may climb a hill in Jerusalem; the beatitude answers a bigger question with the same words, and the reward is no longer standing in a holy place but seeing the One the place was built for.

And notice what David does with the standard when it is applied to him. Psalm 51 is written by the man who failed it in the worst way available, and he does not respond by promising to try harder. He asks God to create a clean heart — the same verb the Bible uses in its first sentence, the one that never takes a human subject. The Hebrew word under his clean is the Old Testament’s counterpart to katharos — the standard word for what is ritually and morally undefiled. He is not asking for maintenance. He is asking for something out of nothing. That is the only honest prayer available to a man who has read Matthew 5:8 and then looked inside.

Which leaves the second half of the verse still standing there, still impossible. God told Moses plainly: You cannot see my face, for man may not see me and live. John says no one has seen God at any time. Paul says God dwells in unapproachable light, whom no man has seen, nor can see. Three witnesses, no loophole. And yet Job, out of the ash heap, says that after his skin is destroyed, then in my flesh shall I see God. John says that when Christ appears we will see him just as he is. Revelation says of the servants of God, they will see his face. The Bible does not resolve this by lowering the promise. It resolves it by putting the seeing on the far side of a change in the man — resurrection, glory, likeness. He cannot survive the sight as he is. He is going to be made into someone who can.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed reading of this verse has to hold three things at once, and dropping any one of them wrecks the other two. First, that God actually judges by the inside, so the standard is real and not rhetorical. Second, that no man can meet it by working on himself, so the verse cannot be turned into a programme. Third, that the promise at the end is nonetheless certain. Calvin takes all three, and he takes the first one while expounding the very commandments Jesus is about to reinterpret.

But God, whose eye nothing escapes, and who regards not the outward appearance so much as purity of heart, under the prohibition of murder, adultery, and thefts includes wrath, hatred, lust, covetousness, and all other things of a similar nature. Being a spiritual Lawgiver, he speaks to the soul not less than the body.

Calvin, Institutes II.8

That is Matthew 5:21–28 stated as a principle. Calvin’s reason for it is worth having, because it is not sentiment: a human lawgiver cannot reach past outward acts, so human law does not try. God has no such limitation. He is not a magistrate working from evidence; He is the one who made the room your thoughts happen in. So the commandment against murder was always about wrath, and always had been, and Jesus is not amending Moses when He says so.

Calvin presses the same point onto repentance, and here he is careful about the order — because men reliably start at the wrong end.

Any one moderately versant in Scripture will understand by himself, without being reminded by others, that when he has to do with God, nothing is gained without beginning with the internal affections of the heart.

Calvin, Institutes III.3

He then reaches for James, and the phrase he lands on is the one this beatitude needs: Cleanse your hands, ye sinners; and purify your hearts, ye double-minded. Two commands, hands and hearts, and Calvin will not let you take them as a pair of equal chores.

Here, indeed, the accessory is set down first; but the source and principle is afterwards pointed out—viz. that hidden defilements must be wiped away, and an altar erected to God in the very heart.

Calvin, Institutes III.3

The hands are the accessory. The heart is the principle. And the word James uses for the men he is addressing — double-minded — is the diagnosis that stands opposite pure in this verse. The problem is not usually that a man has one filthy heart. It is that he has two clean-ish ones, running different programmes, and he has learned to switch between them without noticing. That is exactly the man the Sermon on the Mount is built to expose, and it is why Jesus says in the very next chapter that no one can serve two masters.

Now the second thing, and Calvin says it in a sentence hard enough that most treatments of this beatitude never quote it:

the Lord declares that no degree of holiness is thereby acquired, unless the heart has previously been made pure. And not contented with this, he declares that all the works performed by sinners are contaminated by impurity of heart.

Calvin, Institutes III.14

Previously. The purity is upstream of the good behaviour, not the accumulated result of it. Which closes the self-improvement reading of Matthew 5:8 for good: you cannot get a clean heart by performing the acts a clean heart would perform, because until the heart is dealt with those acts are carrying the contamination outward with them. Calvin is not saying good works are worthless. He is saying they are downstream, and the water is the problem.

So where does a man stand who reads the verse honestly and knows the answer? Calvin puts him under a covering that is not his own — Our imperfection and impurity, covered with this purity, are not imputed but are as it were buried, so as not to come under judgment — and that is the only ground on which the standard and the sinner can be in the same room.

Then the third thing. Calvin will not spiritualize the promise into a metaphor, and he will not pretend we can describe it either:

For though we are truly told that the kingdom of God will be full of light, and gladness, and felicity, and glory, yet the things meant by these words remain most remote from sense, and as it were involved in enigma, until the day arrive on which he will manifest his glory to us face to face.

Calvin, Institutes III.25

Note the honesty of it. He grants that the words are real and grants that they are past us — the thing is true, and we do not have the equipment to picture it, and both of those are permanent facts until the day itself. He anchors it, as John does, in what happens to us: it does not yet appear what we shall be, but we shall be like Him, for we shall see Him as He is. The change in the man and the sight of God arrive together. That is how the impossible promise gets kept.

And in the meantime there is an installment, which Calvin will not let anybody skip past on the way to the last day:

We too must follow this straight path, if we genuinely long for a true vision of God. We must go, I say, to the Word, where the character of God, drawn from his works, is described accurately and to the life.

Calvin, Institutes I.6

A man who wants to see God and will not open the book is not actually longing for God. He is longing for an experience. The face-to-face day is coming and it is not on your schedule; the Word is on the table this morning.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession does not comment on this verse, but chapter 13 answers the question it raises, and it answers it in the same breath as the verse from Hebrews that says the same thing Matthew 5:8 says. Read the whole first paragraph and watch where the purity comes from:

They who are united to Christ, effectually called, and regenerated, having a new heart, and a new spirit created in them, through the virtue of Christ's death and resurrection, are also farther sanctified, really and personally, through the same virtue, by his Word and Spirit dwelling in them

1689 LBCF, ch. 13 — Of Sanctification

A new heart, created in them. That is David’s prayer in Psalm 51 answered in confessional prose, and the order is the order Calvin insisted on — the heart first, then everything else. Then the paragraph runs to its end and lands precisely on our verse’s promise:

the dominion of the whole body of sin is destroyed, and the several lusts thereof are more and more weakened and mortified, and they more and more quickened and strengthened in all saving graces, to the practice of all true holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord.

1689 LBCF, ch. 13 — Of Sanctification

Without which no man shall see the Lord. The confession is quoting Hebrews 12:14, and Hebrews is saying what Matthew 5:8 is saying. Two witnesses, one rule: the sight of God is not handed to men who are indifferent to purity. But look at the verbs the confession chose. Destroyed. Weakened. Mortified. Quickened. Strengthened. Every one is something done to a man, not by him. And note the phrase more and more, twice — this is a slope, not a gate.

The very next paragraph says out loud what an honest man already suspects about himself:

This sanctification is throughout the whole man, yet imperfect in this life; there abide still some remnants of corruption in every part, from which arises a continual and irreconcilable war; the flesh lusting against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh.

1689 LBCF, ch. 13 — Of Sanctification

That paragraph has saved more men from despair than most sermons. Imperfect in this life. Remnants in every part — not one quarantined corner, every part. If you read Matthew 5:8, took inventory, and concluded the verse cannot be about you because the war is still on, the confession says the war is the evidence, not the disqualification. A corpse does not fight.

The Baptist Catechism states the source and the destination in two answers, and they belong together:

Sanctification is the work of God's free grace, by which we are renewed in the whole man after the image of God, and are enabled more and more to die to sin, and live to righteousness.

The Baptist Catechism, Question 38

At the resurrection believers, being raised up in glory, shall be openly acknowledged and acquitted in the day of judgment, and made perfectly blessed, both in soul and body, in the full enjoyment of God to all eternity.

The Baptist Catechism, Question 41
The two halves of the verse, in the confession’s own words

Pure in heart — a new heart created in a man, then worked on by Word and Spirit, more and more, and still imperfect on the day he dies. Purity here is a direction under new ownership, not a score.

They shall see God — made perfectly blessed, both in soul and body, in the full enjoyment of God to all eternity. Body included. The catechism will not let this become a disembodied idea, because the verse promises seeing, and seeing takes eyes.

5. For the Man Reading This

Start by refusing the two easy readings. The first says this verse is about sexual purity and nothing else, which lets a man who has his eyes under control feel finished. The second says it is about sincerity — meaning well, being genuine — which lets everybody off. Purity of heart includes the first and is bigger than the second. The catechism ties the seventh commandment to the preservation of our own and our neighbor's chastity, in heart, speech, and behavior, and that is real and it is not the whole account. A man can be chaste and rotten with ambition. A man can be sincere and sincerely divided.

Audit the seams, not the surfaces. A divided heart shows up at the joins between your lives — the tone that changes when you walk through the door at home, the browser history, the version of the story you tell at work versus the one you tell your wife, what you say about a man to his face and to his back. Nobody is watching the seams. That is the point. Pick one this week and close it: make the private version match the public one, in whichever direction the truth actually lies.

Chase the motive one layer down. Chapter 6 is merciless about this. Giving, praying, fasting — all three good, all three ruined by the question who is watching. So ask it about the thing you are proudest of. Why do you do the hard job well? Why did you tell that story? If the honest answer is that you wanted to be seen, you have already been paid, and the payment is worth exactly what it cost you. Do one good thing this week that nobody will ever be able to trace to you.

Guard the intake. The heart is the source, and the source is fed. What you look at, listen to, argue with, and scroll past every night is not neutral traffic; it is what the command post has to work with tomorrow morning. A man who feeds his heart contempt for eight hours should not be surprised at what comes up the shaft. Cut one line of supply. You know which one.

Do not confuse the war with defeat. The confession says the remnants are in every part and the war is continual and irreconcilable. If you are grieved by what you find inside, that grief is not evidence against you. Unbelief is not grieved. Read the fight as a sign of life, then get back up — but do not use the doctrine as a blanket. Remnants of corruption is a description of a man moving, not a permit to stop.

Pray the impossible prayer. David did not promise God a better performance; he asked to be issued a heart he did not have. That is the prayer, and it is available every morning. Create in me a clean heart, O God. Eight words. If you have nothing else, you have that.

And carry the promise as it is written, because it is the largest thing offered to a man anywhere in Scripture. Moses asked to see the glory of God and was told he could not survive it. You are told that you will. Not a report about God, not a reward from God — God, seen. Everything the Sermon on the Mount asks of a man is priced against that, and against that it is cheap.

In the Sermon on the Mount memorize pack — carry it. Eleven words. Say them the next time you catch yourself running two versions of the same day.
