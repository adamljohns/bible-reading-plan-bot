Verse Study · 2 Corinthians

2 Corinthians 7:10

Two men can weep the same tears over the same sin and end up in opposite places. Paul says the difference is not how hard you cried.

For godly sorrow produces repentance to salvation, which brings no regret. But the sorrow of the world produces death.— World English Bible (public domain)

For godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of: but the sorrow of the world worketh death.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Read the sentence slowly and you will see that it is built as two columns with a wall down the middle. The same noun stands at the head of both — sorrow, and sorrow. The same verb sits in the middle of both — worketh, and worketh. Then the products come out the bottom, and they are not two grades of the same thing. One column ends in salvation. The other ends in death.

That is the first thing to get straight, because it is the thing men get wrong. Paul is not contrasting sorrow with joy, or grief with denial, or a soft heart with a hard one. He is contrasting sorrow with sorrow. Both men are wrecked. Both are genuinely miserable. Both mean it. The verse does not offer the option of not being sorry; it tells you that being sorry has two versions, and that only one of them is going anywhere.

The Strong's-tagged King James makes something visible that the smooth English hides. Where the KJV prints the single word godly, the underlying text carries two tags — G2596 and G2316, kata and theos. Not an adjective. A prepositional phrase: sorrow according to God. The KJV translators knew it, and in the margin of the verse before they printed the alternative themselves — or, according to God. So the qualifier is not a description of the sorrow's temperature. It is a statement about its standard. Sorrow measured by God, aimed at God, reckoned by what God thinks of the thing you did.

The other column gets its qualifier the same way: sorrow of the world. Same grammar, different standard. A man measuring his sin by what it cost him, what people now know, what he lost. Both men are grieving. They are grieving by different rulers.

The words that appear on both sides

  • λύπη lypē · "sorrow" (twice) · Strong's G3077

    Grief, pain of heart, the ache that sits in a man after a wound. The KJV renders it sorrow twice in one line, and that repetition is the whole argument. The noun itself is not holy. It has no direction of its own. The same word can work repentance unto salvation or work death, and nothing in the word decides which. What decides is whose sorrow it is. A man can cry and still be lost. A man can hurt badly and still refuse to turn.

  • κατεργάζομαι katergazomai · "worketh" (twice) · Strong's G2716

    To accomplish, to produce, to work a thing all the way through to completion — the kata prefix adding thoroughness to plain ergazomai, labour. It is a manufacturing word. Paul is not saying the two sorrows tend in different directions or generally correlate with different outcomes. He is saying each one makes something, and carries it through. And he uses the same verb again one verse later, when he lists what godly sorrow actually manufactured in the Corinthians. The machine has a visible output. That is how you tell which machine you are running.

  • κατά kata · "godly," with theos · Strong's G2596

    A preposition of alignment: according to, in conformity with, measured by the standard of. It is the same word that runs Paul's great oppositions — according to the flesh against according to the Spirit, according to the Scriptures. It never names a feeling. It names a rule the thing is being lined up against. Put it in front of theos and you get the only definition of godly sorrow the verse actually gives you: sorrow that has taken God as its measure.

What the first sorrow produces

  • μετάνοια metanoia · "repentance" · Strong's G3341

    A change of mind that leads to a change of life. The word is built from meta and nous — the mind, afterwards; the mind, changed. Note carefully what Paul does not say. He does not say godly sorrow is repentance. He says godly sorrow works repentance. The sorrow is upstream. It is a cause, not the thing itself, and a man who stops at the sorrow and calls it repentance has stopped one step short of the only step that mattered.

  • σωτηρία sōtēria · "salvation" · Strong's G4991

    The New Testament's primary word for salvation — rescue from danger, preservation, and theologically the whole of God's work delivering a man from sin, death and judgment. It is the far end of the chain, and it is worth noticing how long the chain is: sorrow, then repentance, then salvation. Paul does not collapse them. He is not saying your grief saves you. He is tracing a line from a wound in the conscience out to a destination, and God is the one who laid the line.

  • ἀμεταμέλητος ametamelētos · "not to be repented of" · Strong's G278

    An adjective built by putting a negative a- in front of metamelomai, to regret, to have second thoughts. Irrevocable. Unregretted. Not taken back. It appears only twice in the New Testament — here, and in Romans 11:29, where Paul says God's gifts and calling are irrevocable. The same word describes God's commitment and this repentance, which tells you something about the kind of turn Paul has in view: not a decision you will be revisiting.

    And there is a hook two verses back. In verse 8 Paul says of his own severe letter, I do not repent, though I did repent — and the verb there is metamelomai, exactly the word this adjective negates. Paul had second thoughts about wounding them. He got over them, because of what the wound produced. Then he says the repentance it produced in them is a-metamelētos — the kind nobody gets second thoughts about. He has just told you his own regret ran out. Theirs never will.

What the second sorrow produces

  • κόσμος kosmos · "of the world" · Strong's G2889

    The world as an ordered system — the present arrangement of things running by its own logic and against God. The word originally meant order or adornment, and that is exactly the trouble: the world's sorrow is orderly, respectable, entirely appropriate by its own standard. It grieves over consequence. It grieves over exposure. It grieves over a career, a name, a marriage, a number. Every one of those griefs is reasonable. None of them is kata theon, and reasonableness is not the test.

  • θάνατος thanatos · "death" · Strong's G2288

    Death — physical, spiritual, eternal; in Scripture always an intruder, the wages of sin and the last enemy. Set it against sōtēria in the other column and the verse's stakes come clear. Paul is not warning that worldly sorrow is unproductive. He says it works too. It has an output, it carries the work through to completion, and the finished product is death. That is the sentence's sting. The man who only feels bad has not done nothing. He has run the other machine.

2. Where It Sits

2 Corinthians 7:9 — I now rejoice, not that you were made sorry, but that you were made sorry to repentance. For you were made sorry in a godly way, that you might suffer loss by us in nothing.

2 Corinthians 7:11 — For behold, this same thing, that you were made sorry in a godly way, what earnest care it worked in you. Yes, what defense, indignation, fear, longing, zeal, and vengeance! In everything you demonstrated yourselves to be pure in the matter.

This verse is not a devotional aphorism dropped into a letter. It is the theological floor under a very specific, very painful piece of church business, and it will not read right until you know what happened.

Paul had planted the Corinthian church, and the relationship had gone badly wrong. There had been a visit that hurt everybody. Then there had been a letter — the one he describes in chapter 2 as written out of much affliction and anguish of heart, with many tears, not to make them sorry but so they would know how much he loved them. He sent it and then had to wait. Chapter 7 tells you how the waiting went: he got to Macedonia and had no rest, fightings were outside, fear was inside. He was a man who had said a hard thing to people he loved and did not yet know whether he had lost them.

Titus came back with the news, and the news was good. So Paul writes verse 8 with the honesty of a man who has been through it: though I made you sorry with my letter, I do not regret it, though I did regret it. He is admitting, in writing, that he second-guessed himself. Then verse 9 draws the line he wants drawn — I rejoice, not that you were made sorry, but that you were made sorry to repentance. He takes no pleasure in having hurt them. He takes enormous pleasure in what the hurt produced.

Verse 10 is the because under that. The Greek opens with gar — the small conjunction that hangs a reason beneath a claim. Why can Paul be glad about grief he caused? Because grief is not one thing. There is a grief that manufactures a turn toward God, and there is a grief that manufactures a corpse, and he had aimed a letter at the first one and hit it.

Then verse 11 is the receipt. Paul does not leave "godly sorrow" as an atmosphere. He itemises what it worked in them: earnest care, defence, indignation, fear, longing, zeal, vengeance — seven fruits, every one of them an action, and not one of them a feeling about the past. The whole list points forward. That is the diagnostic the verse hands you. You cannot tell which sorrow you have by how bad it feels at midnight. You tell by what is on the receipt in the morning.

Widen the frame one more step. Paul is writing to a church he will warn, five chapters later, that he fears finding people who have sinned before now, and not repented. The letter is soaked in the difference between a church that gets sad about its sin and a church that deals with it. And when the offender in question has been disciplined, chapter 2 has Paul turning around to insist they forgive and comfort him, lest by any means such a one should be swallowed up with his excessive sorrow. That is not a softening. It is the same doctrine from the other side: sorrow that is left to run without the turn to God does not sanctify a man. It swallows him.

Three men who were sorry

Scripture gives the test faces. Saul, caught by Samuel, says the right sentence — I have sinned — and then, in the same breath, asks for the thing he actually wants: yet please honor me now before the elders of my people. He was not grieving the offence. He was managing the optics of it. Judas felt remorse and gave the money back and said out loud that he had betrayed innocent blood — and the word Matthew uses for his remorse is metamelomai, the very verb Paul's ametamelētos negates. He got as far as agony and no further, and then he went out and hanged himself. Peter denied Christ three times, met His eyes across a courtyard, and went out, and wept bitterly — and was back with the others by morning, and fed the sheep for the rest of his life.

Three men, three genuine miseries. The volume was not the variable.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin devotes an entire chapter of the Institutes to repentance, and this verse is one of the load-bearing texts in it. He is worth reading here for two reasons: he refuses to let repentance shrink into a mood, and he refuses to let it swell into a work.

Calvin — on what repentance actually is

Before he touches our verse he insists on a definition, because he has watched the word get used to mean everything from a ritual to a bad afternoon:

Wherefore, it seems to me, that repentance may be not inappropriately defined thus: A real conversion of our life unto God, proceeding from sincere and serious fear of God; and consisting in the mortification of our flesh and the old man, and the quickening of the Spirit.

— Calvin, Institutes III.3

Every clause of that is doing work. A real conversion of our life — not of the feelings, of the life. Proceeding from the fear of God — which is where 2 Corinthians 7:10 comes in. And consisting in two things, a killing and a raising, neither of which is an emotion.

Calvin — on this verse in particular

When he gets to the second part of his definition — that repentance proceeds from a sincere fear of God — he reaches straight for Paul's sentence and tells you exactly how he reads it:

As repentance begins with dread and hatred of sin, the Apostle sets down godly sorrow as one of its causes (2 Cor. 7:10). By godly sorrow he means when we not only tremble at the punishment, but hate and abhor the sin, because we know it is displeasing to God.

— Calvin, Institutes III.3

There is the line, and it is a cleaner test than most of what gets preached off this verse. Not only tremble at the punishment. Trembling at punishment is not condemned — Calvin has just spent a paragraph arguing that a man must be aroused by the thought of divine judgment before his mind will turn at all. Fear of consequence is where it starts. But if it also stops there, the sorrow has been measured by the world's ruler and not by God's. What makes it kata theon is the second half: that the sin itself has become hateful, on the ground that God hates it.

He does not pretend this is gentle. In the same passage he says plainly that unless we are stung to the quick, the sluggishness of our carnal nature cannot be corrected — and that there is in us a rebellious spirit which must be broken as with hammers. Calvin is not a man who thinks a light tap will do.

Calvin — on two sorrows and two ends

He also records the old distinction between two kinds of repentance, and although he goes on to argue that the term must be more carefully taken than the schoolmen took it, he grants that the distinction itself is true. The two portraits map onto Paul's two columns exactly. The first:

Legal repentance; or that by which the sinner, stung with a sense of his sin, and overwhelmed with fear of the divine anger, remains in that state of perturbation, unable to escape from it.

— Calvin, Institutes III.3

Remains in that state. That is the sorrow of the world with its mechanism exposed: real conviction, real dread, and no exit. And the second:

The other they term Evangelical repentance; or that by which the sinner, though grievously downcast in himself, yet looks up and sees in Christ the cure of his wound, the solace of his terror; the haven of rest from his misery.

— Calvin, Institutes III.3

Grievously downcast in himself, yet looks up. Note that the downcast part is identical. The difference is a direction of the eyes, and the difference is everything.

Calvin — on the receipt in verse 11

He then walks through Paul's seven items one at a time, and his comment on the first sets the pattern:

He says then that godly sorrow produces carefulness. He who is really dissatisfied with himself for sinning against his God, is, at the same time, stimulated to care and attention, that he may completely disentangle himself from the chains of the devil.

— Calvin, Institutes III.3

At the same time. Not eventually, not once the feeling has been processed. Calvin's point is that real dissatisfaction with yourself is inseparable from doing something about the entanglement. A grief that leaves the chains exactly where they were has told you what kind of grief it is.

Calvin — on not letting it run away with you

And then, at the end of the same section, a guardrail that most preaching on this verse leaves off, and men are wrecked for the lack of it:

Let us remember, however, that moderation must be used, so that we may not be overwhelmed with sadness, there being nothing to which trembling consciences are more prone than to rush into despair.

— Calvin, Institutes III.3

He names it as one of Satan's tactics: a man he sees overwhelmed with fear he plunges deeper into the abyss of sorrow, so that he may never rise again. Which means a tender conscience grinding itself down for the ninth month over a sin already confessed is not being more serious about 2 Corinthians 7:10 than his cheerful neighbour. He is being worked on from the other flank. Godly sorrow ends in a turn and a Saviour. Sorrow that will not end has become, functionally, the world's kind — no matter how religious it sounds — because it is producing nothing but a slow death.

4. Confessional Anchor

The 1689 gives repentance a chapter of its own, and its title is nearly a paraphrase of our verse: Of Repentance Unto Life and Salvation. The third paragraph is the definition, and it cites the next verse of this very passage among its proofs.

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 15, Paragraph 3

"This saving repentance is an evangelical grace, whereby a person being by the Holy Spirit made sensible of the manifold evils of his sin, does, by faith in Christ, humble himself for it with godly sorrow, detestation of it, and self-abhorrence, praying for pardon and strength of grace, with a purpose and endeavor by supplies of the Spirit, to walk before God unto all well-pleasing in all things."

Read Chapter 15, Of Repentance Unto Life and Salvation →

Take the order seriously, because it settles the two questions men actually have about this verse.

Where does godly sorrow come from? The confession says it is an evangelical grace, and that the man is made sensible of his sin by the Holy Spirit. It is not something you generate by staring hard at your failures. That is why the harder a man tries to feel sufficiently bad, the more he finds he is performing. You cannot manufacture the upstream cause. You can ask for it.

Where does it sit relative to faith? The confession says he humbles himself by faith in Christ — and the divines put the chapter on saving faith immediately before this one. So the sorrow is not the entry fee paid at the door before Christ will look at you. It is the grief of a man who is already looking at Christ, and who therefore can afford to see the sin whole. Calvin makes the same point in his own way: repentance and faith, though constantly linked together, are to be united and not confounded.

Then the confession lists what the grief produces, and it is the same shape as verse 11 — detestation, self-abhorrence, prayer for pardon and strength of grace, and a settled purpose to walk before God. Note the last item. It is future tense. It is the receipt.

Baptist Catechism · Question 92

Q. What is repentance to life?
A. "Repentance to life is a saving grace, by which a sinner, out of a true sense of his sin and an understanding of the mercy of God in Christ, turns from his sin to God, with grief and hatred of his sin, and with full purpose of and effort after new obedience."

Work through the Catechism →

Read that answer as a sequence and you have the verse in miniature. A true sense of his sin — that is the sorrow. And an understanding of the mercy of God in Christ — that is the thing the sorrow of the world does not have, and its absence is exactly why the world's grief has nowhere to go. Then turns: the hinge, the actual repentance, the thing the sorrow works. Then grief and hatred of sin, and then effort after new obedience. Both halves are there — the pain and the turn — and the turn is what the word repentance names.

One more paragraph from chapter 15 belongs on this page, because it is the reason a man can afford to look squarely at what he has done:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 15, Paragraph 5

"…although there is no sin so small but it deserves damnation, yet there is no sin so great that it shall bring damnation on them that repent; which makes the constant preaching of repentance necessary."

Read Chapter 15 →

And the paragraph above it settles the timeline: repentance is to be continued through the whole course of our lives, and it is every man's duty to repent of his particular known sins particularly. Not sin in general. Particularly. By name.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the PROVEN Men memorize pack — carry it. Carry it because of when it comes due. Most memorized Scripture arrives in a quiet moment. This one arrives about twenty minutes after you have been caught, or have caught yourself, and it arrives asking a question you would rather not be asked while your face is still hot.

The question is not whether you feel bad. It is what your grief is measuring. Sit with the actual content of the misery. If you take it apart honestly, most of a man's post-sin wretchedness is composed of consequence: that someone found out, that his wife's face changed, that the pattern he thought he had beaten is clearly not beaten, that he is not the man he had been telling himself he was. Every one of those is a real loss and none of them is God. Worldly sorrow is not the sorrow of a callous man. It is the sorrow of a man grieving his own diminishment. It can be very intense and completely self-enclosed, which is precisely why it produces nothing.

Give it three days, then look at the output. This is the honest test, and it is the one Paul actually supplies. Feelings are not evidence; verse 11 is a list of actions. So: what got confessed to someone who can hold you to it? What got blocked, cancelled, thrown out, or handed over? What conversation got had that you had been avoiding? What did you make right with the person you actually wronged? If the answer three days on is nothing, but I really did feel terrible about it, you have your reading. That is not a verdict on your soul. It is a diagnosis on that particular grief, and there is a remedy.

Beware the counterfeit that runs on repetition. There is a loop that feels like the most serious repentance a man has ever done: fall, agonise, promise, resolve, and fall again on the same day of the next week. Ten years of it. The agony is real every time. Nothing changes, ever, and the sincerity of the agony is exactly what keeps the man from noticing. Paul's verb settles it — godly sorrow manufactures. If the machine has run four hundred times and produced no product, it is not the good machine, however loud it is.

And beware the other ditch. Calvin saw it coming and named it: a trembling conscience rushing into despair, and an enemy who is delighted to help. If you are still grinding on a sin you confessed and forsook two years ago, you are not being rigorous. You are being farmed. The confession's answer is flat — no sin so great that it shall bring damnation on them that repent. Godly sorrow has a terminus, and the terminus is Christ. A grief that will not arrive there is not a deeper version of this verse; it is the second column with religious vocabulary.

What it looks like on a Tuesday. You repent of particular known sins particularly, by name, out loud, without the softening nouns. You tell one man who will ask you again on Friday. You do the concrete thing that costs something — the device handed over, the account closed, the number deleted, the apology made without a single clause explaining why they provoked you. And then you get up and go to work, because the turn is forward and the receipt is written in the days after, not in the intensity of the hour.

Under all of it, this. Paul's word for that repentance is ametamelētos — the word he elsewhere uses of God's own gifts and calling, which are never taken back. You will regret the sin for the rest of your life. You will never once regret the turning. There is no man in Scripture, and none in your church, who got to the end and wished he had repented less.
