Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:1

Before a single word of the sermon, Matthew records four things Jesus did. Every one of them is part of the sermon.

Seeing the multitudes, he went up onto the mountain. When he had sat down, his disciples came to him.— World English Bible (public domain)

And seeing the multitudes, he went up into a mountain: and when he was set, his disciples came unto him:— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

There is no doctrine in this verse. There is a look, a climb, a seat, and an approach — and then verse 2 opens his mouth and the most quoted sermon in the history of the world begins. The temptation is to skim the stage direction to get to the Beatitudes. Resist it. Matthew was a careful writer, and he spent a whole verse on four movements before he let anyone speak.

Matthew 5:2 — He opened his mouth and taught them, saying,

Notice the order. He sees, and then he goes up, and then he sits, and only then do the disciples close the distance. Nothing here is reactive. No one asked him for a sermon.

  • εἴδω eidō · “seeing” · Strong’s G1492

    A primary verb that lives in two tenses with two meanings: the aorist eidon, “I saw,” and the perfect oida, “I know.” Greek runs seeing and knowing together on the same root — what you have seen, you know. Distinguished from ginōskō, the verb for knowledge gathered by experience, this one carries settled knowledge. So the sermon does not begin with a request. It begins with his looking at people, and with the kind of looking that already understands what it is looking at.

  • ἀναβαίνω anabainō · “he went up” · Strong’s G305

    A compound of ana (up) and bainō (to go): to ascend, climb, go up. It is the plain word for walking uphill — the word for going up to Jerusalem, for boarding a ship, for climbing a hill with your own legs. It is also the word the New Testament will use for the Ascension, and for prayers going up to God. Here it is a footpath and nothing more. But Matthew is writing to people who have read Exodus, and he knows exactly what a man going up a mountain sounds like.

  • εἰς eis · “into” / “onto” · Strong’s G1519

    One of the commonest prepositions in the Greek New Testament, and always directional — motion, purpose, result, pointed at something. It is distinguished from en, which means being in a place. That is why the King James can write “into a mountain” and the World English Bible “onto the mountain” without either being wrong. The word is not describing the geography. It is describing a man going somewhere on purpose.

  • μαθητής mathētēs · “disciples” · Strong’s G3101

    From manthanō, “to learn.” In that world a mathētēs was not a student who attended lectures; he was a committed follower who lived with a teacher, imitated his practices, and set out to embody his wisdom. The rabbis’ talmidim followed their rabbi everywhere. It matters that Matthew names them by the word for learner at the exact moment the teaching starts. One chapter earlier the call was not to a curriculum but to a person — “Follow me.” This is what following turned out to mean: sitting down in front of him and being taught.

Two more words in the line are doing heavy work, and both can be read straight off the page without a definition. The first is the one behind “the multitudes.” It is a single ordinary word for a crowd, and it brackets the entire sermon: the great multitudes of Matthew 4:25 are the reason he climbs, and the astonished multitudes of Matthew 7:28 are still there when he stops. He did not go up the hill to get away from them.

The second is the one behind “when he was set.” Matthew tells us what it means by using it twice in the same shape:

Matthew 15:29 — Jesus departed there, and came near to the sea of Galilee; and he went up into the mountain, and sat there.

Went up, and sat. The same two motions in the same order, and both times the teaching follows. The seat is not a rest stop; it is the posture Matthew gives him when he is about to speak with weight. The King James phrase catches it better than anything modern: when he was set. Not settled in. Installed.

2. Where It Sits

Chapter 4 ends at a run. Jesus has begun to preach — “Repent! For the Kingdom of Heaven is at hand” — called fishermen off their boats, and covered the whole region:

Matthew 4:23 — Jesus went about in all Galilee, teaching in their synagogues, preaching the Good News of the Kingdom, and healing every disease and every sickness among the people.

Matthew 4:25 — Great multitudes from Galilee, Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea and from beyond the Jordan followed him.

That is a summary written from a distance: teaching, preaching, healing, whole regions, enormous crowds. Then Matthew stops summarizing. Chapter 5 verse 1 slows the film down to a single afternoon, and for three chapters we are allowed to hear what all that teaching actually sounded like.

This is the first of five great blocks of teaching in Matthew, and he marks the end of each one with the same sentence, five times, so you cannot miss the architecture:

  • Matthew 7:28

    When Jesus had finished saying these things, the multitudes were astonished at his teaching,

  • Matthew 11:1

    When Jesus had finished directing his twelve disciples, he departed from there to teach and preach in their cities.

  • Matthew 13:53

    When Jesus had finished these parables, he departed from there.

  • Matthew 19:1

    When Jesus had finished these words, he departed from Galilee, and came into the borders of Judea beyond the Jordan.

  • Matthew 26:1

    When Jesus had finished all these words, he said to his disciples,

Matthew 5:1 is the opening bracket of the first block. And the closing bracket tells you how it landed:

Matthew 7:29 — for he taught them with authority, and not like the scribes.

Matthew 8:1 — When he came down from the mountain, great multitudes followed him.

Crowds at the bottom of the hill going up, crowds at the bottom of the hill coming down. The sermon is not a private retreat for insiders. He climbed because of the multitudes, taught the men who came close, and walked straight back down into the same crowd he had been looking at in verse 1.

The mountain

A Jewish reader hearing “he went up into a mountain” and then a body of law about murder, adultery, oaths, and enemies is not going to be able to help himself. There is only one other scene in his Bible shaped like that one:

  • Exodus 19:3

    Moses went up to God, and the LORD called to him out of the mountain, saying, “This is what you shall tell the house of Jacob, and tell the children of Israel:”

  • Deuteronomy 9:9

    When I had gone up onto the mountain to receive the stone tablets, even the tablets of the covenant which the LORD made with you, then I stayed on the mountain forty days and forty nights. I neither ate bread nor drank water.

Matthew wants the echo. But look at what he does with it, because the difference is the whole point. Moses went up the mountain to receive. He came down carrying something that had been handed to him. Jesus goes up and sits down and speaks, and nobody hands him anything. He is not the mediator climbing to collect the law. He is the one whose voice it was in the first place.

Which raises the obvious question, and the sermon answers it before anyone can ask twice:

Matthew 5:17 — “Don’t think that I came to destroy the law or the prophets. I didn’t come to destroy, but to fulfill.”

So the mountain is not a second Sinai issuing a replacement code. It is the same Lawgiver, in flesh, saying what he meant the first time.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin — on whether this is a new law

Calvin knew the mistake this scene invites, and he names it directly: the idea that Christ is improving on Moses, that the gospel law is a higher edition of an older and cruder one. He calls it pernicious, and says it insults the Law of God to think so. His reading of what is actually happening on this mountain is one sentence long:

In saying that this is the meaning of the Law, we are not introducing a new interpretation of our own; we are following Christ, the best interpreter of the Law

Calvin, Institutes II.8 · Read the chapter →

The best interpreter. Not the second author. And he presses it:

It is very easy, however, to confute this error, which proceeds on the supposition that Christ added to the Law, whereas he only restored it to its integrity by maintaining and purifying it when obscured by the falsehood, and defiled by the leaven of the Pharisees.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 · Read the chapter →

That is the right frame for everything that follows verse 1. When he says an angry word is murder and a look is adultery, he is not raising the standard. He is scraping the varnish off the standard that was always there. The Pharisees had reduced the Law to a list of external acts a careful man could avoid. He sits down on a hill and gives it back its depth.

Calvin — on why he gets to sit in that chair

Behind the seat on the mountain is the prophetic office. Calvin treats it not as a title but as the reason a Christian is allowed to stop looking for further revelation: the teaching Christ delivered closed the account.

The voice which thundered from heaven, “This is my beloved Son, hear him” gave him a special privilege above all other teachers.

Calvin, Institutes II.15 · Read the chapter →

Matthew will stage that voice himself, twelve chapters later, on another mountain — “This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Listen to him” (Matthew 17:5). The command from the cloud is not admire him. It is hear him, which is precisely the posture of the men in verse 1 who came and sat down. Calvin draws the conclusion:

The purpose of this prophetical dignity in Christ is to teach us, that in the doctrine which he delivered is substantially included a wisdom which is perfect in all its parts.

Calvin, Institutes II.15 · Read the chapter →

Perfect in all its parts. There is nothing to add to the sermon on the mountain, and there is no supplement to go looking for.

Calvin — on being content with one master

And that has a practical edge, which Calvin puts in the middle of an argument about men who prefer speculation to obedience:

But if we decline not to be the disciples of Christ, let us not decline to follow the method which he has prescribed. In this way, being contented with him for our master, we will not only refrain from, but even feel averse to, superfluous speculations which he discourages.

Calvin, Institutes I.14 · Read the chapter →

That is the word in verse 1 turned into a rule of life. If you are a learner, you take the syllabus you were given. Curiosity that runs past what he taught is not advanced discipleship; it is a man leaving the seat he came to sit in.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession explains why there had to be a mountain at all. Christ holds three offices, and the reason he holds the first one is not flattering to us:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 8, Paragraph 10

This number and order of offices is necessary; for in respect of our ignorance, we stand in need of his prophetical office

1689 LBCF, ch. 8 · Read Chapter 8 →

In respect of our ignorance. The crowds in Matthew 4 got healing because they were sick. The disciples in Matthew 5 get teaching because they are ignorant, and so are you, and no amount of sincerity fixes that. A man does not talk himself into knowing the will of God. He has to be told.

The Baptist Catechism · Question 27

Christ executes the office of a prophet in revealing to us, by his Word and Spirit, the will of God for our salvation.

The Baptist Catechism, Q27 · Work through the Catechism →

Revealing. Not suggesting, not modelling, not inspiring — revealing something that was not otherwise available. Matthew 5:1 is that office going to work in a specific field on a specific afternoon, with an audience that had walked a long way to be there.

And because the sermon is about to spend three chapters on the commandments, the confession puts the guardrail in place beforehand:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 19, Paragraph 5

The moral law forever binds all, as well justified persons as others, to the obedience thereof, and that not only in regard to the matter contained in it, but also in respect of the authority of God the Creator, who gave it. Neither does Christ in the gospel in any way dissolve this obligation, but much strengthen it.

1689 LBCF, ch. 19 · Read Chapter 19 →

The confession cites Matthew 5:17–19 for that paragraph, which is to say it cites this sermon. Not dissolve, but much strengthen. That is the correct expectation to bring up the hill with you. Nobody who sat down in front of him that day walked away with less to do.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse opens the Sermon on the Mount pack on the memorize page — a passage pack, Matthew 5 start to finish, a chunk at a time. Carry it. And notice that the pack begins here, with the stage direction, rather than at “Blessed are the poor in spirit.” That is the right place to start, because it makes you memorize the approach before the content. You learn where you are standing before you are told anything.

He saw first. The sermon does not begin with a man asking a question. It begins with Christ looking at a crowd and deciding to teach. Whatever brought you to this text — a hard week, a habit, an argument you lost — you are not the initiator here. He was already looking. That is not sentimentality; it is the plain word order of the verse.

The teaching goes to the ones who close the distance. Everybody on that hillside could hear him; chapter 7 says the multitudes were astonished. But the men addressed are the ones who came to him, which took a walk uphill and a decision to sit down where a learner sits. There is no version of this where you receive the Sermon on the Mount at a comfortable distance. The distance is the thing that has to go.

Sitting down is a posture, and you can tell when you have quit it. The word Matthew uses for those men means learner. A learner takes correction, does not negotiate the syllabus, and does not stop the teacher to explain what he probably meant. Test yourself on that this week — not on whether you agree with the Sermon on the Mount, but on whether you have ever once let it tell you something you did not already believe.

He came back down. Matthew 8:1: when he came down from the mountain, great multitudes followed him. The teaching was not delivered on a summit for men who intended to stay there. It was carried back into the crowd within a chapter, and the first thing that happens at the bottom of the hill is a leper getting touched. Whatever you take off this mountain is meant to be spent down in the noise — on a Tuesday, at a job, in a house with people in it who did not hear the sermon.

And under all of it, the comfort in the confession’s cold word ignorance. You are not expected to have worked this out. The reason there is a prophetic office at all is that men like you and me cannot see the will of God from where we are standing, and God did not leave it there. He went up a hill, sat down, and told us.
