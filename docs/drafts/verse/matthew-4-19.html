Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 4:19

Two clauses. One of them is a command, and it is not the one about fishing.

He said to them, “Come after me, and I will make you fishers for men.”

— World English Bible (public domain)

And he saith unto them, Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men.

— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Count the verbs and note who owns them. There are two. The first is addressed to the men on the beach and it asks one thing of them: come. The second belongs entirely to Jesus and it is set in the future — I will make. Everything the men are told to do is to move; everything that has to change about them is his to accomplish. Read it that way round and the verse stops being a recruiting slogan and starts being a promise with a command bolted to the front of it.

This matters because the sentence is almost always heard backwards. Men take “fishers of men” as the assignment and “follow me” as the preamble. The Greek will not carry that. The imperative is come. The output is his work. A man who cannot tell those two apart will spend his life either paralysed by a job he was never given or proud of a result he did not produce.

  • δεῦτε deute · “follow” / “come” · Strong's G1205 · adverb / interjection

    Our lexicon calls it an urgent invitation — “Come!” or “Come here!” — addressed to more than one person, and notes that it is the vocabulary of divine invitation and divine commissioning. The King James renders the whole phrase “Follow me,” which is fair to the sense and hides the shape. There is no verb to follow in the clause. There is a summons: come, plural, to two brothers at once. And it is the same word Jesus uses in Matthew 11:28 to call the weary to rest, and the word the angel uses at the tomb — come and see the place where he lay. One summons, three errands: rest, resurrection, enlistment. The man who answers this one is answering the voice that issues all three.

  • ὀπίσω opisō · “after me” · Strong's G3694 · adverb / preposition

    Behind, after, following — position or direction in the wake of someone. Put with the summons it is deute opisō mou: come after me. The lexicon puts the geometry bluntly: the disciple's position is always opisō — not beside, not in front, but following. That is worth sitting with, because it is the same word Jesus turns on Peter twelve chapters later when Peter starts advising him about the cross: get behind me. Peter did not stop being a disciple in that moment. He stepped out of position, and the correction was to send him back to the only place a disciple stands. The call in Matthew 4:19 does not offer a partnership. It assigns a place in a line of march.

  • ποιέω poieō · “I will make” · Strong's G4160 · verb

    To do, to make, to create, to produce, to perform — the general verb for action and making, and the word behind our poet, which simply meant a maker. It is the verb used of God making the world and everything in it, and the noun from the same root is the one Paul uses when he says believers are God's handiwork, created in Christ Jesus. Here it is first person and future. Jesus does not say these men will grow into fishers of men, or that he will show them how. He says he will make them that. The same verb that stands behind creation stands behind the remaking of a fisherman, and in both cases the maker is not the material.

  • ἁλιεύς halieus · “fishers” · Strong's G231 · noun, masculine

    A fisherman. Plural, halieis. The previous verse has already used the word of these men as a plain statement of trade — Simon and Andrew were casting a net, for they were halieis. Jesus picks up their own job title and hands it back to them with a different object: halieis anthrōpōn. Our lexicon notes what that says about the founding of the church — these were blue-collar workers, not scribal elite, and the gospel begins at the waterfront, not the academy. He did not take their vocabulary away and issue them a religious one. He took the word they already answered to and enlarged what it caught.

  • ἄνθρωπος anthrōpos · “of men” · Strong's G444 · noun, masculine

    A human being, mankind generically — not specifically male, which is anēr. The distinction is not pedantry. “Fishers of men” in every English version that keeps the old phrase does not mean fishers of males; the word is the one behind the title Son of Man, and it means the human race. So the catch named here is as wide as humanity: the men in the next boat, the women on the shore, the children in the house, the tax collector nobody would sit with. Whatever narrowing a man does later, the verse did not authorise it.

One more thing about the metaphor, since it is the part everybody remembers. Fishing, done properly, kills the fish. The image has an edge, and Scripture uses that edge on purpose elsewhere — Jeremiah has the LORD sending for many fishers who will fish out a rebel nation for judgment, and Matthew himself will later compare the kingdom to a dragnet that hauls in every kind and ends with a sorting nobody enjoys. So the picture is not automatically warm. What keeps it from being grim is a single word Luke preserves from the same scene, and we will get to it.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 4:12 — Now when Jesus heard that John was delivered up, he withdrew into Galilee.

Matthew 4:16 — the people who sat in darkness saw a great light, to those who sat in the region and shadow of death, to them light has dawned.

Matthew 4:17 — From that time, Jesus began to preach, and to say, “Repent! For the Kingdom of Heaven is at hand.”

Matthew 4:18 — Walking by the sea of Galilee, he saw two brothers: Simon, who is called Peter, and Andrew, his brother, casting a net into the sea; for they were fishermen.

Matthew 4:20 — They immediately left their nets and followed him.

Matthew 4:22 — They immediately left the boat and their father, and followed him.

The sequence is tight and it is not decorative. John the Baptist has just been arrested. That is the trigger: the forerunner is off the board, and Jesus withdraws to Galilee — not into hiding, but into the district Isaiah had named centuries earlier as the dark one, Galilee of the Gentiles, the land of Zebulun and Naphtali. Matthew tells us plainly why he went there: so that the prophet's word about a light dawning on people sitting in the shadow of death would be fulfilled. The first thing that light does is preach one sentence. Repent, because the kingdom of heaven has arrived. The second thing it does is walk down to the water and call two working men.

So the call in verse 19 is not a biographical detail about how Jesus met Peter. It is the second act of the kingdom's arrival. Announce, then recruit. And what he recruits them for is spelled out four verses later, when he goes through all Galilee teaching, preaching the good news of the kingdom, and healing every disease. That is the trade they were apprenticed into, and they learned it the way a trade is always learned — by standing behind the man doing it.

Verses 20 and 22 report the response twice with the same word: immediately. And they report the cost with an escalation Matthew clearly intends. Simon and Andrew leave their nets — their tools, their income. James and John leave the boat and their father. The capital equipment goes first and then the family business goes, with Zebedee sitting in it. The verb Matthew uses for their response, akoloutheō (G190), is the ordinary word for following someone down a road, and the lexicon notes that in the Gospels it becomes the technical term for discipleship — committing your whole life direction, not merely walking behind. It demanded leaving nets, tax booths, and family expectations. Matthew has just shown you two of the three.

Then there is Luke's version of the same commission, and it carries the word that saves the metaphor. Luke has Jesus tell Simon, after the catch that nearly sank the boats, that from now on he will be catching people alive. That is one Greek verb, zōgreō (G2221) — to capture alive, to take alive. Our lexicon notes that it was originally a military term for taking prisoners rather than killing them, and that it appears only twice in the New Testament. The other place is 2 Timothy, where it describes men taken captive by the devil to do his will. Two captures, then, and the same verb for both: one that enslaves and one that rescues. What the word does to the fishing image is exact. The fisherman's trade is deadly — fish die when caught. This one is not. The net comes up and what is in it lives.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin never comments on this verse in the Institutes — his exposition of Matthew lives in a commentary that is not held on this site, so we will not put words in his mouth. But the question the verse raises he treats at length, and he treats it exactly where you would want him to: in the chapter on the ministers of the church. The question is why God bothers with men at all. He has the Spirit. He could convert the world without a single fisherman in the loop.

For though it is right that he alone should rule and reign in the Church, that he should preside and be conspicuous in it, and that its government should be exercised and administered solely by his word; yet as he does not dwell among us in visible presence, so as to declare his will to us by his own lips, he in this (as we have said) uses the ministry of men, by making them, as it were, his substitutes

Calvin, Institutes IV.3 — read the chapter

Substitutes. Not partners, not consultants, not volunteers whose help is appreciated. Men standing in for a Lord who rules by his word and has chosen not to speak it audibly out of the sky. That is a startling dignity to hand a man who smells like fish, and Calvin refuses to let it be treated as a small thing. Working through what Paul says about the ministry of the gospel in 2 Corinthians, he puts it as strongly as it can be put:

there is nothing in the Church more noble and glorious than the ministry of the Gospel, seeing it is the administration of the Spirit of righteousness and eternal life

Calvin, Institutes IV.3 — read the chapter

He is honest, though, about what kind of call the one on the beach was. Later in the same chapter, sorting out how ministers are chosen, he draws a line most men skip past. The apostles are not the template for ordinary appointment:

As theirs was an extraordinary ministry, in order to render it conspicuous by some more distinguished mark, those who were to discharge it behoved to be called and appointed by the mouth of the Lord himself. It was not, therefore, by any human election, but at the sole command of God and Christ, that they prepared themselves for the work.

Calvin, Institutes IV.3 — read the chapter

Take that seriously and it removes a fantasy. Nobody reading this was called by an audible voice on a shoreline, and a man who waits for one is waiting for something Calvin says was peculiar to the twelve. But notice that the line Calvin draws is about the mode of the call, not its author. On the agency he is immovable, and he says so in the chapter on election:

The special election that would otherwise remain hidden in God he finally makes plain by his calling.

Calvin, Institutes III.24 — read the chapter

This is also clearly shown by the very nature of calling and the way it is administered, for it consists not merely in the preaching of the Word but also in the illumination of the Spirit.

Calvin, Institutes III.24 — read the chapter

Put the two halves together and the doctrine is plain. Simon heard a man's voice and dropped a net; you heard a sermon, or a friend at a jobsite, or a page. Different mode, same hand on the tiller, because a call that arrives through the preaching of the Word is not thereby a merely human one — the Spirit's illumination is part of how the call is administered. Which is also, precisely, why a man out fishing for men is not wasting his breath. He is holding the outward end of an instrument whose inward end is not in his hands at all.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession states the promise half of Matthew 4:19 as a doctrine, and it uses the same grammar Jesus used: God is the subject of every verb that changes a man.

Those whom God has predestined to life he is pleased, in his appointed and accepted time, effectually to call by his Word and Spirit out of that state of sin and death in which they are by nature, to grace and salvation by Jesus Christ. He enlightens their minds spiritually and savingly to understand the things of God; he takes away their heart of stone and gives them a heart of flesh; he renews their wills, and by his almighty power determines them to that which is good and effectually draws them to Jesus Christ — yet so that they come most freely, being made willing by his grace.

1689 LBCF, ch. 10, para. 1 — Of Effectual Calling

He enlightens, he takes away, he gives, he renews, he determines, he draws — and then the clause that keeps it from being coercion: yet so that they come most freely, being made willing by his grace. That is Matthew 4:20 in confessional prose. Nobody dragged Simon up the beach. He came immediately, and he came freely, and he came because he was made willing. The catechism compresses the same thing into one answer, and then tells you what the net is made of.

Q94. How is the Word made effectual to salvation?

The Spirit of God makes the reading, but especially the preaching, of the Word an effectual means of convincing and converting sinners, and of building them up in holiness and comfort through faith to salvation.

The Baptist Catechism, Q94

The instrument is the Word read and preached. The power is the Spirit. Take either away and there is no fishing: a man with the Spirit's power and no Word has nothing to say, and a man with the Word and no Spirit is casting a net into an empty sea. And lest any man think this is a licence for the ordained only, the confession closes that door itself:

Although it is incumbent on the bishops or pastors of the churches to be instant in preaching the Word by way of office, yet the work of preaching the Word is not so peculiarly confined to them but that others also, gifted and fitted by the Holy Spirit for it, and approved and called by the church, may and ought to perform it.

1689 LBCF, ch. 26, para. 11 — Of the Church

May and ought. The Baptists who wrote that were not building a loophole for freelancers — note the two conditions, gifted by the Spirit and approved by a church. But they would not say the trade belongs to the office alone. Christ made fishers of men out of four fishermen before there was an ordination in sight.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in The Wheel memorize pack — carry it. In that pack it is the Witness spoke: Christ the centre, obedience the rim, and four spokes of Word, Prayer, Fellowship, and Witness. A wheel with three spokes still turns for a while. It does not stay round.

Five things follow from the verse, and none of them are a pep talk.

The only order in the sentence is “come.” You were not commanded to produce converts. You were commanded to get behind him and stay there. Men who invert this end up in one of two ditches: crushed because nobody they talked to believed, or pleased with themselves because somebody did. Both ditches are the same mistake — taking credit for the clause that was never yours.

Your position is behind, and it is not a promotion track. Opisō means following in the wake. Not beside him as a colleague with independent standing, not out ahead of him deciding which waters are worth working. The moment a man starts telling Christ how the mission should be run, he is Peter in chapter sixteen, and the answer he gets is the one word that puts him back in line.

He works through your trade, not around it. He did not call scholars and make them fishermen. He called fishermen and made them fishers, and the shape of the old work carried into the new: patience, early hours, knowing the water, working with your brother, coming home some nights with nothing. Whatever your trade is, it is the water. The men on your crew are the catch. Nobody is going to relocate you to a more spiritual job first.

Nets get left, and sometimes so does the boat. This is the honest cost and Matthew does not soften it. Two men lost their tools; two lost their tools and walked away from their father in the middle of the workday. If following him has never yet cost you a thing you actually wanted — a promotion, a Saturday, a friendship you had to be honest in — it is worth asking whether you have answered the call or merely admired it.

Catch them alive. Luke's word settles the tone of the whole business. You are not collecting trophies, winning arguments, or landing anybody. A man can win every exchange at work and lose every man in the room, and by this verse's standard he has caught nothing. And the sorting is not yours either — the dragnet in Matthew 13 gets sorted by angels at the end of the age, not by fishermen on the beach. Your job is to get the net in the water and bring them up living.

Simon and Andrew had no training, no standing, and no idea what they were agreeing to. What they had was a voice telling them what they would become and a back to walk behind. That is still the whole arrangement. He does the making. You do the coming.
