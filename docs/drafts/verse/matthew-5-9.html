Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:9

Not the peaceful. Not the peaceable. Not the men who are easy to get along with. The men who make peace — and the next line in the sermon says they get hit for it.

Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called children of God.— World English Bible (public domain)

Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Read the line slowly and it stops being comfortable. Jesus does not bless the peaceful. He does not bless men of a calm temperament, or men who avoid a scene, or men nobody argues with. He blesses men who make something that was not there. Peace, in this sentence, is a product. Somebody has to go build it, and building it is work, and the work is done in a place where there is currently a fight.

That is the whole difference between a peacemaker and a peacekeeper. A peacekeeper manages a quiet that already exists, mostly by not touching anything. A peacemaker walks into a room where there is no peace and puts one there. One of those jobs is comfortable. The other one is the one Jesus blessed.

  • μακάριος makarios · “Blessed” · Strong’s G3107

    The Greek word for blessedness — not emotional happiness dependent on circumstances, but the deep well-being of those who are right with God. The Beatitudes open with makarios.

    Seventh of eight, and by now the word is a drumbeat. It is a verdict, not a feeling. Nobody on that hillside felt blessed; they were being told what they were, on God’s reckoning, in advance of anything they could see. That matters here more than in most of the eight, because peacemaking is the beatitude with the worst short-term returns. The man in the middle of two angry parties is generally hated by both. The verdict is handed down over him anyway.

  • εἰρηνοποιός eirenopoios · “peacemaker” · Strong’s G1518

    A compound of eirene (peace) and poieo (to make): one who actively makes peace. It appears exactly once in the New Testament — here.

    One occurrence in the entire New Testament. Not a stock piece of religious vocabulary that Jesus borrowed; a word He uses once, in a list of eight, and never again. So there is no other passage to soften it with. Whatever it means, it means it here.

    And the compound will not let you hide. The second half of the word is make — the plain, ordinary verb for producing a thing. It is not eirenikos, peaceable, describing a man’s disposition. It is not a word about how someone comes across. It names an activity with a result. A man is not a peacemaker because conflict makes him uncomfortable. He is a peacemaker because there is peace somewhere that would not exist if he had not gone and made it.

    The related verb shows up once in the New Testament as well, and where it lands is the whole doctrine: and through him to reconcile all things to himself, by him, whether things on the earth, or things in the heavens, having made peace through the blood of his cross. That is the archetype. God’s own peacemaking is not a mood, a negotiation, or a lowering of the standard. It is a settlement, and it is paid for in blood. Anyone who wants the title in verse 9 is applying for work in that trade.

  • ὅτι hoti · “for” · Strong’s G3754

    One of the most common Greek conjunctions. It either introduces the content of a statement (“that”) or gives the reason for it (“because,” “for,” “since”). Context decides which.

    Two words, load-bearing again. The blessing is not free-floating advice about getting along; hoti chains it to a stated ground. These men are blessed because of what will be said over them. Cut the conjunction and the beatitude becomes a proverb about being nice. Leave it in and it is an announcement about a name, made to men who do not yet have it in hand.

  • καλέω kaleō · “shall be called” · Strong’s G2564

    To call, to name, to summon — in the New Testament, characteristically a summons with divine authority behind it. Romans 8:30: those he called, he justified.

    Note who is doing the calling. The verb is passive and the subject is not named, which in this Gospel is nearly always a way of saying God without saying it out loud — the same construction that runs through the other seven lines, where the mourners shall be comforted and the merciful shall obtain mercy. Nobody is promising that the neighbours will speak well of you. The promise is that God will put a name on you.

    Which is exactly the language John reaches for: See how great a love the Father has given to us, that we should be called children of God! Same verb, same passive, same name. And John adds the part that keeps a man from expecting applause: for this cause the world doesn’t know us, because it didn’t know him. The naming is real and it is not public yet.

    The tense is future in both translations printed above. Not they are called. They shall be called — settled, but not settled today.

  • υἱός huios · “children” · Strong’s G5207

    Son — and in the New Testament, specifically a child with inheritance rights. John 1:12: the right to become children of God. Galatians 4:7: no longer a slave, but a son.

    Our translations say children, and that is fair English for a mixed crowd. But the word underneath is the word for a son, and it is the word carrying the estate. This is not a sentimental promise that God is fond of you. It is a legal one. A son is in the will.

    And it is the same word Jesus uses thirty-six verses later, in the passage that explains this one: love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat you and persecute you, that you may be children of your Father who is in heaven. For he makes his sun to rise on the evil and the good, and sends rain on the just and the unjust. There is the logic, stated in the open. You are not named a son because you earned a wage. You are named a son because you were caught doing what your Father does — extending good to people who have not got it coming. Peacemaking is family resemblance. The name follows the likeness.

  • θεός theos · “God” · Strong’s G2316

    The standard Greek word for God. In classical use it covered any deity; the New Testament transforms it through its Hebrew inheritance — theos is the one true God of Israel, Creator, Sustainer, and Redeemer, revealed definitively in Jesus Christ.

    Whose sons. Not sons of the peace, not sons of the movement, not respected men in a reconciled community. The genitive names the Father, and the Father is the God of Israel who made the world. That is the estate a peacemaker is being written into, and it is why the beatitude can afford to promise nothing at all in the meantime.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 5:8 — Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.

Matthew 5:10 — Blessed are those who have been persecuted for righteousness’ sake, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven.

Look at the neighbours before anything else, because the arrangement is doing an argument.

On one side, purity of heart. On the other, persecution. James puts those two in the same order and draws the line between them for us: the wisdom that is from above is first pure, then peaceful, gentle, reasonable, full of mercy and good fruits, without partiality, and without hypocrisy. First pure, then peaceful. That order is not decorative. A man who makes peace before he is honest is not making peace; he is arranging a truce over a lie, and the lie will come due. Every counterfeit peacemaker in a church, a marriage, or a shop is a man who took the second word without the first.

Then James finishes the thought in the very next line, and it is as close as the New Testament comes to a commentary on our verse: Now the fruit of righteousness is sown in peace by those who make peace. Sown — which is to say, it goes in the ground, and you do not get the harvest the same week.

On the other side stands verse 10, and this is the placement that ought to stop a man cold. Immediately after Jesus blesses the peacemakers, He blesses the persecuted. He does not put those two in tension; He puts them in sequence. The peacemaker is the man they come after. Anyone who thinks this beatitude is a promise of a quiet life has not read to the end of the sentence after it.

Why would that be? Because the Sermon on the Mount has already told us what real peace requires. Twelve verses on: if therefore you are offering your gift at the altar, and there remember that your brother has anything against you, leave your gift there before the altar, and go your way. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift. Notice the direction. It is not if you have something against him. It is if he has something against you — the grievance is on his end, you are the one holding the sacrifice, and you are the one told to walk out of the service and go fix it. Peacemaking in this sermon starts by moving toward the man who is angry, not by waiting for him to cool off.

Later Jesus makes the same move about a genuine offence: if your brother sins against you, go, show him his fault between you and him alone. Go. Alone. Say the thing. That is not conflict avoidance; it is conflict handled at the smallest possible size, which is what peacemaking actually looks like before it is a crisis. Most of what men call keeping the peace is simply refusing to have the small conversation, and then paying for the large one later.

And the hardest guardrail is Jesus’ own, five chapters on: Don’t think that I came to send peace on the earth. I didn’t come to send peace, but a sword. The same Christ who is called Prince of Peace in Isaiah says He came with a sword, and He says the fracture will run through a household. Both are true, and holding them together is the discipline this beatitude demands. Christ makes peace between God and man, and that peace divides men from each other, because not everyone will have it. So a peacemaker is not a man who keeps everybody at the table. He is a man who works for the real thing and accepts that the real thing costs him some of the room.

Paul draws the boundary with unusual honesty: if it is possible, as much as it is up to you, be at peace with all men. Two qualifiers in one short verse. If it is possible — sometimes it is not. As much as it is up to you — your half is your responsibility and the other man’s half is not. That verse has freed more consciences than most sermons. You are commanded to make peace. You are not commanded to succeed, and you are not guilty for the part of the wall the other man refuses to take down.

Behind the whole beatitude stands the reason any of it is possible. Being therefore justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ. That is the vertical peace, and it comes first — not because it is more spiritual, but because it is the only peace a man did not have to manufacture. Everything horizontal in this verse runs downhill from it. And Paul is blunt about what we were before it: for if, while we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, much more, being reconciled, we will be saved by his life. Enemies. Not neutrals, not strangers. The peace we are told to make with difficult men is the peace God already made with us when we were the difficult men.

Then Paul says what God did with the men He reconciled, and it is the job description for verse 9: all things are of God, who reconciled us to himself through Jesus Christ, and gave to us the ministry of reconciliation … we are therefore ambassadors on behalf of Christ, as though God were entreating by us. The reconciled are handed the trade. That is why the beatitude is not merely about smoothing over quarrels: the largest peace a man can make is to carry the terms of the settlement to somebody still at war with God. How beautiful are the feet of those who preach the Good News of peace — the gospel itself is called a peace announcement, and a man puts it on like boots.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed reading of this verse has to keep two things from collapsing into each other. Sonship is not wages for peacemaking; and peacemaking is not optional decoration on sonship. Calvin holds both, and he is unusually useful here because he treats the whole sequence — God’s peace toward enemies, our adoption, and the command to love our own enemies — as a single argument rather than three topics.

Start where he starts: with who moved first.

For it was not after we were reconciled to him by the blood of his Son that he began to love us, but he loved us before the foundation of the world, that with his only begotten Son we too might be sons of God before we were any thing at all.

Calvin, Institutes II.16

The peace did not begin at the negotiating table. It began in the love that sent someone to the table. Which is the order every human peacemaking either copies or contradicts: the party with the grievance and the power moves first, and moves before the other party is sorry. That is not weakness. It is how it was done for you.

And Calvin will not let the word enemies soften on the way through:

For when by gratuitous adoption he admitted those who were enemies to the rank of sons, he even then acted in the character of a Redeemer.

Calvin, Institutes I.10

Enemies to the rank of sons. That single line contains both halves of Matthew 5:9 — the peacemaking and the name — and it puts God on the working end of both. Calvin states the transaction plainly elsewhere:

This will become still clearer if we reflect, that the work to be performed by the Mediator was of no common description: being to restore us to the divine favour, so as to make us, instead of sons of men, sons of God; instead of heirs of hell, heirs of a heavenly kingdom.

Calvin, Institutes II.12

Note the vocabulary: heirs. Calvin is reading huios the way the word actually runs — sonship as estate, not sentiment.

Then the corporate side, and here Calvin is essentially exegeting Ephesians 2, which is the New Testament’s largest treatment of peace between men:

the middle wall of partition, which had long kept the divine mercy within the confines of Israel, was broken down, peace was preached to them who were afar off, as well as to those who were nigh, that being, together reconciled to God, they might unite as one people.

Calvin, Institutes II.11

Watch the order in that sentence, because it is the pattern for every reconciliation between men that lasts. The wall did not come down because the two parties negotiated. It came down because both were reconciled to God, and being reconciled to Him they found themselves on the same side of something bigger than the wall. Peace between men, in this book, is a byproduct of peace with God. That is not a reason to skip the hard conversation. It is the reason the hard conversation has anywhere to land.

Which brings up the question every man asks quietly: is the enemy-loving in Matthew 5 actually a command, or is it an ideal for people with unusual temperaments? Calvin had to answer that in his own century, against theologians who had filed these very verses under advice for professional religious men. He is not gentle about it.

Is there a page of the Law in which any such distinction exists; or rather do we not meet in every page with commands which, in the strictest terms, require us to love our enemies?

Calvin, Institutes II.8

Then he goes to Matthew 5:44–45 itself — the passage that supplies verse 9 with its logic — and makes the argument from the promise attached to it:

For what is left to us if we are excluded from the number of the children of God?

Calvin, Institutes II.8

That is the point in one question. Jesus does not hang children of your Father on an optional extra. If loving enemies were advice, then sonship would be advice, and there is nothing left. The same reasoning binds our verse: peacemaking is not an aptitude some believers happen to have. It is what the family does.

And Calvin’s working rule for how a man gets himself to do it is worth memorizing, because it takes the decision out of the hands of your feelings about the person:

our fundamental principle must ever be, Let a man be what he may, he is still to be loved, because God is loved.

Calvin, Institutes II.8

He arrives there by a route worth copying: in an act of love, turn your eyes first not to the man — whose face, Calvin says drily, more often produces hatred than love — but to God, who requires that the love we bear Him be spread over everyone. So the peacemaker does not have to manufacture warmth he does not feel toward a man who has earned none. He has to look somewhere else first.

Now the guardrail on the other side, so this does not become sentiment. Calvin was no pacifist; he defends the magistrate’s sword through the last chapter of the Institutes, and he still writes that when a ruler must go to war, assuredly all other means must be tried before having recourse to arms. Force is not forbidden and it is not first. That is a peacemaker’s standard for any conflict he has authority in — every other avenue spent before the costly one, and no appetite for the costly one.

Nor is peacemaking the same as pretending a problem is not there. In his treatment of church discipline Calvin cites Augustine at length on exactly this, and the phrase he keeps is Paul’s: we must also endeavour, by bearing with each other, to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. Bearing with. Not ignoring, not papering over — carrying. Peace among real people is a weight somebody agrees to hold.

Finally, where a man is meant to stand while he does any of this:

Our only confidence and boasting, our only anchor of salvation is, that Christ the Son of God is ours, and that we are in him sons of God and heirs of the heavenly kingdom, being called, not by our worth, but the kindness of God, to the hope of eternal blessedness.

Calvin, Institutes III.17

Not by our worth. Which closes the door on the reading a hard-working man will reach for first — that if he brokers enough peace he will have earned the name. Calvin says the name was given on other grounds entirely, and the peacemaking is the evidence of the giving, not the price of it.

We must, however, constantly hold, that God never was a Father to angels and men save in respect of his only-begotten Son: that men, especially, who by their iniquity were rendered hateful to God, are sons by gratuitous adoption, because he is a Son by nature.

Calvin, Institutes II.14

One Son by nature; everyone else by adoption, and adoption purchased. Matthew 5:9 promises the family name to peacemakers, and the only reason there is a family to be named into is that the Son who is a Son by nature made the peace at the cross.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession has no chapter on peacemaking. It has a chapter on the promise at the end of the verse, and reading it beside Matthew 5:9 shows how much freight the phrase shall be called children of God is carrying. Here is the whole of it, in two halves.

All those who are justified, God has graciously made partakers of the grace of adoption, in and for the sake of his only Son Jesus Christ. By this grace they are taken into the number of the children of God and enjoy their liberties and privileges. They have his name put upon them, receive the Spirit of adoption, have access to the throne of grace with boldness, and are enabled to cry, Abba, Father.

1689 LBCF, ch. 12 — Of Adoption

They have his name put upon them. That is Matthew 5:9 in five words, and the confession did not get it from our verse — it is reading Revelation and Romans and Galatians and landing in the same place. The naming is not a compliment paid to a man’s character. It is a legal act with a document behind it, and the document is signed in and for the sake of his only Son.

Notice also the sequence the confession insists on: all those who are justified. Adoption follows justification; it does not compete with it. So the man who reads verse 9, takes an inventory of the fights he has started and the ones he has ducked, and concludes he cannot possibly be in the family, has the order backwards. He is not named a son because he made peace well. He is named a son because Christ made the peace, and now he goes and does what sons do.

Then the second half, which is where the confession stops being abstract:

They are pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened by him as by a Father, yet never cast off, but are sealed to the day of redemption, and inherit the promises as heirs of everlasting salvation.

1689 LBCF, ch. 12 — Of Adoption

Chastened by him as by a Father, yet never cast off. Set that against the working life of an actual peacemaker. He will get it wrong. He will step into a fight he had no business in, or stay out of one he should have entered, or broker a truce that collapses by Thanksgiving. The confession says the chastening comes and the casting-off does not. A man can afford to try a hard reconciliation and fail at it, because his standing was never resting on the outcome.

The Baptist Catechism states the same grant in one sentence:

Adoption is an act of God's free grace, by which we are received into the number and have a right to all the privileges of the sons of God.

The Baptist Catechism, Question 37

And then, two questions later, it lists what a son actually gets to carry around in the meantime — and the second item on the list is the thing this beatitude is about:

The benefits which in this life do accompany or flow from justification, adoption, and sanctification, are assurance of God's love, peace of conscience, joy in the Holy Spirit, increase of grace, and perseverance in it to the end.

The Baptist Catechism, Question 39
The two halves of the verse, in the confession’s own words

Peacemakers — men who do the family work, which is reconciliation, because they were reconciled first: enemies to the rank of sons.

Shall be called children of God — they have his name put upon them, with access to the throne of grace with boldness, never cast off, and peace of conscience in hand while the outward peace is still being fought for.

5. For the Man Reading This

Refuse the two easy readings before you do anything else. The first says a peacemaker is a man who does not fight — which makes cowardice a virtue and lets every conflict-avoidant man in the room feel commended for the thing that is actually ruining his home. The second says a peacemaker is a professional: a diplomat, a mediator, somebody with a title. Neither is the verse. The verse blesses a man who goes and makes a peace that did not exist, at whatever level of the world he has been handed.

Start with the peace you did not make. Romans 5:1 comes before Romans 12:18 for a reason. If you are trying to broker peace between other people while you are at war with God, you are running a business you do not have the capital for. Settle that first, on the terms already paid.

Go to the man who is angry at you. Matthew 5:23–24 puts the burden on the one who remembers the grievance, not the one who holds it. So think of the person who has something against you right now — you know who — and do not wait for him to soften. Send the message this week. Do not attach a defence to it. You are not trying to win the account of what happened; you are trying to make peace, and those are different jobs.

Have the small conversation. Almost every catastrophic conflict in a marriage, a church, or a shop was once a two-minute conversation somebody would not have. Go, show him his fault between you and him alone. Alone is the operative word. A grievance you take to a third party before you take it to the man is not peacemaking; it is recruiting.

Do not buy quiet with a lie. First pure, then peaceable — James put those in that order and it is not negotiable. A peace built on an unsaid truth is a loan at a terrible rate, and it comes due at the worst possible moment. If the only way to keep the room calm is for everyone to agree not to mention the thing, you are not the peacemaker in that room. You are the accountant.

Own your half and release the rest. If it is possible, as much as it is up to you. Some men will not be reconciled to you, and Scripture does not make you responsible for their half of the wall. Do everything up to the limit of what is yours, then stop, and do not let a bitter man make you feel guilty for a peace he refused.

Expect the cost, and read it correctly. The next verse is about persecution. If stepping into a fight to make peace has cost you a friendship, a position, or your reputation with one of the parties, you have not failed the beatitude — you have found out it was true. Peacemakers get hit. That is why the promise attached to the job is a name and an inheritance rather than a quiet afternoon.

Seek the peace of the place you are stuck in. Calvin points to the Lord’s command in Jeremiah to exiles who had every reason to sulk: seek the peace of the city where I have caused you to be carried away captive, and pray to the LORD for it; for in its peace you shall have peace. You may not have chosen the town, the unit, the shift, or the family situation. Work for its peace anyway. Your own is downstream of it.

Carry the biggest peace you have. The largest reconciliation you can broker is not between two neighbours; it is between a man and God, and you have been handed the terms. We are therefore ambassadors on behalf of Christ. Most men never say a word about it because they are waiting to feel qualified. The qualification is the commission.

In the Sermon on the Mount memorize pack — carry it. Ten words. Say them the next time you are deciding whether to make the call or let it ride another month.

And carry the promise the way it is written. Not they shall be thanked. Not they shall be understood. They shall be called sons of God — named into a house, in a will, by the only voice whose naming holds. On the day that name is read out publicly, nobody there will remember what it cost you to make peace with your brother-in-law.
