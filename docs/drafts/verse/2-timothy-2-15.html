Verse Study · 2 Timothy

2 Timothy 2:15

Paul is not telling a young pastor to read more. He is telling him whose review he is working under, and warning him there is a wrong way to handle a true book.

Give diligence to present yourself approved by God, a workman who doesn’t need to be ashamed, properly handling the Word of Truth.— World English Bible (public domain)

Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

One sentence, one command, and everything else in the verse hangs off it. Notice first what the sentence takes for granted. It assumes a man can handle a true book badly. It assumes the handling can be measured. And it assumes the measuring is done by God, not by the room — which means a man can win the argument in front of him and still fail the verse. Paul is not worried here about Timothy being outvoted. He is worried about Timothy being ashamed.

The two public-domain English versions above disagree on the first word, and the disagreement is worth a minute. The KJV says study; the WEB says give diligence. Modern ears hear "study" and picture a desk and a lamp. The Greek verb underneath is not about furniture. It is about effort.

  • σπουδάζω spoudazō · "Study" · Strong's G4704

    To be diligent — to make haste that is earnest, not sloppy. The lexicon puts it bluntly: this is not a library hobby, it is the workman's hurry. A lazy man can own a shelf of Bibles and never once do what this verb asks. The verb governs the whole charge. Everything after it — the presenting, the approval, the unashamed standing, the straight cut — is what the diligence is for.

  • παρίστημι paristēmi · "to shew" · Strong's G3936

    From para (beside, before) and histēmi (to stand): to place beside, to stand before, to present, to offer. It is the word Paul uses in Romans 12:1 for presenting the body as a living sacrifice. That matters here. Timothy is not told to present his work for inspection. He is told to present himself. The workman is the thing handed over the counter.

  • δόκιμος dokimos · "approved" · Strong's G1384

    Approved, tested, proven — passed the examination and found genuine. This is the assayer's word, the language of a man testing metal to see what it really is. So "approved" here does not mean liked. It means the sample went into the fire and came back out still itself. Approval of that kind is not assumed. It is sought, and it can be missed.

  • ἐργάτης ergatēs · "a workman" · Strong's G2040

    The laborer, the one who performs work. In the Gospels it is a field hand at harvest — "the harvest is plentiful but the workers are few." Paul had every image available to him and did not reach for the scholar in his chair. He reached for the man who is paid by the day and whose work is visible when he stops. Handling Scripture, in this verse, is a trade.

  • ἀνεπαίσχυντος anepaischuntos · "that needeth not to be ashamed" · Strong's G422

    Unashamed, without cause for shame. The word occurs once in the New Testament — here. It is not describing a man who has talked himself out of feeling bad. It describes a man who has no grounds for shame, because the work will stand when it is looked at. Shame, in this verse, is an outcome you can avoid by working.

  • ὀρθοτομέω orthotomeō · "rightly dividing" · Strong's G3718

    Built from orthos (straight) and temnō (to cut): to cut straight, to lay a true line through the word so a man can walk it. The KJV's "rightly dividing" has been misread for centuries as permission to slice the Bible into favorite portions. It is the opposite. It is the workman's care not to bend the truth. And it carries a warning most of us would rather not hear: a crooked cut still uses the same book.

The object of the cutting is "the word of truth" — logos joined to alētheia. That noun names truth as what is real and unhidden, not a private feeling; it is the word Christ takes onto his own lips in John 14:6. And the exact phrase is not unique to this verse. Paul uses "the word of the truth" in Ephesians 1:13 and Colossians 1:5 for the gospel itself — the message people heard and believed. So the thing being cut straight is not a general category called religion. It is the announcement about Jesus Christ, in writing, with edges.

2. Where It Sits

2 Timothy 2 is a chapter about handing something on. It opens with Paul telling Timothy to commit what he has heard to faithful men who will teach others also. Then come three pictures of a man under obligation: the soldier who does not entangle himself, the athlete who is not crowned unless he competes by the rules, the farmer who labors and then eats. Every one of them is about somebody else's rules and somebody else's clock. Paul then says, in effect, work it out — "Consider what I say, and may the Lord give you understanding in all things."

By verse 14 the tone sharpens. Timothy is to charge them in the sight of the Lord not to argue about words — a fight that turns "no profit" and subverts the people listening. Verse 15 is the positive counterpart to that prohibition, and verse 16 immediately returns to the negative: shun empty chatter, because it goes further into ungodliness. Verse 17 names the gangrene and then names two men, Hymenaeus and Philetus, who erred concerning the truth by saying the resurrection was already past, and overthrew the faith of some.

That is the neighborhood. On one side, men who fight about words to no profit. On the other, men who got a doctrine wrong badly enough to wreck other people's faith — and Paul prints their names. Verse 15 sits between the two, and it is not a call to retreat from either. It is a call to competence. The alternative to a bad handler of Scripture is not a man who declines to handle it. It is a workman who can be trusted with live material.

Read that way, the shame Paul has in mind is specific. It is not the shame of losing a debate; he has just said the debates are worthless. It is the shame of a man who handled the word carelessly and left a casualty. And the paragraph does not end at verse 15. It runs on to the Lord's servant who must not quarrel, but be gentle toward all, able to teach, patient, correcting opponents in gentleness. Cutting straight is not only a matter of accuracy. It produces a temper.

The verse also has a long spine behind it in the Old Testament, and the cross-reference chain finds it:

  • Ezra 7:10

    For Ezra had set his heart to seek the LORD’s law, and to do it, and to teach statutes and ordinances in Israel.

  • Deuteronomy 17:19

    It shall be with him, and he shall read from it all the days of his life; that he may learn to fear the LORD his God, to keep all the words of this law and these statutes, to do them;

  • Psalm 119:9

    How can a young man keep his way pure? By living according to your word.

  • John 17:17

    Sanctify them in your truth. Your word is truth.

Ezra's order is the one to notice: seek it, do it, then teach it. The king in Deuteronomy 17 copies out the law and reads it every day of his life, and the stated purpose is that he would learn to fear God and keep the words — not that he would become the resident expert. Neither man is doing research. Both are under the book they are handling.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin never wrote a line of what follows about 2 Timothy 2:15 specifically; his commentary on the Pastoral Epistles is not among the works hosted on this site. But the Institutes circles this verse's problem repeatedly, because it was the live question of his century too: how does a man handle a book he did not write and cannot edit?

His answer comes as two rules, and they are as close to a working definition of a straight cut as the Reformed tradition gives:

"let us here remember that on the whole subject of religion one rule of modesty and soberness is to be observed, and it is this, in obscure matters not to speak or think, or even long to know, more than the Word of God has delivered. A second rule is, that in reading the Scriptures we should constantly direct our inquiries and meditations to those things which tend to edification, not indulge in curiosity, or in studying things of no use."

Calvin, Institutes I.14 · Read the chapter →

Both rules are aimed at exactly what Paul is aiming at. The first forbids going past the text — speaking further than God has spoken. The second forbids the profitless question, which is Paul's own complaint in verses 14 and 16. Calvin is not asking for less study. He is asking that the study be pointed somewhere that helps somebody.

Elsewhere he states the fence around the word in its bluntest form:

"The soberness which our Lord once prescribed to his Church, he wishes to be perpetually observed. He forbade that anything should be added to his word, and that anything should be taken from it."

Calvin, Institutes IV.8 · Read the chapter →

Adding and subtracting are the two ways to cut crooked. The first is the man who imports what is not there. The second is the man who quietly drops what is. Hymenaeus and Philetus did the second — the resurrection got moved into the past, where it could not bother anyone — and Paul calls the result gangrene.

One more, because it guards the verse from becoming a technique. Calvin is arguing against men who set the Bible aside for private impressions, and he answers that the Spirit does not work apart from the word he gave:

"As they feel that without the Spirit of God they are utterly devoid of the light of truth, so they are not ignorant that the word is the instrument by which the illumination of the Spirit is dispensed."

Calvin, Institutes I.9 · Read the chapter →

So the diligence Paul commands is not self-powered, and it is not optional either. The Spirit gives the light; the word is the instrument he gives it through. A man who will not do the work has declined the instrument.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession answers the practical question this verse raises and leaves open: how does a workman cut straight? Its answer is not a method book. It is a rule about where the ruler comes from.

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 1, Paragraph 9

"The infallible rule of the interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself; and therefore when there is a question about the true and full sense of any Scripture (which is not many but one), it must be searched out by other places that speak more clearly."

1689 LBCF, ch. 1 · Read Chapter 1, Of the Holy Scriptures →

Two things there are worth carrying. First, a passage has one true sense, not many — which is why the cut can be straight or crooked at all. If every text meant whatever it meant to you, there would be nothing to cut and nothing to be ashamed of. Second, the tool for finding that sense is the rest of the book. The clearer place interprets the darker one.

And before anyone concludes that this puts the standard out of reach of an ordinary man, the same chapter says otherwise:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 1, Paragraph 7

"All things in Scripture are not alike plain in themselves, nor alike clear to all; yet those things which are necessary to be known, believed, and observed for salvation, are so clearly set forth and opened in some place of Scripture or other, that not only the learned, but the unlearned, in a due use of ordinary means, may attain to a sufficient understanding of them."

1689 LBCF, ch. 1 · Read Chapter 1, Of the Holy Scriptures →

That is the mercy inside the command. Paul is not requiring scholarship. He is requiring diligence in the due use of ordinary means — and the catechism spells out what the ordinary means actually are:

The Baptist Catechism · Question 95

"That the Word may become effectual to salvation, we must attend to it with diligence, preparation, and prayer; receive it with faith and love, lay it up in our hearts, and practice it in our lives."

The Baptist Catechism, Q95 · Work through the Catechism →

Diligence, preparation, prayer, faith, love, memory, practice. Six of those seven cost time you do not currently have, and the seventh costs more than that. But notice the shape: the catechism ends where Paul ends. The word is not handled properly until it is practiced. A man who can parse a passage he does not obey has cut himself out of the verse.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse sits on the middle finger of The Word Hand — the memorize pack that puts hearing on the pinky and moves through reading, studying, memorizing, and meditating to the thumb. The middle finger is the Studying finger, and 2 Timothy 2:15 is the companion there to Acts 17:11 and the Bereans. That is not decoration. A grip needs the longest finger; the pack is arguing that a man who only hears and reads has a weak hold on the book. Carry it.

Four things follow, and none of them are comfortable.

Change who you are working in front of. The verse says approved unto God. Most of us handle the Bible with an audience in mind — a small group, a wife, a man at work who asked a question we wanted to answer well. Paul relocates the review board. The question is not whether the room was impressed. It is whether the handling would survive being looked at by the one who wrote it.

Treat it as labor, not talent. The word is workman. Nobody is born able to do this and nobody arrives. The ordinary means are unglamorous: read the paragraph before you quote the verse, read the whole letter through in one sitting at least once, find out who "them" is before you apply a promise to yourself. That is an hour, not a degree. Most of what passes for a hard passage is a passage nobody read the top of.

Accept the negative test. Verses 14 and 16 give you a way to check your own work that does not require you to be right about the text. If your handling of Scripture reliably produces a fight that goes nowhere, you have failed this verse whether or not your exegesis was sound. And verses 24 through 25 close the door on the escape hatch: the Lord's servant must not quarrel, but be gentle, able to teach, patient, correcting opponents in gentleness. A man cutting straight leaves fewer people bleeding, not more.

Count the cost of being correctable. The hardest part of this verse is not the reading. It is being willing to drop a reading you have taught, defended, or built something on, when the clearer place will not let you keep it. That costs pride, and sometimes it costs standing. It is also the only thing that separates a workman from a man with a position.

And underneath it, the thing that makes any of it worth the hours. Paul wrote this in chains — "in which I suffer hardship to the point of chains as a criminal. But God's word isn't chained." The man handing Timothy this charge was out of options himself, and he still believed the book would go where he could not. That is what you are handling. Not a subject. A word of truth that is still moving.
