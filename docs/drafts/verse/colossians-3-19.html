Verse Study · Colossians

Colossians 3:19

Two orders in one breath: love her, and do not go sour on her. The second is how most men lose the first.

Husbands, love your wives, and don’t be bitter against them.— World English Bible (public domain)

Husbands, love your wives, and be not bitter against them.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Paul gives the husband one sentence. The wife gets one. The children get one. The servants get four. The brevity is not neglect. It is a man-sized order: short enough to remember in the truck, long enough to take the rest of your life.

Two verbs carry it. The first is positive and open-ended — love. The second is negative and specific — do not be bitter. Set them next to each other and the sentence starts telling on you. Paul does not warn husbands here against adultery, or desertion, or violence. He warns them against souring. He is writing to men who are still in the house, still paying the bills, still married, and going cold. That is the failure he expects, which means that is the failure most common among men who consider themselves faithful.

Below are the dictionary forms of the words the verse is built on. The inflected Greek line is not reproduced on this page.

  • ἀνήρ anēr · Husbands · Strong’s G435

    An adult male, and in a household passage like this one, a husband in particular. Greek has a broader word for a human being of either sex; Paul does not reach for it. He is addressing men in an office, not men in general. The same noun stands under Ephesians 5:23, where the husband is named the head of the wife, and under 1 Timothy 3:2, where an overseer must be the husband of one wife. Here it is the subject of a command. The office is being handed its orders.

  • ἀγαπάω agapaō · love · Strong’s G25

    Not a passing feeling but a settled will to seek another’s good. It is the verb of John 3:16, where God loved the world and gave His Son, and of Galatians 2:20, where the Son of God loved me and gave Himself for me. Paul is not asking a husband to manufacture an emotion. He is telling him to aim, at one woman, permanently, the same deliberate goodwill he already knows how to extend to men he respects — and to keep aiming it on the weeks she has not earned it. That is why the command can be given at all. You cannot be ordered to feel. You can be ordered to act, and the feeling usually follows the acting rather than leading it.

  • γυνή gynē · your wives · Strong’s G1135

    Woman, or wife; context decides which, and here the possessive decides it. Notice what Paul does not write. Not love women. Not love the household. Not love the family. One woman, identified by her relation to this man. A husband’s love is not a general benevolence he mists over the house and hopes lands somewhere. It has a name and an address, and it can be audited.

  • πικραίνω pikrainō · be … bitter · Strong’s G4087

    From pikros, bitter — to make bitter, to embitter. It is a single Greek verb; the King James simply splits it across “be … bitter.” The same verb describes water gone poisonous in Revelation 8:11, where a third of the waters were made bitter and men died of them. That is the picture Paul is working with. Not a blow — a contamination. Nobody watches a well go bad. It just stops being drinkable, and then people who had no part in the argument start getting sick. English versions divide over whether Paul forbids the inward souring or the outward harshness it produces. He forbids both, because in a marriage they are the same thing on a short delay.

  • πρός pros · against · Strong’s G4314

    The ordinary preposition of direction — toward, facing, in the presence of. It is the word John uses of the Word who was with God, face to face. Here it aims the prohibition. Paul does not say “do not be a bitter man,” which a man could always deny by pointing at how well he gets on with everybody else. He says do not be bitter toward her. A man can be genial at the shop, easy in the truck, warm at church, and carry a standing grudge in exactly one direction. Paul names the direction and closes the exit.

2. Where It Sits

Colossians 3 opens high. You were raised with Christ, so seek the things above; you died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God. Then Paul comes down the ladder rung by rung. Put to death what is earthly. Put away anger, wrath, malice, slander, filthy talk. Put on compassion, kindness, humility, patience. Bear with one another and forgive each other, as Christ forgave you. Above all, love, which binds the rest together. Let the peace of Christ rule, and the word of Christ dwell in you richly.

And then, having taken us from the right hand of God to the dinner table in eighteen verses, Paul says: wives. Husbands. Children. Fathers. Servants. Masters.

Colossians 3:18 — Wives, be in subjection to your husbands, as is fitting in the Lord.

Colossians 3:21 — Fathers, don’t provoke your children, so that they won’t be discouraged.

That descent is the argument. The Colossian error was a spirituality of the exotic — visions, regulations, a diet, a calendar, a class of insiders with access to something the ordinary believer lacked. Paul answers it not with a higher secret but with a lower one. The place your union with the risen Christ becomes visible is the place you least want it inspected: the tone you use with your wife on a Thursday night when you are tired and she is wrong.

Two details in the paragraph are worth holding onto. First, verse 19 is not a new subject. Verse 8 already told this church to put away anger, wrath and malice, and verse 13 already told them to bear with one another and forgive. Verse 19 simply aims those commands at the one relationship where a man is most inclined to grant himself an exception — because she is not a stranger, because she knew and married him, because at home he is off duty. Paul says there is no off duty.

Second, Paul addresses each party directly. He does not instruct the head of the house about his dependents; he speaks to the wife, the child, and the servant as answerable to the Lord in their own right — and then turns and puts the head of the house under orders too, in front of everyone. The command to the husband is public. His wife heard this letter read out loud in the same room he did.

Ephesians 5 gives the long version of the same charge, and the Old Testament had already refused to let a man treat his wife as furniture:

  • Ephesians 5:25

    Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the assembly, and gave himself up for it;

  • Ephesians 5:28–29

    Even so husbands also ought to love their own wives as their own bodies. He who loves his own wife loves himself. For no man ever hated his own flesh; but nourishes and cherishes it, even as the Lord also does the assembly;

  • Genesis 2:23

    The man said, “This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh. She will be called ‘woman,’ because she was taken out of Man.”

  • Proverbs 5:18

    Let your spring be blessed. Rejoice in the wife of your youth.

  • Psalm 128:3

    Your wife will be as a fruitful vine, in the innermost parts of your house; your children like olive plants, around your table.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin — the standard is not your patience, it is Christ’s

Calvin takes up Ephesians 5 in the middle of an argument about something else entirely. He is dismantling the claim that marriage is a sacrament, and he has no patience for the exegesis behind it. But in the middle of the demolition he states, plainly, what Paul is actually doing in the passage:

Paul, in order to show husbands how they ought to love their wives, sets Christ before them as an example. As he shed his bowels of affection for the Church, which he has espoused to himself, so he would have every one to feel affected toward his wife.

Calvin, Institutes IV.19 (read the chapter →)

Set that beside Colossians 3:19 and the shorter verse gets its measure. Paul in Colossae does not repeat the Christ-and-the-church comparison, but he is not working from a lower standard. The command is the same command with the argument left out, written to a church that had already been told, four verses earlier, to forgive as Christ forgave them. The husband’s benchmark is not the man next door, or his own father, or how he behaved last year. It is a Bridegroom who bore with a bride who had nothing to offer him.

Calvin — the duty does not wait on her performance

The second passage is not about marriage at all. Calvin is arguing that a subject owes honor to a bad ruler, and he reaches for the household as an analogy a reader will not be able to dodge. What matters for us is what he treats as too obvious to argue: that husbands are under standing orders, and that those orders are what makes the abuse an abuse.

Husbands are bound by mutual duties to their wives, and parents to their children. Should husbands and parents neglect their duty… should the former treat with the greatest contumely the wives whom they are enjoined to love and to spare as the weaker vessels; would children be less bound in duty to their parents, and wives to their husbands?

Calvin, Institutes IV.20 (read the chapter →)

Calvin’s conclusion there runs toward the subject and the ruler, and it cuts against the wife’s convenience, not the husband’s. But read it from the husband’s chair and it removes the excuse he most wants. Her duty is not the payment he collects for doing his. She is bound whether he loves her or not — and he is bound whether she is easy to love or not, which means his bitterness cannot be filed as a response. It has no permission slip. Notice also what Calvin assumes without argument: the husbands are “enjoined to love and to spare.” To spare — to go easy where you could press, to leave force unused. A man who has never once declined to win an argument he could have won has not begun to obey this verse.

Calvin — where the bitterness actually starts

One more, from the discussion of the law’s two tables. It explains the mechanism:

First, our mind must be completely filled with love to God, and then this love must forthwith flow out toward our neighbour.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 (read the chapter →)

That is the order, and it explains why bitterness at home so often has nothing to do with home. A man whose mind is not filled from above arrives at his own door with nothing to give and a grievance about being asked. The love that is supposed to flow out toward the nearest neighbour has no source. Paul put the same diagnosis three verses earlier when he told this church to let the word of Christ dwell in them richly. A man cannot pour out what he never took in.

4. Confessional Anchor

No confessional paragraph cites this verse as a proof-text, so the anchor is by doctrine. The Baptist Catechism files a husband’s conduct under the fifth commandment — the commandment governing what the divines called relative duties, the obligations attached to the place you occupy rather than to the person you happen to like.

Baptist Catechism · Question 69

The fifth commandment requires the preserving the honor and performing the duties belonging to everyone in their various places and relations, as superiors, inferiors, or equals.

The Baptist Catechism, Q. 69 (work through it →)

The fifth commandment forbids the neglect of, or doing anything against, the honor and duty which belongs to everyone in their various places and relations.

The Baptist Catechism, Q. 70 (work through it →)

Read those two together and Colossians 3:19 stops being advice. Husband is a place. It comes with duties that are owed, and the neglect of them is not a shortcoming — it is a breach of the commandment. That is a harder word than “try to be nicer,” and a cleaner one. A man can argue about whether he is a good husband. He cannot argue about whether he has a duty.

Behind the fifth commandment stands the whole:

Baptist Catechism · Question 47

The sum of the ten commandments is to love the Lord our God with all our heart, with all our soul, with all our strength, and with all our mind; and our neighbour as ourselves.

The Baptist Catechism, Q. 47 (work through it →)

Your wife is the nearest neighbour you have. Whatever else the second table means, it means her first — and the man who is generous with strangers and short with the woman across the table has the commandment exactly backwards.

The 1689 Confession then says what this command is for in the life of a man already justified:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 19, Paragraph 6

As a rule of life, informing them of the will of God and their duty, it directs and binds them to walk accordingly. It discovers also the sinful pollutions of their nature, hearts, and lives, so that examining themselves by it they may come to further conviction of, humiliation for, and hatred against sin — together with a clearer sight of their need of Christ and the perfection of his obedience.

1689 LBCF, ch. 19 (read the chapter →)

Both uses are running in this one verse. It directs — here is what a husband does. And it discovers — here is what you are. Most men can read “love your wives” and nod. Very few can read “be not bitter against them” without something specific coming to mind: a grievance kept warm, a tone, a subject he brings up because he knows it lands. That flinch is the second use of the law doing its work, and it is meant to end where the confession says it ends — not in self-loathing, but in a clearer sight of your need of Christ and the perfection of His obedience. He was never bitter toward His bride. That is your righteousness before it is ever your assignment.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the Family Captain memorize pack — carry it. Ten words. It is short enough that you will have it by Wednesday, and pointed enough that having it will cost you something. That is the trade. A verse you carry gets to interrupt you, and this one interrupts in the middle of the sentence you were about to say.

Bitterness is a ledger, so close the ledger. Nobody wakes up embittered. It is built one entry at a time — the thing she said in 2019, the way she handled the money, the time you were right and never got the acknowledgement. Paul put the remedy four verses up: bear with one another, forgive each other, as Christ forgave you. Forgiveness is not deciding it did not matter. It is deciding you will not collect. Go find the oldest entry on your list and write it off tonight, out loud, to God. Then stop reopening it in your head at red lights.

Watch the tone before you defend the content. Most men litigate the wrong question. You will insist you were right, and you may well have been. The verse does not ask whether you were right. It asks what you were like. Bitterness rarely shows up in what a man says; it shows up in how — the clipped answer, the sigh, the correction offered in front of the kids, the silence deployed as a weapon. If your wife could describe your tone this week in one word, and you would not want that word written down, you already have your answer.

Provision is not affection, and she can tell the difference. Working hard is honorable and it is not the command. A man can carry a household on his back for twenty years and still be bitter toward the woman he is carrying it for — in fact the carrying is often what he uses to justify the bitterness. Look at what Paul actually says here. He gives the husband no achievement to point at. He gives him a disposition to maintain. You cannot invoice for it and you cannot finish it.

Do it on a Tuesday. The command is aimed at ordinary time. Not the anniversary, not the crisis, not the week you are trying to make something up to her. Tuesday: dishes nobody thanked you for, a conversation you would rather not have, a second cup of coffee you carry to her instead of drinking yourself. That is what agapaō looks like when nobody is filming.

And when you fail it — and you will — take it to the right place. The confession said the law shows you your need of Christ and the perfection of His obedience. That is not a soft landing; it is the only landing there is. The Bridegroom who never went sour on His bride is the same one who forgave you, and He is not embittered toward you this morning either. Go apologize to your wife with your name on the failure and no clause beginning “but if you hadn’t.” Then start again. There is no version of this verse you obey once.
