Verse Study · Ephesians

Ephesians 5:3

Paul does not tell this church to keep three sins under control. He tells it to keep them out of the conversation.

But sexual immorality, and all uncleanness, or covetousness, let it not even be mentioned among you, as becomes saints;— World English Bible (public domain)

But fornication, and all uncleanness, or covetousness, let it not be once named among you, as becometh saints;— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The sentence is short, and its verb is not the one you expect. Paul lists three sins and then, instead of forbidding the acts, forbids the naming. He has already forbidden the acts — the whole back half of chapter 4 does that work. Here he moves the line back. Not do not commit these, but do not let them be mentioned. The standard is not the absence of scandal. It is the absence of a comfortable vocabulary for it.

And the reason he gives is not that the sins are too shocking to say out loud. The reason is the last three words: as becomes saints. Conduct is being measured against identity, not against consequences. Paul is not warning the Ephesians what these sins will cost them. He is telling them what they are, and letting the mismatch do the arguing.

  • πορνεία porneia · fornication · Strong’s G4202

    The broad New Testament term for sexual sin outside of God-ordained marriage — fornication, adultery, prostitution, incest, and by extension every sexual act that violates God’s design. The English word pornography comes straight off this root.

    It stands first in the list because it is a category, not an incident. A man can hear a sermon against adultery and file it under other men’s problems. Porneia does not let him. And the modern form of the sin is a private one, conducted alone with a screen — which is exactly the privacy this verse refuses to grant it. What is not named is not repented of.

  • ἀκαθαρσία akatharsia · uncleanness · Strong’s G167

    Uncleanness, impurity, moral filth. It is built on katharos, clean, with the alpha-privative in front of it: the negation of clean. In Paul’s vice lists it sits with the works of the flesh and stands opposite the holiness God calls His people to.

    This is the wider word, and Paul attaches all to it. Where porneia names acts, akatharsia names a condition — the grime that settles on a man who has been living in that air. It is the word he already used one chapter earlier of Gentiles who, having gone callous, gave themselves up to work all uncleanness with greediness. The believer is being told that he came out of that. The word is a reminder of the smell of the old house.

  • πλεονεξία pleonexia · covetousness · Strong’s G4124

    Covetousness, greed, avarice — literally a desire to have more. It describes the self-aggrandizing pursuit of gain, and Paul in Colossians 3:5 flatly calls it idolatry, because it puts material gain where only God belongs.

    This is the word that makes men uncomfortable, because it does not belong in a list about sex — until you see what all three have in common. Pleonexia is appetite that will not close its hand. Point it at a body and you get the first two words in the verse; point it at a bank balance and you get this one. Paul is not padding a list. He is naming one disease twice, and he does it again two verses later when he says the covetous man is an idolater.

  • ὀνομάζω onomazō · named · Strong’s G3687

    To name — to put a word in the mouth so the thing has standing. The verb can honor a man with a title, or give a sin a seat at the table by speaking of it as ordinary talk. It is not about private knowledge. It is about what a church is willing to say out loud.

    This is the hinge of the verse. Paul uses the same verb earlier in this letter for the name of Christ, lifted above every name that is named, and for the Father from whom every family is named. Naming, in Ephesians, is how honor gets assigned. So to let these three be named among you is not merely to gossip about them. It is to seat them — to grant them a place in the ordinary speech of a people who bear another Name.

  • ἅγιος hagios · saints · Strong’s G40

    Set apart, consecrated, morally pure. It is used of God and of the Spirit, and then — this is the revolutionary move of the New Testament — of ordinary, imperfect believers, who are simply called hagioi, holy ones.

    Everything in the verse hangs on this word, and it is a statement of fact before it is a standard. Paul does not write as befits men who are trying hard. He writes as becomes saints, because that is already what they are. The argument is not aspiration. It is consistency. Under the Old Covenant holiness was fenced into sacred space, sacred time, sacred persons; here it is walking around in a port city, talking, and it is asked to sound like itself.

2. Where It Sits

Ephesians 5:2 — Walk in love, even as Christ also loved you, and gave himself up for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling fragrance.

Ephesians 5:4 — nor filthiness, nor foolish talking, nor jesting, which are not appropriate; but rather giving of thanks.

Ephesians turns on its hinge at 4:1. Three chapters of what God has done — chosen, adopted, raised, seated, reconciled — and then: walk worthy of it. Everything after that point is the therefore. Nothing in chapter 5 is a freestanding rule; it is all the shape of a life that has been given something.

The immediate run-up matters. In 4:17–19 Paul describes the old way: minds gone futile, understanding darkened, hearts hardened, and men who having become callous gave themselves up to lust, to work all uncleanness with greediness. That is the same pairing as our verse — uncleanness and greed, side by side — and it is filed under what the Ephesians used to be. Then comes the put-off and put-on of 4:22–24, and a run of specifics: falsehood, anger, theft, corrupt speech, bitterness. Each one gets a replacement, not just a prohibition. The thief does not merely stop stealing; he works so he has something to give away.

Then 5:1–2 raises the ceiling. Be imitators of God. Walk in love, the way Christ loved and gave Himself up. And immediately: But. The but of verse 3 is doing real work. Love, in verse 2, is defined as a self-given away. The three words of verse 3 are all appetite taking. They are not the opposite of holiness in the abstract; they are the opposite of the cross, which is the standard the sentence just set.

Verse 4 keeps going into speech — filthiness, foolish talking, jesting — which confirms that verse 3 was already about the mouth. Verse 5 supplies the stake: no sexually immoral, unclean, or covetous person has any inheritance in the Kingdom. Verse 6 warns that someone will show up with empty words to explain all this away. And verse 12 circles back around: of the things done in darkness, it is a shame even to speak. The verse is not a stray moral rule dropped into a letter about grace. It sits on the seam between what Christ did and how His people talk.

  • 1 Corinthians 6:18

    Flee sexual immorality! “Every sin that a man does is outside the body,” but he who commits sexual immorality sins against his own body.

  • Colossians 3:5

    Put to death therefore your members which are on the earth: sexual immorality, uncleanness, depraved passion, evil desire, and covetousness, which is idolatry;

  • 1 Thessalonians 4:3

    For this is the will of God: your sanctification, that you abstain from sexual immorality,

  • Romans 1:24

    Therefore God also gave them up in the lusts of their hearts to uncleanness, that their bodies should be dishonored among themselves,

  • Psalm 24:4

    He who has clean hands and a pure heart; who has not lifted up his soul to falsehood, and has not sworn deceitfully.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin handles the Ten Commandments by asking, for each one, what the prohibition is actually protecting — what he calls its purport. That method lands him in exactly the place Paul lands: behind the act, at the appetite. On the seventh commandment he refuses to let the reader treat it as a rule about one particular deed.

The purport of this commandment is, that as God loves chastity and purity, we ought to guard against all uncleanness. The substance of the commandment therefore is, that we must not defile ourselves with any impurity or libidinous excess.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 — read the chapter →

Then he closes the exit a man is most likely to reach for. Every one of us keeps a private scoreboard on which the only thing that counts is the outward act, and Calvin takes the scoreboard away:

And let not a man flatter himself, that because he abstains from the outward act he cannot be accused of unchastity. His mind may in the meantime be inwardly inflamed with lust. For Paul’s definition of chastity is purity of mind, combined with purity of body.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 — read the chapter →

He does the same thing with the other half of Paul’s list. When he comes to the tenth commandment he will not let covetousness be reduced to a plan of action either. The commandment reaches all the way down to the first stirring:

The sum of the whole commandment, therefore, is, that whatever each individual possesses remain entire and secure, not only from injury, or the wish to injure, but also from the slightest feeling of covetousness which can spring up in the mind.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 — read the chapter →

Put those together and you have the Reformed reading of Ephesians 5:3. The apostle is not being fastidious about vocabulary. He is treating speech as the place where the appetite first shows itself in public. A man does not wake up an adulterer. He gets there by degrees, and one of the earliest degrees is the moment a sin stops being unthinkable and becomes something he can say lightly in company.

4. Confessional Anchor

The Baptist Catechism reaches for this very verse. When it asks what the seventh commandment forbids, it lists Ephesians 5:3–4 among its proof-texts — the divines read our verse as the seventh commandment applied to a man’s mouth. Note the three words in the answer just before it: heart, speech, and behavior.

The seventh commandment requires the preservation of our own and our neighbor’s chastity, in heart, speech, and behavior.

The Baptist Catechism, Q. 76 — work through the Catechism →

The seventh commandment forbids all unchaste thoughts, words, and actions.

The Baptist Catechism, Q. 77 — work through the Catechism →

The same catechism handles the covetous half of the verse the same way, refusing to stop at the outward grab:

The tenth commandment forbids all discontentment with our own estate, envying or grieving at the good of our neighbor, and all inordinate motions and affections to anything that is his.

The Baptist Catechism, Q. 86 — work through the Catechism →

Behind the catechism stands the confession’s account of how any of this happens. The 1689 does not describe holiness as a resolution a man makes. It describes a work done in him, progressive and contested, in which the appetites are not merely restrained but killed off by degrees:

the dominion of the whole body of sin is destroyed, and the several lusts thereof are more and more weakened and mortified, and they more and more quickened and strengthened in all saving graces, to the practice of all true holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord.

1689 LBCF, ch. 13, para. 1 — Of Sanctification →

And the confession draws the corporate line that Paul draws with the words among you. A visible church is a body of people whose profession and whose manner of life are not at war with one another:

All persons throughout the world, professing the faith of the gospel and obedience unto God by Christ according to it — not destroying their own profession by any errors overthrowing the foundation, or by unholiness of conversation — are and may be called visible saints; and of such ought all particular congregations to be constituted.

1689 LBCF, ch. 26, para. 2 — Of the Church →

The older sense of conversation covers a man’s whole manner of life, not merely his talk. But Ephesians 5:3 insists the talk is part of it, and the confession is naming the same test: a congregation whose speech has quietly made room for what its confession condemns has begun to destroy its own profession.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the PROVEN Men memorize pack — carry it. Carrying it does something specific: it puts a verdict on the room before the room gets going, so you are not deciding under pressure with everyone watching.

It regulates the joke. Most men reading this will never commit adultery. Nearly all of them will laugh. Paul puts those two facts on the same page on purpose, and verse 4 says the quiet part — foolish talking, jesting. The test is not whether you would do the thing. The test is whether you find it funny. A man’s laugh is the most honest report he files all day, because he does not compose it first.

It regulates the drive to the jobsite and the group chat. That is where sin actually gets named among you: the story told for effect, the comment about a woman who is not in the room, the running commentary on what another man owns. Paul put greed in this sentence, so the same rule governs the way you talk about his truck, his house, and his pay.

It is not a gag order on confession. Eight verses later Paul tells the same church to reprove the unfruitful deeds of darkness, which cannot be done silently. Naming a sin in order to kill it is obedience. Naming it for entertainment is what this verse forbids. If you cannot tell which one you are doing, notice who benefits: reproof costs the speaker something, and the joke costs him nothing.

The ground is not your record. The sentence ends as becomes saints, not as becomes men who are managing well. You are not being asked to earn the name. You are being asked to sound like the name you were already given, and that is a very different weight to carry. The man who thinks he is auditioning will quit the first week he fails. The man who knows he is already named has somewhere to stand while he fights.

The cost is real and small and constant: you will be the one who does not laugh, and everyone will notice. There is no version of this obedience that is invisible. What it buys is also real — a daughter who never has to wonder what her father says when she is not there, a wife who is not competing with anything, a conscience that does not go looking for you at two in the morning, and a church where a new believer is not handed the vocabulary of his old life on the way to the parking lot.
