Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:2

Eight words of throat-clearing, or the most deliberate sentence in the chapter. Matthew did not write it by accident.

He opened his mouth and taught them, saying,— World English Bible (public domain)

And he opened his mouth, and taught them, saying,— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

On its face the verse is redundant. Of course he opened his mouth. He taught them; nobody teaches with his jaw wired shut. A modern editor would strike the clause and lose nothing, and several modern translations quietly do exactly that.

Matthew keeps it. He was not a wordy writer — the whole scene up to here has taken him one verse — and he spends four Greek words on a fact you could have assumed. That is worth stopping over, because “he opened his mouth” is not description. It is a formula, and it has a job.

  • ἀνοίγω anoigō · “he opened” · Strong’s G455

    The standard Greek verb for opening — doors, eyes, heavens, books, tombs. Its range runs from the plainly physical to the plainly divine, which makes it one of the most theologically loaded ordinary words in the New Testament. Line up what Scripture opens and you see the pattern: the heavens at his baptism, the eyes of the blind, the tomb of Lazarus, the sealed scroll, Lydia’s heart, the Scriptures themselves after the resurrection. In almost every one of those, the opening is something done to a person by God. Here the subject and the object are the same man. Nobody opened this mouth for him.

  • στόμα stoma · “mouth” · Strong’s G4750

    A noun that means the mouth in your face, and by extension the speech that comes out of it — voice, words, utterance — and, oddly to us, the edge of a sword. Scripture treats the mouth as the instrument of confession, prophecy, praise and judgment. Christ’s own weapon in Revelation is the sword of his mouth. And Matthew himself, seven chapters later, tells you what a mouth is for: out of the abundance of the heart, the mouth speaks (Matthew 12:34). So when Matthew reports whose mouth is opening, he is telling you in advance where the next three chapters came from.

  • διδάσκω didaskō · “taught” · Strong’s G1321

    To teach, to instruct, to pass on knowledge and skill — the authoritative transmission of truth that changes what a man understands and therefore what he does. In the Gospels this is Jesus’ primary public activity alongside healing. Note what Matthew did not write. Not that he preached at them, not that he moved them, not that he declared something. He taught. Teaching has content, order, and a syllabus, and it assumes a learner who does not yet know the material.

  • λέγω legō · “saying” · Strong’s G3004

    To say, to speak, to declare — the commonest verb of speech in the New Testament, and from the same root as logos, the word for a word. It is the verb behind the line that will detonate six times before this chapter ends: but I say to you. Christianity is a religion of the spoken word, truth communicated rather than merely intuited, and this is the plain word for it. The participle here is a hinge: everything after it is quotation.

Two small words carry more than their size. The conjunction kai (G2532) that opens the King James rendering is the commonest word in the Greek New Testament, and its relentless and…and…and is what gives Gospel narrative its forward pressure — the sermon is not a separate document dropped into the book, it is the next thing that happened. And the possessive behind “his mouth” is autos (G846), the pronoun that in its emphatic uses insists on personal, direct action: not a proxy, not a messenger, but the man himself.

One more thing the line does that English hides. Our own lexicon renders the clause “he began to teach them,” and that is not a loose paraphrase; it is catching something in the Greek verb that a flat past tense loses. This is not one utterance. It is the opening of a course of instruction that runs three chapters and does not stop until 7:27.

The formula

Now the phrase itself. Hebrew and its Greek children use “he opened his mouth” the way a court reporter uses the witness was sworn. It marks the speech that follows as deliberate, prepared, and weighty — not an aside, not a remark in passing. Watch it work:

Job 3:1 — After this Job opened his mouth, and cursed the day of his birth.

Job 33:2 — See now, I have opened my mouth. My tongue has spoken in my mouth.

Psalm 78:2 — I will open my mouth in a parable. I will utter dark sayings of old,

Proverbs 31:8 — Open your mouth for the mute, in the cause of all who are left desolate.

Job has sat seven days in silence before the formula appears; then he opens his mouth. Asaph uses it to announce that what follows is not small talk but the buried history of God with Israel. The mother of King Lemuel uses it as a command — a man with a voice is obligated to spend it on people who have none.

The New Testament keeps the idiom, and Luke in particular puts it at the hinges of the book of Acts:

Acts 8:35 — Philip opened his mouth, and beginning from this Scripture, preached to him about Jesus.

Acts 10:34 — Peter opened his mouth and said, “Truly I perceive that God doesn’t show favoritism;

Both times the gospel is about to cross a border it has never crossed — to an Ethiopian in a chariot, and to a Roman officer in a house full of Gentiles. Luke reaches for the same formula both times. Paul asks the Ephesians to pray for him along the same line, that utterance may be given to me in opening my mouth, to make known with boldness the mystery of the Good News (Ephesians 6:19).

And the formula is not sentimental. It reports what is coming, whatever is coming. Revelation 13:6 says of the beast that he opened his mouth for blasphemy against God. An opened mouth is a committed mouth. That is precisely why Matthew uses the phrase here, and why it is not filler.

2. Where It Sits

Verse 1 was four movements and no words: he saw, he climbed, he sat, they came. Verse 2 is the sound coming on. Verse 3 is “Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven.” This one clause is the doorway into the most quoted teaching in the history of the world, and it is nine syllables long.

Consider what Matthew has done to you for four chapters. He has told you, repeatedly, that Jesus taught — and never once told you what he said.

Matthew 4:23 — Jesus went about in all Galilee, teaching in their synagogues, preaching the Good News of the Kingdom, and healing every disease and every sickness among the people.

Teaching in their synagogues. Whole regions of it. Not one line of content. That is a summary written from a great distance, and Matthew holds you at that distance on purpose, all the way to 5:2. Then the mouth opens and we are inside the room for three chapters. When he finally closes it, Matthew tells you what it did to the people standing there:

Matthew 7:28 — When Jesus had finished saying these things, the multitudes were astonished at his teaching,

Matthew 7:29 — for he taught them with authority, and not like the scribes.

The same verb, didaskō, opens the sermon in 5:2 and closes it in 7:29. Matthew brackets three chapters with the word for teaching and then names the difference between this teacher and every other one they had ever heard. The scribes had a mouth too. They opened it constantly. What they lacked was the thing 5:2 quietly assumes.

Matthew and this phrase

There is a reason this idiom was ready to hand for Matthew: he had been reading Psalm 78, and later in his Gospel he says so outright.

Matthew 13:35 — that it might be fulfilled which was spoken through the prophet, saying, “I will open my mouth in parables; I will utter things hidden from the foundation of the world.”

Asaph’s line about opening his mouth is, to Matthew, a prophecy about how the Messiah would speak. So when Matthew writes the same phrase in 5:2, he is not being wordy. He is a man who has thought about what it means when this particular mouth opens.

The mouth that stayed shut

Hold 5:2 next to the end of the same Gospel and the phrase turns over in your hand. Isaiah had written of the Servant that he was oppressed, yet when he was afflicted he didn’t open his mouth (Isaiah 53:7) — the text the Ethiopian was reading when Philip opened his. And Matthew stages it:

Matthew 26:63 — But Jesus held his peace. The high priest answered him, “I adjure you by the living God, that you tell us whether you are the Christ, the Son of God.”

Matthew 27:14 — He gave him no answer, not even one word, so that the governor marveled greatly.

The same mouth. On the hill it opens and will not stop for three chapters. In the courtroom it closes and will not open for a governor. Both are obedience, and the difference is what he had to give. On the mountain he had a word to give, so he gave it. In the trial he had a life to give, and a defense would have gotten in the way.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin — on mouths that could not open first

Calvin spends a long stretch of the Institutes on a question that sounds academic and is not: where does a teacher in the church get the right to say anything? His answer for the whole Old Testament is a hard limit, and he states it in exactly the vocabulary of our verse:

Accordingly, none of the prophets opened his mouth unless preceded by the word of the Lord.

Calvin, Institutes IV.8 · Read the chapter →

Read Matthew 5:2 against that sentence and the whole scene changes shape. Every man who ever legitimately opened his mouth in Israel did so second. The word came, and then the mouth. Thus saith the LORD is not a flourish; it is a licence, and no prophet had one without it. On this hillside there is no such preface. Nothing precedes the mouth. He opens it, and what comes out is the word of the Lord.

Calvin knows what that means, and he says it plainly a few paragraphs later:

But when at length the Wisdom of God was manifested in the flesh, he fully unfolded to us all that the human mind can comprehend, or ought to think of the heavenly Father.

Calvin, Institutes IV.8 · Read the chapter →

All that the human mind can comprehend, or ought to think. That is a ceiling and a floor at once. And then the line that belongs over this verse more than any other in the Institutes:

And, indeed, what can now be expected or desired from man, when the very Word of life has appeared before us, and familiarly explained himself?

Calvin, Institutes IV.8 · Read the chapter →

Familiarly explained himself. That is what a man does when he sits down on a hill and starts talking about salt, and lamps, and anger, and marriage, and money, and worry, and two ways to build a house. There is nothing remote about the Sermon on the Mount. The Word of life opened his mouth and explained himself in the vocabulary of a working man’s week.

Calvin — on why a teacher submits

Calvin will not let the point stay ornamental. If Christ is the one mouth that needed no word before it, then everything about how he taught becomes the rule for everyone who teaches after him:

He who always was the only and eternal counsellor of the Father, who by the Father was constituted Lord and Master of all, yet because he performed the ministry of teaching, prescribed to all ministers by his example the rule which they ought to follow in teaching.

Calvin, Institutes IV.8 · Read the chapter →

The rule is the one Jesus states himself in John 7:16 — my doctrine is not mine, but his that sent me. The eternal counsellor of the Father, who of all speakers had the standing to freelance, deliberately did not. Which is the whole answer to any man who has ever opened his mouth in a pulpit or at a kitchen table and been tempted to say something interesting instead of something true.

Calvin — on the silence

And Calvin sees the other half too. Writing on how Christ obeyed, he turns to the same Isaiah text the Ethiopian was puzzling over, and treats the closed mouth as an act, not an absence:

Hence our Lord, after testifying, “I lay down my life for the sheep,” distinctly adds, “No man taketh it from me,” ( John 10:15 , 18 ). In the same sense Isaiah says, “ Like a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he opened not his mouth,” ( Is. 53:7 ).

Calvin, Institutes II.16 · Read the chapter →

Calvin’s point is that the silence was voluntary, the same way the death was voluntary. Nobody shut that mouth either. The man who opened it in Matthew 5:2 kept it shut in Matthew 27:14, and both times he was in complete command of it. That is a rarer thing than eloquence.

4. Confessional Anchor

The obvious objection to all of this is that you were not on the hill. If the decisive thing about Matthew 5:2 is a mouth opening, and that mouth closed twenty centuries ago, what exactly do you have? The confession answers in its very first paragraph:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 1, Paragraph 1

Therefore it pleased the Lord at various times and in different ways to reveal himself, and to declare his will to his church … to commit the same wholly to writing — which makes the Holy Scripture to be most necessary, those former ways of God's revealing his will to his people being now ceased.

1689 LBCF, ch. 1 · Read Chapter 1 →

Read that carefully. The former ways have ceased — there is no second hillside coming. But the reason they ceased is not that God went quiet. It is that what he said got written down, on purpose, so that it would survive the corruption of the flesh and the malice of Satan and reach a man in a kitchen in 2026. You are holding the transcript. That is not a downgrade from the hillside; the confession calls it the better preserving of the truth.

Which is also why the confession will not let anybody add a page to it:

1689 London Baptist Confession · Chapter 1, Paragraph 6

The whole counsel of God concerning all things necessary for his own glory, man's salvation, faith, and life, is either expressly set down or necessarily contained in the Holy Scripture; to which nothing at any time is to be added, whether by new revelation of the Spirit or traditions of men.

1689 LBCF, ch. 1 · Read Chapter 1 →

Then the catechism tells you how the transcript still works on a living man — and note that it does not say the Word is a good influence. It says the Spirit makes it do something:

The Baptist Catechism · Question 94

The Spirit of God makes the reading, but especially the preaching, of the Word an effectual means of convincing and converting sinners, and of building them up in holiness and comfort through faith to salvation.

The Baptist Catechism, Q94 · Work through the Catechism →

But especially the preaching. The catechism, seventeen centuries after the fact, still thinks the ordinary way this works is a mouth opening in front of people. That is not a coincidence of church furniture. It is Matthew 5:2 becoming the shape of every Lord’s Day since.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse sits in the Sermon on the Mount pack on the memorize page — Matthew 5 from the top, a chunk at a time. Carry it. It is worth committing precisely because it is the least quotable line in the chapter. Nobody puts “he opened his mouth” on a coffee mug. Learn it anyway, and you will never again be able to read the Beatitudes as if they floated in from nowhere.

He opened his mouth on purpose. Almost nothing that comes out of yours does. Most of a man’s speech is reaction — to a text message, to a tone, to being contradicted in front of somebody. Matthew records a mouth opening under total control, saying a prepared thing, at a chosen moment, to a chosen audience. Then in the same book he records that same mouth refusing to open when opening it would have been easy and self-serving. Between those two you have the entire discipline of the tongue, and there is no third option that involves saying whatever occurs to you.

What comes out is a report on what is inside. Matthew 12:34: out of the abundance of the heart, the mouth speaks. That cuts both ways and men usually only feel one of them. Yes, it means the thing you said in the truck at 6:40 this morning was not out of character — it was a reading. It also means that the fix is not tighter control of the mouth. You can clamp the mouth for about a week. The abundance is the actual problem, and the actual problem is what the next three chapters go to work on.

He taught. So learn something. The word Matthew chose assumes a learner who does not yet know the material. The catechism spells out what that costs:

The Baptist Catechism · Question 95

That the Word may become effectual to salvation, we must attend to it with diligence, preparation, and prayer; receive it with faith and love, lay it up in our hearts, and practice it in our lives.

The Baptist Catechism, Q95 · Work through the Catechism →

Count the verbs: attend, prepare, pray, receive, lay up, practice. Exactly one of them happens while the sermon is being delivered. The rest are yours, on your own time, during the week, where nobody is watching and nobody will ask.

Then open your own. The idiom Matthew uses is the same one Luke hands to Philip and to Peter at the two moments the gospel jumps a fence. Neither man had anything Jesus did not give him first. Proverbs 31:8 makes it a command to a king — open your mouth for the mute, in the cause of all who are left desolate — and it is not less binding on a man with a job and a family. There are people in your life who cannot speak for themselves, and there are people in your life who have never heard what was said on that hill. Both are waiting on somebody’s mouth to open.

And under all of it, the comfort. He did not have to say anything. He looked at the crowd first, and then he climbed, and then he sat, and then — without being asked, without being petitioned, without anybody deserving a syllable of it — he opened his mouth. The silence would have been just. The speech is grace.
