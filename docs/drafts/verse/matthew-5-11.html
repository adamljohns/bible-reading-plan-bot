Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:11

Verse 10 talked about them. Verse 11 turns and talks to you — and quietly swaps for righteousness’ sake for for my sake. It is the longest line in the list, the only one that names three separate things men will do to you, and the only beatitude with the word falsely holding the door.

“Blessed are you when people reproach you, persecute you, and say all kinds of evil against you falsely, for my sake.— World English Bible (public domain)

Blessed are ye, when men shall revile you, and persecute you, and shall say all manner of evil against you falsely, for my sake.— King James Version (public domain). The KJV margin glosses falsely: “Gr. lying.”

1. The Text and Its Words

Read it beside the line before it and the first thing that moves is the pronoun. Verses 3 through 10 are all third person — blessed are those, blessed are they, theirs is the kingdom. A man can listen to eight sentences like that from a safe distance, nodding at a category he has not yet decided to join. Verse 11 closes the distance in one word. Blessed are you. Jesus stops describing the kind of people the kingdom is made of and starts telling the men in front of him what is going to happen to them.

The second thing to notice is the conjunction. It is not if. It is when. The Greek is a temporal clause, not a conditional one; it does not raise a possibility, it fixes a time that has not been scheduled yet. Jesus is not saying should this ever befall you. He is saying on the day it does. Nobody in the sermon is offered the option of a life where this never comes up.

Third, count the verbs. There are three, and they are not three words for the same thing. Men will revile you — insult thrown in your face, loud enough to be heard by others. Men will persecute you — pursue you, chase you down, not let it drop when you leave the room. And men will say all manner of evil against you — speech about you, in rooms you are not in, entered into the record where you cannot reach it. The first you can hear. The second you can feel. The third you usually find out about months later from somebody who assumed you already knew.

Then the sentence lands two qualifiers at the end, and they carry the entire weight of it. The evil spoken is spoken falsely — and this is the only beatitude with that word in it. And the whole business happens for my sake. Take away either one and the verse blesses something it does not bless. Without falsely, it would comfort a man who earned every word said about him. Without for my sake, it would comfort a man who is simply hard to work with.

And that last phrase is the one that should stop a reader cold. Verse 10 says the cause is righteousness. Verse 11 says the cause is me. The two lines are laid on top of each other on purpose, and the second is a claim no prophet in Israel ever made about himself. Isaiah told men not to fear the reproach of men because they carried God’s law in their heart. Jesus tells men they are blessed when they are slandered on his account, and then in the next breath puts them in the same line as the prophets. Either that is the most self-regarding sentence in the sermon, or the man saying it is who he says he is. There is no third reading.

  • μακάριος makarios · “Blessed” · Strong’s G3107

    Not emotional happiness dependent on circumstances, but the deep well-being of those who are right with God. The Beatitudes open with this word and it governs every line in the list.

    The Greek word had a settled use before Jesus touched it: it described the gods, who lived above human trouble, and the dead, who were past it. Both meanings say the same thing — the blessed are the ones nothing can reach. Then this verse hands the word to a man who is being reached, hourly, by everyone around him. That is not a softening of the term. It is a claim that the thing which puts a man beyond harm is not distance from trouble but standing with God, and that a man can therefore be untouchable and bleeding at the same time. Every use of makarios in the Beatitudes is a verdict, not a mood. This one is announced over a man in the middle of the worst week of his life.

  • ὀνειδίζω oneidizō · “revile” · Strong’s G3679

    To throw reproach at a man — to heap insult so that his name is made small. Not a quiet disagreement. Public scorn.

    This is the first of the three verbs and it is the one nearly every believer will actually meet. It is face-to-face and it is meant to be overheard; the point of reviling is not to inform a man of your opinion but to lower him in front of the people whose opinion he cares about. Note the direction of the damage. Reviling does not take a man’s money or his freedom. It takes his standing. And it works by making him small enough that nothing he says afterward has to be answered — which is exactly why it is used against people who are saying something that would otherwise have to be answered.

  • διώκω diōkō · “persecute” · Strong’s G1377

    To pursue. The verb carries two senses in the New Testament — to pursue with hostile intent, and to pursue with earnest positive intent, to press toward, to strive after. Paul was once the great diōkō-persecutor of the church; he became the great diōkō-pursuer of the prize.

    The root idea is chase, and the chase is what distinguishes persecution from a bad afternoon. Anybody can be insulted once. Persecution is the insult that follows you — to the next job, into the next room, onto the next platform, after you have already conceded the argument and gone home. It implies intensity and sustained effort, never passive drift, and the same word is used of a man running hard after Christ. That double sense is not an accident of vocabulary. The energy is identical; only the direction is reversed. The man hunting you is spending exactly the kind of effort you are supposed to be spending on the kingdom.

  • πονηρός ponēros · “evil” · Strong’s G4190

    From ponos, labour or pain. Actively evil — not merely morally defective but malicious, harmful, wicked in intent and effect. It can describe evil people, evil deeds, or, as a substantive, the Evil One himself.

    The KJV renders the phrase all manner of evil, and the adjective is not the mild one. This is not misunderstanding, and it is not an unflattering but fair account. It is speech engineered to do harm and calculated to do it. That the same word serves as a title for Satan is worth sitting with for a minute, since the accuser’s trade is precisely this: manufactured charges against people God has already acquitted. When a man is being lied about for Christ’s sake, he is not merely dealing with unpleasant neighbours. He is downwind of an older operation.

  • ψεύδομαι pseudomai · “falsely” · Strong’s G5574

    To lie — to speak what is not true, to invent a charge. This is the word behind the KJV margin at Matthew 5:11: falsely: Gr. lying.

    Here is the guardrail, and it is placed exactly where a man is most likely to want it gone. The evil speech in this Beatitude is not a hard truth the disciple needed to hear. It is a lie told about him because of Christ. The blessing does not cover a man who earned the charge; it covers the man they had to lie about. Which means this verse issues every reader a test he can actually run, and it takes about five honest minutes. Is the thing they are saying about you true? If it is, you are not being persecuted. You are being reported on, and the right response is repentance, not endurance.

  • ἕνεκα heneka · “for my sake” · Strong’s G1752

    A causal and purposive preposition — for the sake of, on account of, because of. It introduces the reason behind an action: what a man suffers or acts for.

    The same preposition governs both halves of the pair. Verse 10 blesses the man persecuted heneka dikaiosynēs, for righteousness’ sake. Verse 11 blesses the man reviled heneken emou, on my account. Set the two side by side and the sermon has told you what it means by righteousness: not a free-standing ethical programme a man could adopt without Christ, but Christ’s own righteousness worked out in a life, so that hating the one and hating the other turn out to be the same act. The preposition is also why this beatitude cannot be claimed casually. It does not describe the suffering. It names the cause of it — and the identical insult, the identical firing, the identical silence in a room is blessed or simply earned depending on what follows this one small word.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 5:10 — Blessed are those who have been persecuted for righteousness’ sake, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven.

Matthew 5:12 — Rejoice, and be exceedingly glad, for great is your reward in heaven. For that is how they persecuted the prophets who were before you.

The beatitudes proper end at verse 10. Eight of them, all third person, opening with a beggar and closing with a fugitive, and the first and the last handed the identical deed — theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven. Verses 11 and 12 are not a ninth beatitude. They are the eighth one turned around to face the audience. Same subject, same cause, same promise; the only change is that Jesus is no longer talking about a category of people and has begun talking to a group of men who are sitting on a hillside listening to him and have not yet grasped what they have signed for.

That is why the verse expands where it does. Verse 10 gave the fact — persecution. Verse 11 gives the texture: what it will actually look like when it arrives at your address. Insults you can hear. Pursuit you cannot shake. And a version of you circulating in rooms you will never enter, assembled out of things that are not true. Most men brace for the second and are undone by the third.

Then verse 12 does something that has no parallel anywhere else in the list. Every other beatitude ends in a promise. This one ends in a command. Rejoice, and be exceedingly glad — two imperatives, stacked, and the second is the stronger of the two. And Jesus gives two reasons for the order, not one. The first is the ledger: great is your reward in heaven. The second is the company: that is how they persecuted the prophets who were before you. A man being lied about for Christ’s sake is not an unlucky outlier in the story of God’s people. He is standing in the main line of it, and the men on either side of him are Jeremiah and Elijah.

Luke preserves the same saying with the edges left sharper. In his sermon it runs: Blessed are you when men shall hate you, and when they shall exclude and mock you, and throw out your name as evil, for the Son of Man’s sake — and then, Rejoice in that day, and leap for joy, for behold, your reward is great in heaven, for their fathers did the same thing to the prophets. Note the two additions. Exclude names the specific mechanism most Western believers will meet: not violence, but removal from the room. And Luke supplies the mirror image four verses later, which Matthew leaves out and which every preacher should be made to read once a year: Woe, when men speak well of you, for their fathers did the same thing to the false prophets. Universal approval is not a neutral result. In this sermon it is a symptom.

And then, having just told them the world will hunt them and lie about them, Jesus says: you are the salt of the earth, you are the light of the world, a city set on a hill cannot be hidden. The order forecloses the obvious escape. The right answer to verse 11 is not to get quieter, smaller, harder to find. It is to keep the light where people can see it and let the record be assembled by whoever wants to assemble it.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin is unusually useful on this verse for a reason that has nothing to do with exegetical technique. He treats the loss of a good name as a real loss. He does not tell a slandered man that reputation does not matter and he should rise above it. He tells him it matters a great deal, that he has genuinely been robbed, and then shows him where the compensation is kept.

Start with how he ranks the damage. In the chapter on bearing the cross he lists the ordinary calamities and refuses to pretend any of them are pleasant:

Poverty, indeed considered in itself, is misery; so are exile, contempt, imprisonment, ignominy: in fine, death itself is the last of all calamities.

Calvin, Institutes III.8

Contempt and ignominy are on that list, sitting between imprisonment and death, and nobody has told the reader they belong further down. Then Calvin goes further and says something most men will not say out loud about themselves:

as to ingenuous natures, it is more bitter to suffer disgrace than a hundred deaths, Paul expressly reminds us that not only persecution, but also disgrace awaits us, because we trust in the living God

Calvin, Institutes III.8

More bitter than a hundred deaths. That is not rhetorical excess; it is an observation about a certain kind of man, and if you are that kind you already know it. You would take the beating. What you cannot take is being described, accurately in tone and falsely in substance, to people whose respect you wanted. Calvin’s point is that the apostle saw this coming and named it separately from persecution, precisely because it is a distinct wound.

His remedy is not a thicker skin. It is a competing testimony:

Let us then be contented with the testimony of Christ rather than with the false estimate of the flesh, and then, after the example of the Apostles, we will rejoice in being counted worthy to suffer shame for his name

Calvin, Institutes III.8

Two verdicts are in evidence and they contradict each other. One is the false estimate of the flesh, assembled by men who had to lie to assemble it. The other is the testimony of Christ, which is what verse 11 actually is — a spoken verdict, in the present tense, over the man being lied about. Calvin does not ask the reader to stop caring what is said about him. He asks him to notice that a higher court has already ruled, and to be contented with that ruling. And the shame, once reclassified, becomes an honour: counted worthy.

He then says the same thing from the other end, and this is the sentence that gives persecution its place in the Christian life rather than treating it as an interruption of it:

this form of the cross is the most appropriate to believers, being that by which Christ desires to be glorified in us

Calvin, Institutes III.8

The most appropriate. Not the exceptional case, not the missionary’s hazard — the fitting one, the form of the cross that suits the shape of the men carrying it, because it is the form in which Christ has chosen to be made visible in them.

The same sin, from the other side of the table

Calvin’s most valuable contribution to this verse is not in the chapter on suffering at all. It is in his exposition of the ninth commandment, where he is describing the sin from the perspective of the man committing it. The disciple in Matthew 5:11 is on the receiving end of a breach of the ninth commandment, and Calvin defines that breach with a precision the reader can use as a diagnostic:

by evil-speaking, we understand not the rebuke which is administered with a view of correcting; not accusation or judicial decision, by which evil is sought to be remedied; not public censure, which tends to strike terror into other offenders; not the disclosure made to those whose safety depends on being forewarned, lest unawares they should be brought into danger, but the odious crimination which springs from a malicious and petulant love of slander.

Calvin, Institutes II.8

Read the four exemptions before the comma that matters. Correction is not slander. Formal accusation is not slander. Public censure of a public offence is not slander. Warning people who are about to get hurt is not slander. A man who has been rebuked, charged, censured or warned about has not been handed Matthew 5:11; he has been handed something he needs to deal with. What the commandment forbids — and what this verse describes being done to the disciple — is the invented charge that comes out of a settled appetite for tearing people down.

And Calvin will not let anyone treat the resulting damage as trivial:

if a good name is more precious than riches, a man, in being robbed of his good name, is no less injured than if he were robbed of his goods

Calvin, Institutes II.8

Robbed. Not disappointed, not embarrassed — robbed, and of something he is told plainly is worth more than his money. Verse 11 is therefore not a verse about hurt feelings. It is a verse about theft, and the man who has been robbed is told that the estate he actually lives on was never in that account.

One more line from the same passage, because it names the modern instrument exactly:

the commandment extends so far as to include that scurrilous affected urbanity, instinct with invective, by which the failings of others, under an appearance of sportiveness, are bitterly assailed

Calvin, Institutes II.8

Under an appearance of sportiveness. That is the joke that is not a joke, the affectionate-sounding line with a blade in it, the quote-tweet that is only ever kidding until somebody objects. Calvin filed it under the ninth commandment four and a half centuries early. Which means it is also on the list of things a man reviled for Christ’s sake is forbidden to pick up and return.

Finally, Calvin makes an argument from this verse’s exact situation that runs all the way out to the last day. Writing about what a man ought to conclude when he watches the world work:

when we see the righteous brought low by the ungodly, attacked with injuries, buried under slanders, and torn by insult and abuse — while, on the other hand, the wicked flourish, prosper, and gain ease and honor, all without punishment — we ought at once to conclude that there will be a life to come in which wickedness will receive its punishment and righteousness its reward.

Calvin, Institutes I.5

Buried under slanders, and the liars comfortable. Calvin says the honest response to that spectacle is not to demand that God settle it this quarter but to conclude that the books are not closed. This is precisely the logic of verse 12. Great is your reward in heaven is not a change of subject after verse 11. It is the answer to it — the assurance that there is an accounting, that the false version of you now in circulation will be read aloud beside the true one, and that the man who assembled it will be there when it is.

4. Confessional Anchor

Neither the confession nor the catechism has a chapter on being slandered. What they have is the doctrine underneath every clause of this verse: a commandment that names exactly what is being done to the disciple, and an account of who he is that explains why it is being done to him at all.

Start with the commandment, because the catechism will not let the reader treat his reputation as a spiritual irrelevance:

The ninth commandment requires the maintaining and promoting of truth between man and man, and of our own and our neighbor's good name, especially in witness bearing.

The Baptist Catechism, Question 82

The ninth commandment forbids whatever is prejudicial to the truth, or injurious to our own or our neighbor's good name.

The Baptist Catechism, Question 83

Two things fall out of that, and they cut in opposite directions, which is how you know it is not sentiment. First, the men in Matthew 5:11 are not merely being unkind. They are breaking one of the ten words, and they are breaking it against a man whose good name God has placed under his own protection. Second — and this is the clause men skip — the commandment requires the maintaining of our own good name too. A believer is not commanded to be indifferent to slander. He is permitted, even obliged, to tell the truth about himself. What he is not permitted is to make that defence the business of his life, because the same catechism has already told him where his standing actually comes from, and it is not from the verdict of the room.

Then the confession answers the question the verse raises and never explains: why for my sake? Why should a lie told about a carpenter’s apprentice in Galilee be counted as a lie told for Christ’s account? The chapter on adoption gives the reason in eleven words:

They have his name put upon them, receive the Spirit of adoption, have access to the throne of grace with boldness, and are enabled to cry, Abba, Father.

1689 LBCF, ch. 12 — Of Adoption

His name put upon them. That is the whole mechanism. The disciple is not being insulted for holding opinions that happen to resemble Christ’s. He is carrying a name that was placed on him, and the insult lands on the name. Which is why Peter can say that the men doing it are blaspheming on their part while Christ is glorified on yours, and why the apostles could walk out of a flogging counting it an honour: the dishonour was not, finally, theirs to absorb.

The confession states the union that makes this more than a legal fiction:

All saints who are united to Jesus Christ their head by his Spirit and by faith — although not thereby made one person with him — have fellowship in his graces, sufferings, death, resurrection, and glory.

1689 LBCF, ch. 27 — Of the Communion of Saints

Note the guardrail the divines put in the middle of their own sentence: although not thereby made one person with him. The believer is not absorbed into Christ and his sufferings do not atone for anything. But the fellowship is real, and it is a fellowship in the whole list — graces, sufferings, death, resurrection, glory. You do not get to take the first and last items and leave the middle. A man being lied about for Christ’s sake has been let into the second item on that list, which is the item his Lord went through first and came out the far side of.

And the confession is realistic about what that costs in public. Writing about the church in the world, it grants the worst and then refuses the conclusion:

Christ always has had, and ever shall have, a kingdom in this world unto the end of it, of such as believe in him and make profession of his name.

1689 LBCF, ch. 26 — Of the Church

Make profession of his name. Profession is public by definition; there is no such thing as a private one. The kingdom in this world is made of people who have said out loud whose they are, which is the precise act that draws the fire in Matthew 5:11. The confession is not describing two groups — the professing and the persecuted. It is describing one.

The verse, in the confession’s own words

Say all manner of evil against you falsely — a breach of the ninth commandment, which forbids whatever is prejudicial to the truth, or injurious to our own or our neighbor’s good name.

For my sake — because they have his name put upon them, and have fellowship in his graces, sufferings, death, resurrection, and glory.

Blessed are you — a verdict already entered over men who make profession of his name, whatever the room has decided.

5. For the Man Reading This

Run the word falsely first, and run it honestly. Before you claim this verse, answer one question: is what they are saying about you true? Not is it fair, not is it the whole picture, not would they say it about anyone else — is it true. If it is, you are not being persecuted; you are being reported on, and the way out is not endurance but repentance. This is the most common misuse of Matthew 5:11 and it is a comfortable one, because a man who has decided he is being persecuted never has to change anything. Five honest minutes and one trusted friend will settle it. Do that before you do anything else in this list.

Then run for my sake. Calvin’s four exemptions are the working checklist: correction, formal accusation, public censure, and honest warning are not slander. Neither, we can add, is being disliked for being abrasive, for being unable to let a thing go, for saying the true thing in the cruellest available way, or for inserting yourself where you had no business. The verse does not bless a man reviled for his temperament, his politics, or his hobby-horse. It blesses a man reviled because he will not let go of Christ. Those are frequently not the same, and most of us have to be told so by somebody else.

Expect the first verb and stop waiting for the third. Insult, pursuit, and the lie behind your back are listed in that order, and for most men in this country the first is the whole of it — the room that goes flat, the joke at your expense in the group chat, the tone somebody takes when your name comes up. Do not disqualify that because it is not a prison cell. Calvin put contempt on the same list as imprisonment. And do not inflate it either. It is what it is: the ordinary, sub-dramatic form of a thing Jesus said would come.

The lie will hurt more than the loss, and you are allowed to say so. More bitter to some men than a hundred deaths — that is Calvin, not a therapist. You are not required to perform serenity you do not have. Grieve the theft of your name out loud, to somebody, and then keep standing. Pretending it does not sting is not faith. It is a performance, and performances are expensive to maintain over a long season.

Make your case once, plainly, and then let it go. The ninth commandment requires you to maintain your own good name, so you are not obliged to stay silent while a lie runs. Tell the truth about yourself, in the right room, to the people who actually need it. Then stop. The moment defending your name becomes the organising project of your year, the slander has taken more from you than it originally stole. There is a court that will hear this. It is not the one you are currently appealing to.

Do not return fire in kind. The instrument that will be used on you — the joke with a blade in it, invective under an appearance of sportiveness — will be lying right there on the ground, and it works. Picking it up costs you the verse. A man who answers slander with slander has not been robbed of his good name; he has traded it. Answer with the facts, or answer with silence, and leave the other tool where it fell.

Watch the woe as carefully as the blessing. Luke records the other half: woe when all men speak well of you, because that is how their fathers treated the false prophets. Universal approval is not proof of faithfulness and it is not neutral. If nothing about your life has ever cost you standing with anyone, that is not evidence you have been gracious. It may be evidence that nobody has yet been able to tell what you actually believe.

Rejoice is an order, and it is about a ledger. Verse 12 does not ask how you feel; it tells you what to do, and it supplies the two reasons. There is a reward, and it is great, and it is somewhere the slanderer cannot reach. And there is a line of men ahead of you who got the same treatment for the same reason. Rejoicing here is not manufactured cheerfulness. It is the reasonable response of a man who has read the accounts and found that he is up, not down.

Find the men in the line with you. The apostles left the council rejoicing, and they left together. Nothing in this verse is designed to be carried alone, and slander does most of its damage in isolation, where there is no one to say that is not true and I have known you fifteen years. If you are the only man you know paying anything for this, that is a problem to fix this month.

In the Sermon on the Mount memorize pack — carry it. It is the verse to have ready on the day you find out what has been said about you in a room you were not in.

And keep the shape of it straight. Jesus is not telling you that being lied about is good. He is telling you that when men have to invent something in order to have a charge against you, they have testified to what you are without meaning to — and that the verdict which decides your case was handed down on a hillside in Galilee before any of them opened their mouths. The world gets to write the account. It does not get to enter the judgment.
