Verse Study · Luke

Luke 9:23

Luke adds one word Matthew and Mark do not, and it changes the whole demand from a moment into a habit.

He said to all, “If anyone desires to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me.— World English Bible (public domain)

And he said to them all, If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily , and follow me.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Three imperatives, in a fixed order. Deny yourself. Take up your cross. Follow me. The order is not decorative — each one makes the next possible, and reversing them produces a different religion.

Start with who is addressed. Luke says he said to all. This is not a private word to the Twelve about the special cost of apostleship. Luke is deliberate about it, and the effect is to remove the escape hatch by which ordinary believers assign the hard sayings to professionals.

  • ἀπαρνέομαι Aparneomai · “let him deny” · Strong’s G533

    The Greek verb aparneomai (a strengthened form of arneomai, G720) means to deny completely, to disown, to renounce, to repudiate. The prefix apo- intensifies the denial — not merely declining something but actively distancing oneself from it.

    Aparneomai is arneomai (to deny) with the prefix intensified — to disown completely, to repudiate, to actively distance oneself from. It is the same verb Luke uses later in this Gospel for what Peter does to Jesus in the courtyard. That is a deliberate and uncomfortable echo. Peter denies Christ; the disciple is called to do to himself what Peter did to his Lord.

    Which tells you what self-denial is not. It is not giving something up for a season, and it is not low self-esteem. It is disowning the self as the authority — refusing to answer to it, the way Peter refused to be known as Jesus' man.

  • σταυρός stauros · “cross” · Strong’s G4716

    The Roman cross — instrument of execution and, in Christian theology, the pivot of history. Paul boasts in nothing except the cross (Gal 6:14) and determines to know nothing but Christ crucified (1 Cor 2:2).

    Stauros meant one thing to a first-century audience and it was not jewelry. A man carrying a crossbeam through the streets was a condemned man walking to his execution, already sentenced, already publicly disgraced. There was no version of that image that ended in survival.

    So take up your cross is not a phrase about enduring an irritation. We have domesticated it — a difficult boss, a chronic ache, “my cross to bear.” Jesus is describing a man who has accepted a death sentence and is carrying the instrument.

  • ἡμέρα hēmera · “daily” · Strong’s G2250

    The basic word for a day — whether a literal 24-hour period, the daylight portion (as opposed to night), or a broader era/period ('in the days of Herod,' 'the day of the Lord'). It appears over 380 times in the NT, structuring narratives ('on the third day'), marking eras ('the last days'), and pointing to eschatological culmination ('that day').

    Here is Luke’s addition. Matthew and Mark record the saying without it; Luke writes daily. And that single word converts the image from a heroic moment into a routine.

    It is also, oddly, a mercy. A one-time cross would demand a single act of total courage that most men do not have. A daily cross asks for something smaller and much harder to fake: the same decision again tomorrow, on an ordinary Tuesday, with no audience.

  • ἀκολουθέω akoloutheō · “follow” · Strong’s G190

    To follow someone on a road, to accompany as a companion. In the Gospels, akoloutheō becomes the technical term for discipleship — following Jesus means committing your entire life direction to Him, not just walking behind.

    Akoloutheo is to follow on a road, to accompany. In the Gospels it becomes the technical term for discipleship, and its ordinariness is the point. After the drama of denial and crucifixion, the third command is simply: walk behind Him. The Christian life ends up looking less like a series of grand renunciations and more like a long walk in someone’s tracks.

2. Where It Sits

The placement in Luke is everything. Immediately before this, Jesus asks the disciples who the crowds say He is, and then who they say He is. Peter answers: the Christ of God. Jesus then tells them, for the first time, that the Son of Man must suffer many things, be rejected, be killed, and be raised.

Verse 23 follows directly. The order is confession, then cross for Him, then cross for them. Jesus does not ask anyone to carry a cross until He has said He will carry one first, and He does not explain the terms of discipleship until the disciples know who He is.

And immediately after: whoever will save his life shall lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake shall save it. Then a question — what is a man profited if he gains the whole world and loses himself? The whole paragraph is an argument about arithmetic. Jesus is not asking for a sacrifice that leaves you poorer. He is telling you the exchange rate.

Eight days later, Luke puts the Transfiguration. The men who heard this saying were shown, within the week, what the Man giving the order actually looks like.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition read this saying under the heading of mortification — the daily putting to death of what remains of the old man — and was careful to keep it out of the foundation. Self-denial in this tradition is never the price of acceptance; it is the shape of a life already accepted.

That guard matters, because the verse is easily turned into a works-religion with better vocabulary. Read as a condition of salvation, it produces men who are constantly auditing their own sincerity. Read as the description of a disciple, it produces men who get up and follow again.

The tradition also refused the monastic reading — that the cross means withdrawal from ordinary life. Luke’s daily points the other way. The cross is carried at work, in a marriage, with children, in the same week as everyone else’s.

4. Confessional Anchor

The dominion of the whole body of sin is destroyed, and the several lusts thereof are more and more weakened and mortified, and they more and more quickened and strengthened in all saving graces, to the practice of all true holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord.

1689 LBCF, ch. 13 — Of Sanctification

More and more weakened and mortified is the confession’s version of Luke’s daily. Not a single execution but a long one; not a decisive act of the will but a slow campaign in which ground is taken gradually.

Notice the pairing, though. Weakened and quickened. Mortification never stands alone in this tradition — something is being put to death precisely because something else is being strengthened. A self-denial that only subtracts produces a hollow man. Jesus' third command is what keeps it from being subtraction: follow me means there is somewhere to go.

5. For the Man Reading This

Watch for the two counterfeits, because both are common and both feel spiritual.

The first is calling your circumstances your cross. The illness, the difficult in-laws, the job you dislike — these are real burdens and God is in them, but you did not take them up and you cannot put them down. A cross in this verse is chosen. It is the thing you take up, which means it is something you could have declined.

The second is heroic self-denial as a substitute for obedience. It is much easier to give something up dramatically than to do the ordinary thing you are avoiding — the apology owed, the conversation with your son, the hour of work you keep deferring. Grand renunciation can be a way of not following.

So make it daily and make it specific. Ask what, today, you are being asked to disown — not in general, not for Lent. Today. Then carry that, and walk behind Him. The measure of this verse is never how much you gave up. It is whether you are still following on Thursday.

In the Foundations memorize pack — carry it.
