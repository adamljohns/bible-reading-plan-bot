Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 22:36-39

A man asks Jesus to rank the law. Jesus answers with two sentences he did not write, and neither of them is a rule about behaviour.

“Teacher, which is the greatest commandment in the law?” Jesus said to him, “‘You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind.’ This is the first and great commandment. A second likewise is this, ‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself.’— World English Bible (public domain)

Master, which is the great commandment in the law? Jesus said unto him, Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Start with what Jesus does not do. He does not compose an answer. A lawyer asks him to name the greatest commandment in the law, and he replies by quoting two verses the man had known since he was a boy — Deuteronomy 6:5, the Shema he recited daily, and Leviticus 19:18, a line buried in the middle of a chapter about gleaning fields and paying wages on time. Nothing new is issued here. Jesus tells a professional student of the law what the law he already owned was always for. That is a far harder answer than a new rule would have been, because a new rule can be added to a schedule. A summary cannot. It audits everything already on it.

Then notice the arithmetic. The question asks for one; the answer gives two, and it refuses to grade them apart. The second is not a footnote appended out of generosity. Jesus calls it homoios — of the same kind. One command with two objects, or two commands cut from the same cloth, but never a major and a minor.

And notice what kind of thing is being commanded. The lawyer wanted a hierarchy of statutes. He got a hierarchy of affections. Every verb in the answer is the verb to love. There is no observable act in the sentence at all — no offering, no Sabbath, no tithe. The greatest thing God requires of a man is something that cannot be inspected from outside, and the whole visible law is downstream of it.

  • ἐντολή entolē · “commandment” · Strong's G1785 · noun, feminine

    Our lexicon keeps this word to its essentials: divine order and instruction. Note the shape of the lawyer's question — he asks which single entolē stands highest within the nomos, the law as a whole. He is asking for a ranking inside a body of legislation he already accepts. And the same lexicon entry points at the place this word finally lands: John 14:15, if you love me, keep my commandments. In Christ's own vocabulary command and love are welded together, which is exactly why he can answer a question about commandments with an answer about love and not change the subject.

  • μέγας megas · “great” · Strong's G3173 · adjective  /  πρῶτος prōtos · “first” · Strong's G4413 · adjective

    Megas is greatness of size, degree, importance or intensity — the word behind our prefix mega-. That is the word the lawyer chose: which command is the biggest. Jesus accepts the word and then adds one the lawyer did not use. Prōtos is the superlative of pro, before: first in time, first in rank, first in priority, and its opposite is eschatos, last. So the answer is not merely that this command weighs the most. It is that this command comes first, and everything else lines up behind it in order. A man can agree that loving God is the weightiest duty and still put it fourth on the list. Jesus closes that door.

  • ἀγαπάω agapaō · “thou shalt love” · Strong's G25 · verb

    Our lexicon defines it as love that is not a passing feeling but a settled will to seek another's good. That definition is doing heavy lifting here, because a passing feeling cannot be commanded and a settled will can. This is the same verb used of the Father giving the Son in John 3:16, of Christ's self-giving in Galatians 2:20, and of the husband's charge in Ephesians 5:25. It is, as the entry puts it, both gift and command — first received, then practised. Which means the order of the whole Christian life is already inside the word: nobody generates this love to qualify for it. 1 John 4:19 keeps the sequence straight — we love him, because he first loved us.

  • ὅλος holos · “all” · Strong's G3650 · adjective

    Three times in one clause, and it is the most dangerous word in the verse. Holos means the whole, the totality, the entirety — and our lexicon marks the distinction that matters: it stresses completeness rather than quantity, the unity of the whole rather than the count of the parts, unlike pas, which means every and all in the sense of individual members. English gets holo- from it, as in holograph and holistic. So the command is not for a large percentage of the heart. It is for the heart as an undivided thing. There is no arithmetic in which a man supplies most of it and holds a remainder back, because a divided whole is not a smaller whole; it is a different object entirely.

  • καρδία kardia · “heart” · Strong's G2588 · ψυχή psychē · “soul” · Strong's G5590 · διάνοια dianoia · “mind” · Strong's G1271

    Read as a psychology this list will mislead you; read as an inventory it will not. Kardia in our lexicon is the inner person, the centre of the will — not the seat of sentiment but the place decisions are actually made. Psychē, the word we get psychology from, carries life, the self, the inner person, and the self that outlasts the body; it is the word in Christ's question about gaining the world and forfeiting your psychē. Dianoia is dia plus nous, thinking through — the mind in its working capacity, moral reasoning, the deep orientation of thought and imagination. Our entry notes what that inclusion settles: faith engages the intellect and not merely the emotions. Three words, no gaps. Matthew lists three; Mark 12:30 and Luke 10:27 add strength. The point of the piling-up is not taxonomy. It is that a man reading the list is meant to fail to find the compartment that was exempted.

  • ὅμοιος homoios · “is like unto it” · Strong's G3664 · adjective

    Our lexicon puts it in five words: like — of the same kind, not a cheaper copy. This is Matthew's own hinge word; he stacks it through chapter 13, where the kingdom of heaven is like unto a mustard seed, leaven, treasure in a field, a merchant, a net, a householder. In none of those does the word mean roughly comparable to. It means: this is what the thing actually is. So when Jesus says the second command is homoios to the first, he is not being polite about a lesser duty. He is saying the two are of one kind, from one source, aimed by one authority. Which is why the New Testament can turn round and say the whole second table is summed up in it — Romans 13:9 gathers adultery, murder, theft and covetousness under this one line, and James 2:8 calls it the royal law.

  • πλησίον plesion · “neighbour” · Strong's G4139 · adverb / noun

    Literally the near one — somebody close by geography or by circumstance. That is all the word carries on its own, and it is not a moral category; it is a position report. Our lexicon tracks what Jesus then does with it in the parable of the Good Samaritan: the neighbour is not defined by ethnicity, religion or proximity, and the question “Who is my neighbour?” gets turned inside out into “Who are you being a neighbour to?” The entry's summary is worth carrying: plesion is not the person next door, but every human being who crosses your path with a need you have the power to meet. The lawyer standing in front of Jesus was, at that moment, the nearest one.

One phrase has no Strong's entry worth linking and still decides the whole second command: as yourself. It is not a licence to work up self-esteem first and then love others out of the surplus. It is a measurement. Whatever you already, reliably, instinctively do for your own comfort, safety, reputation and future — that is the unit. You do not have to be taught to feed yourself when hungry or to defend yourself when accused. The command takes that existing, uncoached competence and points it at someone else.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 22:15 — Then the Pharisees went and took counsel how they might entrap him in his talk.

Matthew 22:23 — On that day Sadducees (those who say that there is no resurrection) came to him. They asked him,

Matthew 22:34 — But the Pharisees, when they heard that he had silenced the Sadducees, gathered themselves together.

Matthew 22:35 — One of them, a lawyer, asked him a question, testing him.

Matthew 22:40 — The whole law and the prophets depend on these two commandments.”

Matthew 22:46 — No one was able to answer him a word, neither did any man dare ask him any more questions from that day forward.

This is not a seminar. Matthew 22 is a day of ambushes in the temple courts during the last week of Jesus' life, and the chapter reads like a relay. The Pharisees send their disciples with the Herodians to ask about paying taxes to Caesar — a fork with a cliff on both sides. The Sadducees follow with the trick question about seven brothers and one widow, designed to make the resurrection look absurd. Both parties leave: one marvelling, one silenced. Then the Pharisees regroup and send a lawyer, and Matthew tells us plainly why — testing him.

The trap in this third question is subtler than the other two and better aimed. Ask a man to name the greatest command and whatever he names, he has demoted everything else. Name a ceremonial law and the moralists have him; name a moral law and the temple party has him; and either way an unschooled Galilean has just publicly ranked the Torah, which is a charge waiting to be written up. There is no safe answer available at the level the question was asked.

So Jesus does not answer at that level. He answers with Scripture, twice, and then does something the lawyer did not ask for: he tells him what holds the rest of it up. On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets, as the King James has it — the picture is a door on its hinges. Take the hinges out and you do not have a slightly weaker door. You have a slab of wood on the floor.

Two things sit either side of this exchange and both matter. Immediately after it, Jesus stops answering and starts asking: whose son is the Christ? The Pharisees say David's; he presses them on Psalm 110, where David calls his own son Lord — and nobody can answer, and nobody dares ask him anything again. The great commandment is not the climax of the chapter. The identity of the man giving it is. And immediately after that comes chapter 23, the woes, where he tells scribes who tithe their garden herbs that they have left undone the weightier matters of the law — justice, mercy and faith. That is what a man looks like who keeps the details and misses the sum.

Behind it all stands the Old Testament, which never treated this as a new idea. Moses had already reduced the whole requirement to fear, walk, love and serve with all the heart and soul (Deuteronomy 10:12). And Moses had already conceded the problem: Deuteronomy 30:6 promises that the LORD will circumcise the heart of his people so that they may love him with all their heart and soul. The command and the promised ability to keep it are in the same book, in the same hand. That is the only reason this verse is good news and not just an indictment.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin has no commentary on Matthew held on this site, so nothing here is put in his mouth from one. But his exposition of the moral law in the Institutes runs straight through this verse, and he begins where Jesus begins — with why there are two tables and why they come in this order.

God thus divided his Law into two parts, containing a complete rule of righteousness, that he might assign the first place to the duties of religion which relate especially to His worship, and the second to the duties of charity which have respect to man. The first foundation of righteousness undoubtedly is the worship of God. When it is subverted, all the other parts of righteousness, like a building rent asunder, and in ruins, are racked and scattered.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 — read the chapter

Racked and scattered, not merely weakened. That is the answer to every generation that keeps the second command and quietly retires the first — the decent, humane, godless man who is kind to his neighbours and owes nothing to heaven. Calvin's claim is that the second table is not free-standing masonry. Pull the foundation and the walls do not stand shorter. They come down. Later in the same chapter he states the direction of travel in a single sentence:

First, our mind must be completely filled with love to God, and then this love must forthwith flow out toward our neighbour.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 — read the chapter

But he is honest enough to raise the obvious objection against himself, and he raises it in the words a modern reader would use. If Scripture keeps testing men by how they treat other men, is neighbour-love not the real business, with God-love as the pious framing?

Is it then true, you will ask, that it is a more complete summary of righteousness to live innocently with men, than piously towards God? By no means; but because no man, as a matter of course, observes charity in all respects, unless he seriously fear God, such observance is a proof of piety also.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 — read the chapter

A proof, not a substitute. And there is a reason God arranges the test this way, which Calvin puts with characteristic bluntness:

the Lord, well knowing that none of our good deeds can reach him … does not demand from us duties towards himself, but exercises us in good works towards our neighbour.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 — read the chapter

God is not short of anything you could bring him. So he stations the practice ground where the need actually is. From which Calvin draws the plainest rule of life in the chapter:

Let us therefore hold, that our life will be framed in best accordance with the will of God, and the requirements of his Law, when it is, in every respect, most advantageous to our brethren.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 — read the chapter

And on why the man in front of you has that claim, he grounds it not in the man's merit but in two facts he cannot lose:

Scripture notes a twofold equity on which this commandment is founded. Man is both the image of God and our flesh. Wherefore, if we would not violate the image of God, we must hold the person of man sacred—if we would not divest ourselves of humanity we must cherish our own flesh.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 — read the chapter

Now the hard part, and Calvin does not flinch from it. Men who find the great commandment sweet have generally not read it slowly. He is arguing against people who claimed that loving your enemy was too heavy a burden to be a real command, and he demolishes them by comparison:

As if any thing could be imagined more difficult than to love the Lord with all the heart, and soul, and strength. Compared with this Law, there is none which may not seem easy, whether it be to love our enemy, or to banish every feeling of revenge from our minds. To our weakness, indeed, every thing, even to the minutest tittle of the Law, is arduous and difficult. In the Lord we have strength. It is his to give what he orders, and to order what he wills.

Calvin, Institutes II.8 — read the chapter

That last line is the hinge of the whole study. It is his to give what he orders, and to order what he wills. The command is not calibrated to your current capacity, and it was never meant to be. Elsewhere, writing about Christian liberty, Calvin takes the same verse and measures a serious believer against it:

To accomplish this, the soul must previously be divested of every other thought and feeling, the heart purified from all its desires, all its powers collected and united on this one object. Those who, in comparison of others, have made much progress in the way of the Lord, are still very far from this goal.

Calvin, Institutes III.19 — read the chapter

For the law demanding perfect love condemns all imperfection, unless its rigor is mitigated.

Calvin, Institutes III.19 — read the chapter

Read those two together and you have the honest verdict: nobody reading this page has kept Matthew 22:37 for a single waking hour. If that is where it stopped, the verse would be a sentence of death with a nice cadence. It does not stop there. Calvin's resolution is not to lower the standard but to change who is receiving the work — a servant dares not appear before a master with a half-finished task, but a son hands his father the botched thing anyway:

Such should be our feelings, as we certainly trust that our most indulgent Parent will approve our services, however small they may be, and however rude and imperfect.

Calvin, Institutes III.19 — read the chapter

4. Confessional Anchor

The Baptist Catechism does not treat this verse as one text among many. It uses it as the definition of the law itself, and cites Matthew 22:37-40 as its proof:

Q47. What is the sum of the ten commandments?

The sum of the ten commandments is to love the Lord our God with all our heart, with all our soul, with all our strength, and with all our mind; and our neighbour as ourselves.

The Baptist Catechism, Q47

That placement settles an argument before it starts. Two questions earlier the catechism has said that the moral law is summarily comprehended in the ten commandments; now it says the ten commandments are summarily comprehended in this. So love is not an alternative to the commandments for people who find the commandments cold, and the commandments are not a fallback for people who find love vague. They are the same requirement at two magnifications. The confession draws the same two-table architecture Jesus and Calvin both assume:

The same law that was first written in the heart of man continued to be a perfect rule of righteousness after the fall, and was delivered by God upon Mount Sinai in ten commandments, written in two tables — the first four containing our duty toward God, and the other six our duty to man.

1689 LBCF, ch. 19, para. 2 — Of the Law of God

Four and six. God first, and then man — the same order Jesus gives the lawyer, and the same order Calvin refuses to let anyone reverse. And lest anyone read grace as an exemption, the confession forecloses that too:

The moral law forever binds all, as well justified persons as others, to the obedience thereof, and that not only in regard to the matter contained in it, but also in respect of the authority of God the Creator, who gave it. Neither does Christ in the gospel in any way dissolve this obligation, but much strengthen it.

1689 LBCF, ch. 19, para. 5 — Of the Law of God

Much strengthen it. The man who says he is under grace and therefore this verse is optional has misread both words. But the confession also knows what happens to an honest man who takes the measure of himself against this standard, and it does not leave him there:

It discovers also the sinful pollutions of their nature, hearts, and lives, so that examining themselves by it they may come to further conviction of, humiliation for, and hatred against sin — together with a clearer sight of their need of Christ and the perfection of his obedience.

1689 LBCF, ch. 19, para. 6 — Of the Law of God

Neither are the forementioned uses of the law contrary to the grace of the gospel, but do sweetly comply with it — the Spirit of Christ subduing and enabling the will of man to do that freely and cheerfully which the will of God, revealed in the law, requires to be done.

1689 LBCF, ch. 19, para. 7 — Of the Law of God

There is the whole doctrine in two paragraphs. The command shows you what you are and drives you to Christ; the Spirit of Christ then works in you the very obedience the command asks for — and works it freely and cheerfully, not by pressure. That is Deuteronomy 30:6 stated as confessional prose. The circumcised heart is not a metaphor for trying harder.

5. For the Man Reading This

This verse is in the PROVEN Men memorize pack — carry it. It is a strange one to carry, because most memory verses hand you a promise and this one hands you a standard. That is exactly its use. A man in a fight with his own appetites does not need another slogan about victory. He needs to know what the target actually is, and why the thing he is being pulled toward is not a small infraction but a rival claim on the first place.

The order is not negotiable, and inverting it is the most respectable way to break the law. A man can be a decent husband, a fair boss and a good neighbour with no love for God at all, and the world will call him better than he is. Calvin's building comes down anyway. If you are going to fail here, fail knowing which table you abandoned.

Name the compartment. Holos is the word to pray over. Every man has a room he keeps for himself — the money, the phone, the ambition, the grudge, the hour nobody knows about. The command does not ask you to shrink that room. It denies the room exists. Loving God with a reserved wing is not partial obedience; it is a different thing altogether, and you already know its name.

Feelings are not the evidence, and their absence is not the excuse. Since agapaō is a settled will to seek another's good, “I do not feel it today” does not get you out and “I felt it Sunday” does not get you in. Ask instead where your mind goes when it is not directed, what you spend on, what you protect. That is the will reporting honestly.

Your neighbour is assigned, not selected. Plesion means the near one. Not the deserving one, not the one you chose, not the one who will thank you. The wife in the next room, the man on your crew who talks too much, the neighbour whose politics you cannot stand. Proximity is the whole qualification, and mercy is the whole job.

The measure is your own reliable self-interest. You do not schedule time to care about your own reputation or comfort; it happens by default. Take one thing you would automatically do for yourself this week — make the call, get the rest, defend the name — and do it for one other person on purpose. That is the command at a size a Tuesday can hold.

And when the standard flattens you, let it. You will not keep this. Nobody in this study's chain of witnesses claimed to. The confession says the law is meant to give you a clearer sight of your need of Christ and the perfection of his obedience — his, not yours. The one man who loved God with all his heart, soul and mind, and his neighbour as himself, was standing in the temple that Tuesday answering a trick question, and by Friday he had loved his neighbour to the point of dying in his place. Carry the verse as a mirror and a map both: it shows you what you are not, and it shows you exactly what he is making you.
