Verse Study · John

John 13:14

The night before He died, the Lord of glory knelt on a dirt floor with a towel. Then He told the men watching that they owed the same.

If I then, the Lord and the Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another’s feet.— World English Bible (public domain)

If I then, your Lord and Master, have washed your feet; ye also ought to wash one another's feet.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The sentence is an argument from the greater to the lesser. If the one at the top did the lowest job in the house, then nobody below Him is excused from it. Jesus does not ask the disciples to admire what He did. He tells them they owe it.

Look at the order of the titles. One verse earlier Jesus says, “Ye call me Master and Lord: and ye say well; for so I am.” Here He turns them around: “your Lord and Master.” He puts Lord first. The point is not that He set His authority aside to wash their feet. The point is that the one with all authority used it this way.

  • κύριος kyrios · “your Lord” · Strong’s G2962

    Appearing over 700 times, kyrios is a paramount christological title. In everyday Greek: "sir" or "master." In the Septuagint, it translates both Adonai and the divine name YHWH (H3068).

    Kyrios is the word that in the Greek Old Testament stands for the name of God. John has already told us, just before the washing begins, that Jesus knew “that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he was come from God, and went to God.” The Lord who washed their feet knew exactly who He was. Humility did not come from forgetting His rank. It came from being entirely secure in it.

  • διδάσκαλος didaskalos · “Master” · Strong’s G1320

    A teacher, instructor, master. Didaskalos is the Greek equivalent of Hebrew rabbi.

    Teacher — the Greek equivalent of rabbi. Disciples serve their teacher; that was the order of things, and Peter’s protest shows every man in the room knew it. The scene was upside down.

  • νίπτω níptō · “have washed” · Strong’s G3538

    Strong's defines G3538 as to cleanse (especially the hands or the feet or the face); ceremonially, to perform ablution. The King James translators rendered it wash.

    Strong’s gives this word for washing a part of the body — especially the hands, the feet or the face. In verse 10 Jesus sets it beside a different verb, the one for being washed all over: “He that is washed needeth not save to wash his feet, but is clean every whit.” The first washing stands for the cleansing a man receives once, when he comes to Christ. The feet still pick up the dust of the road. Both pictures are in the room.

  • πούς poús · “feet” · Strong’s G4228

    Strong's defines G4228 as a "foot" (figuratively or literally). Strong's gives the derivation as a primary word.

    Feet were the dirtiest part of a man in a world of dirt roads and sandals, and washing them was a servant’s job. That is why Peter protests, “Lord, dost thou wash my feet?” He is not being polite. He is scandalized.

  • ὀφείλω Opheilō · “ought” · Strong’s G3784

    To be morally indebted or obligated Theological Significance Romans 13:8: owe no one anything except love USMC Ministries Greek & Hebrew Lexicon © 2026 U.S.M.C. Ministries · Bible Translation Engine

    Opheilo is the language of debt — to owe, to be morally bound. Jesus does not say it would be nice if they washed each other’s feet, or that the gifted ones might consider it. He says they are obligated. Paul pairs this same word with the next one in Romans 13:8, for the only debt a Christian is never finished paying: “Owe no man any thing, but to love one another.”

  • ἀλλήλων allēlōn · “one another's” · Strong’s G240

    The reciprocal pronoun meaning 'one another' or 'each other.' It appears in over 100 NT verses, forming the backbone of Christian community ethics — the 'one another' commands that define life together in the body of Christ. Usage & Theological Significance The allēlōn commands are the DNA of Christian community: love one another, forgive one another, bear one another's burdens, encourage one another, serve one another.

    One another. Not the crowds, not the poor in general, not the man you will never see again. The men in that room had been arguing about rank. Jesus pointed the duty straight at them — at each other.

2. Where It Sits

John 13 opens the last night. The public ministry is over. From here to the end of chapter 17, Jesus speaks only to His own. John sets the tone in one sentence: “having loved his own which were in the world, he loved them unto the end.”

Then comes a detail that changes everything about the scene. “The devil having now put into the heart of Judas Iscariot, Simon’s son, to betray him.” Jesus knows. He says so at verse 10 — “ye are clean, but not all” — and John explains that He knew who would betray Him. So the feet Jesus washed included the feet of the man who would walk out of that room and sell Him. Whatever this verse asks of us, it does not let us choose only the deserving.

Luke tells us something John leaves out. At that same supper, “there was also a strife among them, which of them should be accounted the greatest.” Jesus answered them, “I am among you as he that serveth.” Put the two accounts together and you see what the towel was answering. The disciples were jockeying for position. Jesus took the one position none of them wanted.

After the washing He sits back down and asks, “Know ye what I have done to you?” Verse 14 is the first answer. Verse 15 makes it plain: “I have given you an example, that ye should do as I have done to you.” Verse 16 seals it: “The servant is not greater than his lord.” And verse 17 adds the line most men skip: “If ye know these things, happy are ye if ye do them.”

Read this alongside Philippians 2. Paul says Christ “made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant.” John 13 is that sentence acted out with water and a towel, a few hours before it was acted out on a cross.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin’s chapter on self-denial names the exact sickness that the towel answers. Every man, he says, keeps a private throne:

The poor man yields to the rich, the plebeian to the noble, the servant to the master, the unlearned to the learned, and yet every one inwardly cherishes some idea of his own superiority. Thus each flattering himself, sets up a kind of kingdom in his breast;

Calvin, Institutes III.7

That is the room in Luke 22. Twelve men, each with a kingdom in his breast. Jesus did not argue them out of it. He knelt.

Calvin then explains what serving actually looks like once that kingdom is pulled down. It is not occasional heroics. It is stewardship:

Thus, whatever the pious man can do, he is bound to do for his brethren, not consulting his own interest in any other way than by striving earnestly for the common edification of the Church. Let this, then, be our method of showing good-will and kindness, considering that, in regard to everything which God has bestowed upon us, and by which we can aid our neighbour, we are his stewards, and are bound to give account of our stewardship; moreover, that the only right mode of administration is that which is regulated by love.

Calvin, Institutes III.7

“Bound” is Calvin’s word, and it is the right one. It is the same note as ought in the verse. Your gifts, your strength, your time and your money are not yours to spend on your own rank. They are held in trust for your brothers.

Some churches have taken the command as a rite to be repeated. Calvin notes in passing (Institutes IV.10) that the practice was disputed even in the early church: he reports Augustine’s word that certain churches of his day rejected the formal imitation of Christ in the washing of feet, so that it would not be confused with baptism. The Reformed Baptist position follows from the Confession below. What Christ commands here is a way of life, not a third sacrament.

4. Confessional Anchor

The 1689 Confession names only two ordinances that Christ appointed to be continued in His church:

Baptism and the Lord’s Supper are ordinances of positive and sovereign institution, appointed by the Lord Jesus, the only lawgiver, to be continued in his church to the end of the world.

1689 LBCF, ch. 28 — Of Baptism and the Lord's Supper

That keeps the verse from being shrunk into a ceremony. A man can wash feet once a year in a church basement and never once take the low place at home. The duty of John 13:14 is wider and harder than any rite.

Where the Confession does land this verse is in its chapter on the communion of saints:

And being united to one another in love, they have communion in each other’s gifts and graces, and are bound to the performance of such duties, public and private, as in an orderly way conduce to their mutual good, both in the inward and outward man.

1689 LBCF, ch. 27 — Of the Communion of Saints

Bound again. Public and private. Inward and outward man. The Confession says what Jesus said: you owe this, and you owe it to the people you actually sit with. It covers the soul and the body — prayer for a brother and a ride to the doctor are both on the list.

5. For the Man Reading This

Start with where Jesus started. He knew who He was, where He came from and where He was going, and then He picked up the towel. Men who are unsure of themselves guard their rank. Men who are settled in Christ can afford to kneel. If you find you cannot take the low place without resentment, the problem is usually not your schedule. It is the kingdom in your breast.

Then go home. The first feet a married man is told to wash are under his own roof. That looks like doing the job nobody thanks you for — the dishes after the long day, the night feeding, the hard conversation you would rather leave to your wife. Leadership in your house is real, and it is not cancelled by this verse. It is defined by it. Your Lord used His authority to serve; so must you.

Then look at your church. Who is the man nobody calls? Whose need is beneath your notice because it is inconvenient or dull? Calvin says your gifts are held in trust. Spend them there.

Remember Judas. Jesus washed the feet of a man He knew would betray Him. You will be asked to serve men who are ungrateful, men who have wronged you, men who will not return the favor. The verse makes no exception for them.

And remember verse 17. Most men can explain this passage. Few do it. “If ye know these things, happy are ye if ye do them.” The blessing is not attached to the knowing.
