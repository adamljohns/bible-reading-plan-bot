Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:3

The first word out of Jesus' mouth in His first sermon is a congratulation, and He aims it at the people nobody congratulates.

“Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven.— World English Bible (public domain)

Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

This is the opening line of the Sermon on the Mount, and everything after it is shaped by the shock of it. Jesus sits down to teach — the posture of a rabbi with authority — and the first thing He says is that the bankrupt are the fortunate ones.

Note the tense in the second clause. Every other beatitude promises something future: they shall be comforted, they shall inherit, they shall be filled. This one is present. Theirs is the kingdom. The poor in spirit are not queued for it.

  • μακάριος makarios · “Blessed” · Strong’s G3107

    The Greek word for blessedness — not emotional happiness dependent on circumstances, but the deep well-being of those who are right with God. The Beatitudes (Matt 5) open with makarios.

    Makarios is not “happy” in the way English uses the word. It does not describe a mood produced by circumstances; it describes the settled well-being of a man who is right with God, whatever his week looks like. Which is the only reason the sentence can work. If makarios meant cheerful, “blessed are those who mourn” two verses later would be nonsense.

  • πνεῦμα pneuma · “in spirit” · Strong’s G4151

    A word of remarkable range: wind, breath, the human spirit, an evil spirit, or — most significantly — the Holy Spirit of God. 379 times.

    Pneuma covers wind, breath, the human spirit. The qualifier matters enormously: poor in spirit, not simply poor. Luke’s version of this beatitude drops the qualifier and says blessed are you who are poor, and the two are not in conflict — but Matthew’s phrase names the inward condition that material poverty often produces and wealth usually prevents.

  • βασιλεία basileia · “the kingdom” · Strong’s G932

    The Greek word for kingdom, reign, or royal power, appearing 162 times — and the central theme of Jesus' preaching. "The kingdom of God" (hē basileia tou theou) was the heart of Jesus' message: "The kingdom of God has come near.

    Basileia is kingdom, reign, royal power — and it is the central theme of Jesus' preaching, appearing 162 times. Worth hearing as reign rather than realm. The promise is not primarily real estate. It is that these people are the ones under the government of God.

  • πτωχός ptōchos · “are the poor” · Strong’s G4434

    The Greek adjective ptōchos names the beggar-poor — the man who has to crouch for help, not the working man who still has a wage. Greek had another word for that thinner poverty, penēs (G3993).

    Everything turns on this word, and the lexicon now carries it. Greek has two words for poor. Penēs is the working poor — a man with nothing spare who still earns his bread. Ptōchos comes from a root meaning to crouch or cower: a beggar, someone with nothing and no prospect of getting it, living on what is handed to him.

    Jesus chose the second. Note how the King James itself renders the word elsewhere — beggar, beggarly — which settles the sense from the text rather than from a dictionary. Poor in spirit is not modesty about your spiritual attainments. It is an empty hand held out.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 4 ends with crowds — from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea, beyond the Jordan. Jesus has been healing everyone brought to Him, and the crowd is enormous and largely there for the healings.

Then chapter 5 opens: seeing the multitudes, He went up on a mountain, and when He was seated His disciples came to Him. The teaching is aimed at the disciples with the crowd listening in, and it is delivered from a mountain by a man who has just been compared, throughout Matthew’s Gospel, to Moses.

That is the frame. A new Moses on a new mountain, and the first word of the new law is not a command. It is a blessing, and it is pronounced over people with nothing.

The beatitudes that follow are not eight separate character traits to cultivate. They read far better as a single portrait deepening: a man who knows he is bankrupt (v. 3) grieves over it (v. 4), stops asserting himself (v. 5), starts craving righteousness he does not have (v. 6) — and out of that come mercy, purity, peacemaking, and the persecution that follows.

Which means this verse is the door. Nothing else in the list is reachable without it.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed tradition read this beatitude as the first act of grace rather than the first achievement of the believer. A man does not make himself ptochos; he discovers he already is, and the discovery is the Spirit’s work.

That reading protects the verse from the obvious corruption. Poverty of spirit can be performed — there is a whole style of self-deprecating religious speech that is really a bid for admiration. The tradition’s answer was that genuine poverty of spirit is not something you display, because a beggar holding out his hand is not making a statement about himself.

It also explains the present tense. If the kingdom belonged to those who had achieved a quality, the promise would have to be future, pending assessment. Because the qualification is emptiness, the promise can be now.

4. Confessional Anchor

This saving repentance is an evangelical grace, whereby a person being by the Holy Spirit made sensible of the manifold evils of his sin, does, by faith in Christ, humble himself for it with godly sorrow, detestation of it, and self-abhorrence, praying for pardon and strength of grace.

1689 LBCF, ch. 15 — Of Repentance Unto Life and Salvation

Being by the Holy Spirit made sensible is the confession’s answer to how a man becomes poor in spirit. Passive construction, deliberately. He is made aware; he does not achieve the awareness.

And an evangelical grace is the phrase that keeps this beatitude from becoming a work. Repentance in this account is not the price of the kingdom, it is one of the kingdom’s gifts. Which is exactly why Jesus can call the man in that condition makarios — he is not being congratulated on his effort but on what has evidently already happened to him.

Praying for pardon and strength of grace is the beggar’s hand, in the confession’s own vocabulary.

5. For the Man Reading This

The hardest part of this verse for a competent man is that it cannot be achieved by competence. Everything else you have succeeded at, you succeeded at by application. This one goes the other way, and effort applied directly to it produces a counterfeit — a self-conscious humility that is still, underneath, a performance being graded.

So the practical route in is not to try to feel poor. It is to stop maintaining the accounts. Most of us run a quiet ledger where our decent qualities offset our bad ones and the balance stays acceptable. Poverty of spirit is what happens when you stop keeping that book — not because you talked yourself into feeling worthless, but because you finally looked at what the standard actually is.

And take the present tense as the comfort it is. Every other beatitude asks you to wait. This one does not. If you are holding an empty hand this morning, you are not being promised the kingdom eventually, pending improvement. Theirs is — present, already, on the day you have nothing to bring.

That is the door Jesus put first, and He put it first because it is the only one that opens.

In the Sermon on the Mount memorize pack — carry it.
