Verse Study · Philippians

Philippians 4:6-7

Two sentences doing two different jobs. The first is an order you can obey tonight. The second is a promise you were never asked to manufacture.

In nothing be anxious, but in everything, by prayer and petition with thanksgiving, let your requests be made known to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your thoughts in Christ Jesus.— World English Bible (public domain)

Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

Read the two verses as what they are grammatically: a command, and then a consequence. Verse 6 tells a man what to do. Verse 7 tells him what God will do. Paul nowhere commands the peace. He commands the praying, and then reports what follows it.

That distinction carries the whole pastoral weight of the passage. A man ordered to stop being anxious has been handed a job he cannot do by trying, and the trying makes it worse — nobody ever calmed himself down by concentrating hard on how uncalm he was. Paul does not ask for that. He redirects the traffic. The anxiety has somewhere to go, and the going is the obedience.

Notice also what is missing. There is no promise here that you will get what you asked for. Make the request known, Paul says; he does not say the request will be granted. What is guaranteed is not the outcome. It is the garrison.

  • μεριμνάω merimnaō · “Be careful” · Strong’s G3309

    The Greek verb merimnaō means to be anxious about, to take thought in the old English sense — not mere planning, but a mind divided. It comes from the noun merimna (G3308), care that splits a man into parts.

    The King James “be careful” is four centuries old and means precisely what the Greek means: full of care, care-full, carrying it. The picture is not forethought. It is division — a mind pulled into pieces by something it cannot settle. Jesus uses this same verb through the Sermon on the Mount, always as a prohibition, and once in a kitchen, to a woman He loved: Martha, thou art careful and troubled about many things. Paul is not forbidding you to think about your problem. He is forbidding the split.

  • δέησις deesis · “supplication” · Strong’s G1162

    From deomai (“to be in want, to beg”), deesis denotes a specific, urgent request — supplication arising from felt need. It is more focused than proseuche (G4335, general prayer) and emphasizes the petitioner’s sense of need and dependence.

    Paul stacks two words where one would have done. Proseuche is prayer as such — the standing address to God, the thing a man does at the table and beside the bed. Deesis is the other kind. It comes off a verb meaning to be in want, and it is the beg. A man who only ever prays proseuche has a discipline. A man who prays deesis has admitted he is short. Paul asks for both and puts the desperate one second, which is where it usually turns up anyway.

  • εὐχαριστία eucharistia · “thanksgiving” · Strong’s G2169

    Thanksgiving, the giving of thanks, gratitude. From eu (well) + charis (grace) — literally ‘good grace’ or ‘gracious response.’ Eucharistia is both the attitude of gratitude and the act of giving thanks, particularly in worship.

    This is the word in the sentence that costs something. Anyone in trouble can ask; asking is instinct. Thanking, while the trouble is still sitting on you and unresolved, is not instinct. It is a decision about who God has already proved Himself to be. And Paul does not schedule it for after the answer comes. He puts it inside the asking, as the condition of it.

  • γνωρίζω gnōrizō · “let … be made known” · Strong’s G1107

    The Greek verb gnōrizō means to make known, reveal, or declare — to bring something from hidden to known. It is used of God making His will known, of the gospel being proclaimed, and of believers making known the mystery of Christ.

    An odd verb to aim at God, who is not short of information. It is the word used elsewhere of God disclosing a mystery — dragging what was hidden out into the open. Used this way it points not at what God learns but at what you do: the requests are made known because you say them, in words, in specifics. And what you make known has to be an aitema — a defined, articulated petition, not a mood. Prayer that never gets past a general sense of dread has not yet obeyed this verse.

  • ὑπερέχω hyperechō · “passeth” · Strong’s G5242

    The Greek verb hyperechō means to hold above, to stand over, to surpass. It is hyper (over) plus echō (to have or hold). KJV in Philippians 4:7 renders it passeth. The point is not that peace is vaguely large. The point is that this peace holds a rank above the mind that tries to measure it.

    “Passeth all understanding” gets misread as bigger than you can imagine, which quietly flatters us with the size of our imagination. The verb is positional. It holds a rank above. This peace is not going to be argued into you, and your mind is not required to sign off on it first — it arrives above your pay grade and takes up a post there. The thing it outranks is nous, the reasoning mind itself, and Paul says all of it. His included.

  • φρουρέω phroureō · “shall keep” · Strong’s G5432

    The Greek verb phroureō means to keep a military watch — to garrison a city, to post a guard so nothing gets in or out without leave. It is not a soft “keep” as in storage. It is a sentry.

    This is the word the whole passage has been walking toward, and it is a soldier’s word. Not keep as in store. Keep as in hold the line. A garrison does not remove the enemy from the map; it stands between the enemy and the town. Paul is dictating this from custody. He has already told this church that the whole palace guard knows why he is in chains (Philippians 1:13), and he signs the letter off with greetings from believers in Caesar’s household (4:22). He knows exactly what a posted watch looks like from the wrong side of it. He says God will post one on you.

  • νόημα noema · “minds” · Strong’s G3540

    Noema comes from noos (mind) and means a deliberate thought, scheme, or mental purpose. The word bridges ordinary cognition and strategic intent.

    Two things get the guard: the heart — the inner man, the seat of the will, not merely of feeling — and the noemata. The second is the interesting one, because Paul uses this same noun elsewhere for the enemy’s schemes and for thoughts taken captive. It is not the mind as an organ. It is what the mind is running: the plans, the rehearsals, the three-in-the-morning arguments you keep having with a man who is not in the room. Those are the rooms anxiety raids first. Those are the rooms the sentry is posted in.

2. Where It Sits

Philippians is a thank-you note written from custody. Paul is in chains, and the congregation he is writing to has just sent him money by the hand of a man named Epaphroditus (4:18). The letter is warm nearly to the point of sentiment, and it is also, from end to end, a letter about joy under conditions that do not warrant any.

Chapter 4 opens on the ordinary trouble of a real church. Two women, Euodia and Syntyche, are not getting along, and Paul names them in a letter he knows will be read out loud to everybody (4:2-3). He does not treat it as a small thing — he asks a third party to step in. Then, immediately: rejoice, always (4:4). Let your gentleness be known; the Lord is at hand (4:5). And then our two verses.

That is the running order. A church quarrel, a command to rejoice, a reminder that Christ is near, and then the instruction about anxiety. This is not a devotional aside dropped into a closing paragraph. It is what Paul says to a congregation with something real going wrong inside it.

Look at what brackets the promise, too. Verse 7 offers the peace of God. Verse 9, two sentences later, offers the God of peace. Paul turns the phrase around on purpose. Standing between them is verse 8, the command to think on whatever is true and honorable and just — which is the same faculty, the noemata, that verse 7 has just put a guard on. The garrison gets posted. You still decide what you walk through the gate.

And one guard against over-reading. Verse 6 is not a ban on caring. Four verses later Paul thanks the Philippians warmly because their care for him has flourished again (4:10) — and the word there is a different one, phroneō, the settled orientation of a mind. Their concern for him was not sin. It was love with a delivery problem. What verse 6 forbids is the divided mind, not the engaged one.

  • Matthew 6:25

    Therefore I tell you, don’t be anxious for your life: what you will eat, or what you will drink; nor yet for your body, what you will wear. Isn’t life more than food, and the body more than clothing?

  • Psalm 55:22

    Cast your burden on the LORD, and he will sustain you. He will never allow the righteous to be moved.

  • Psalm 62:8

    Trust in him at all times, you people. Pour out your heart before him. God is a refuge for us. Selah.

  • Isaiah 26:3

    You will keep whoever’s mind is steadfast in perfect peace, because he trusts in you.

  • John 14:27

    Peace I leave with you. My peace I give to you; not as the world gives, give I to you. Don’t let your heart be troubled, neither let it be fearful.

  • Romans 8:26

    In the same way, the Spirit also helps our weaknesses, for we don’t know how to pray as we ought. But the Spirit himself makes intercession for us with groanings which can’t be uttered.

Notice where that chain lands. Jesus in Matthew 6 forbids the same verb Paul forbids here. David in Psalm 55 gives the same instruction in Hebrew — cast it, hand it off, it is not yours to hold. Isaiah says it from the other end: kept in perfect peace, and the reason given is trust, not technique. And Romans 8 covers the case Paul does not raise in Philippians — the man so far under that he cannot get the request into words at all. He is not excluded. The Spirit does the naming for him.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

Calvin gives prayer the longest chapter in the Institutes, and Philippians 4:6 sits near the middle of it. Start with what he refuses to require. The standard way to fail this verse is to conclude that you must first be calm enough to pray properly. Calvin cuts that off:

I do not here insist on a mind so disengaged as to feel none of the gnawings of anxiety; on the contrary, it is by much anxiety that the fervor of prayer is inflamed.

Calvin, Institutes III.20

That is the reverse of what an anxious man assumes. The anxiety is not the disqualification; more often it is the engine. What Calvin actually demands is narrower — that the mind not be, in his phrase, driven to and fro in vague suspense. Which is exactly what merimnaō describes: not intensity, but division.

Then the thanksgiving, which he will not let anyone treat as garnish:

Nay, we must attend to the declaration of Paul, that all wishes are vicious and perverse which are not accompanied with thanksgiving.

Calvin, Institutes III.20

Strong language, and he says straight away who he is aiming at:

Because many, under the influence of moroseness, weariness, impatience, bitter grief and fear, use murmuring in their prayers, he enjoins us so to regulate our feelings as cheerfully to bless God even before obtaining what we ask.

Calvin, Institutes III.20

Murmuring is the counterfeit of prayer, and from the outside the two are nearly identical: a man talking to God about his trouble. The difference is the thanksgiving. One brings the trouble. The other brings the trouble and a verdict on God’s character to carry it in. Paul’s word order builds the second and forecloses the first.

And then the outcome, which Calvin describes in almost Paul’s own terms:

Hence, admirable peace and tranquillity are given to our consciences; for the straits by which we were pressed being laid before the Lord, we rest fully satisfied with the assurance that none of our evils are unknown to him, and that he is both able and willing to make the best provision for us.

Calvin, Institutes III.20

Look at what he grounds the peace on. Not on the trouble being solved. On two facts about God — that He knows, and that He is both able and willing. Neither one of those requires you to see the outcome first, which is the only reason the peace can arrive before the answer does.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession does not treat prayer as a mood or a knack a man either has or lacks. It treats it as worship: a duty owed, with a shape.

Prayer, with thanksgiving, being one part of natural worship, is by God required of all men. But that it may be accepted, it is to be made in the name of the Son, by the help of the Spirit, according to his will — with understanding, reverence, humility, fervency, faith, love, and perseverance; and when with others, in a known tongue.

1689 LBCF, ch. 22 — Of Religious Worship and the Sabbath Day

Read that against Paul’s sentence and the overlap is not accidental. “Prayer, with thanksgiving” is Philippians 4:6 in four words. “In the name of the Son, by the help of the Spirit” is why the peace of verse 7 is in Christ Jesus and not available on general terms. And “perseverance” answers the question the verse raises without addressing: what a man does when he has made the request known and nothing at all has changed. He makes it known again.

The catechism defines the thing itself, and lists this very verse among its proofs:

Prayer is an offering up of our desires to God, by the assistance of the Holy Spirit, for things agreeable to his will, in the name of Christ, believing, with confession of our sins, and thankful acknowledgment of his mercies.

The Baptist Catechism, Q. 105

An offering up of our desires. Not the suppression of them, and not the pretense that a man in trouble does not want anything. The desires go up. What the catechism adds — and what Paul assumes — is the filter: for things agreeable to his will. That is why verse 7 can promise peace without promising outcomes. You are told to ask for what you want. You are not told that what you want is what is best, and the peace does not wait on you being right.

One more, because honesty requires it. The verse promises a garrison, not a permanent felt calm, and the confession says plainly that the sense of these things can go dark on a true believer:

True believers may have the assurance of their salvation in various ways shaken, diminished, and interrupted — by negligence in preserving it; by falling into some special sin which wounds the conscience and grieves the Spirit; by some sudden or vehement temptation; or by God’s withdrawing the light of his countenance and suffering even those who fear him to walk in darkness and to have no light.

1689 LBCF, ch. 18 — Of the Assurance of Grace and Salvation

A man can obey verse 6 exactly and still have a bad night. That is not the promise failing. A garrison holds the wall whether or not the people inside can hear it working.

5. For the Man Reading This

The practical shape of this is smaller than you would like, and it has three moves.

Name the thing. Out loud, in words, to God, with the actual figures attached — the amount of the bill, the name of the man you are afraid of, the diagnosis, the date. Aitema means a defined petition. Vague dread prayed vaguely stays dread. Most men skip this step because saying the thing makes it real, and a fog you have never named still feels survivable. It is not. Name it.

Thank Him in the same breath. Not for the trouble — Paul does not say that, and neither will we. Thank Him for what was already true before the trouble started and did not move when your circumstances did. This will feel dishonest the first several times. It is not; it is the correction of an honest man’s incomplete report. You were about to tell God the truth about your problem. Tell Him the truth about Himself on the way in.

Then stop. Your part ends there. There is no fourth clause where you also generate the calm. A peace that surpasses all understanding is by definition not something your understanding produces, and if you go hunting for the feeling as proof the transaction cleared, you have made your own emotional weather the receipt. It is not the receipt. The obedience is.

This verse is the prayer spoke of The Wheel — Christ at the center, obedience as the rim, and four spokes running out to the Word, prayer, fellowship, and witness. The pack pairs it with John 15:7, and the pairing is deliberate: one verse on abiding as the condition of asking, this one on asking as the cure for fear. Carry both. Carry this one especially against the hours when you cannot look anything up — two in the morning, the parking lot before you walk in, the drive home after the call. That is what memorized Scripture is for. A verse you have to go find is not available when you actually need it. Carry it →
