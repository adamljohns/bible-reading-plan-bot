Verse Study · Matthew

Matthew 5:7

Nine words, and men have been misreading them for two thousand years as a trade — be soft with people and God will be soft with you. It is not a trade. It is a diagnosis with a promise attached.

Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy.— World English Bible (public domain)

Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy.— King James Version (public domain)

1. The Text and Its Words

The sentence is built out of one root used twice. The men are merciful; what they get is mercy. In Greek that is not a coincidence of translation but the actual shape of the line — the same stem in the description and in the promise, one at the front and one at the back, with a single small word holding them together. Whatever this verse means, it means that the thing given and the thing received are the same thing.

That is where most readings go wrong. Take the sentence as a transaction and you get a religion of leverage: put mercy in, draw mercy out, and the man with the biggest deposit gets the best terms. Jesus has spent four beatitudes dismantling exactly that man. He blessed the spiritually bankrupt, the grieving, the man who will not grab, and the man starving for a righteousness he does not own. None of those four have anything to trade. Verse 7 does not suddenly hand them a currency. It tells them what happens to a man who has been handed something he could not buy.

Notice too what the verse does not say. Not blessed are the nice. Not blessed are the tolerant. Mercy in Scripture is not the absence of a verdict; it is what happens after the verdict, when the one holding the sentence declines to execute it. You cannot be merciful to a man you never judged. That is why mercy is strong and niceness is not.

  • μακάριος makarios · “Blessed” · Strong’s G3107

    The Greek word for blessedness — not emotional happiness dependent on circumstances, but the deep well-being of those who are right with God. The Beatitudes open with makarios.

    The fifth time in five verses. It is a verdict, not a mood, and it is announced in the present tense over men whose promise is still future. The merciful man is called blessed now, while the mercy he will obtain has not yet arrived. That gap is the whole comfort of the Beatitudes: God says what a man is before He gives him what He owes.

  • ἐλεήμων eleēmōn · “are the merciful” · Strong’s G1655

    A person characterized by eleos — one who feels the suffering of another and acts to relieve it. The word carries both the emotional resonance of being moved by another’s pain and the practical action of doing something about it.

    This is an adjective describing a kind of man, not a verb describing an occasion. Jesus is not blessing men who were merciful once, under good conditions, to someone who deserved it. He is naming a settled disposition — what a man is when nobody has asked him to perform. And the word is rare: in the whole New Testament it appears twice. Here, and in Hebrews 2:17, where it is used of Christ Himself, who was made like His brothers in every way that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest. The only other man in the New Testament called eleēmōn is the Lord who is speaking. That settles the standard and the source in the same stroke.

  • ἔλεος eleos · the noun behind both halves · Strong’s G1656

    Mercy, compassion, pity for the afflicted — the disposition to spare and help the suffering. Eleos translates the Hebrew chesed (lovingkindness) in the Septuagint, linking God’s covenant faithfulness with His tender mercy toward the broken.

    That Septuagint link is the key to the whole verse, because it means Jesus is not introducing a new virtue. He is naming the family resemblance of the covenant God. Chesed is the word for the loyal, stubborn, undeserved kindness God keeps showing a people who keep breaking faith with Him. When Matthew’s Jesus twice throws Hosea at the professionals — I desire mercy, and not sacrifice — this is the noun He uses, and it is the same noun in the list of things they had left undone: justice, mercy, and faith, the weightier matters of the law. Mercy is not the soft edge of religion. In Matthew it is one of the heavy things, and the men who tithed their herb garden are condemned for skipping it.

  • ὅτι hoti · “for” · Strong’s G3754

    One of the most common Greek conjunctions. It either introduces the content of a statement (“that”) or gives the reason for it (“because,” “for,” “since”). Context decides which.

    The whole argument of this study hangs on one unglamorous word. Hoti can carry a reason, and it can carry a content — but it does not carry a condition. It is not the Greek for if. Jesus does not say if you are merciful, then mercy will be extended. He states a fact about the merciful and then states what is true of them. The same construction runs through all eight beatitudes: theirs is the kingdom, they shall be comforted, they shall inherit. Nobody reads verse 4 as a bargain in which grief purchases comfort. Read verse 7 the same way you read verse 4.

  • ἐλεέω eleeō · “shall obtain mercy” · Strong’s G1653

    To have mercy, to show compassion, to pity. The verbal form of eleos, and in the Gospels it is the word in the mouths of beggars: “Lord, have mercy on us.” It combines compassionate feeling with concrete action.

    The promise is passive, like every other promise in the Beatitudes. Shall obtain mercy — shall be shown mercy, by Someone. The merciful man is not the agent of his own outcome. He is the recipient again, at the end, exactly as he was at the beginning. And this is the same verb the master uses in Matthew’s hardest parable, when the forgiven servant throttles a man over pocket change: Shouldn’t you also have had mercy on your fellow servant, even as I had mercy on you? Twice in one sentence, once for the servant and once for the master. Same verb, same mercy, and the servant lost everything not because he failed to earn his pardon but because he proved he never understood he had one.

2. Where It Sits

Matthew 5:6 — Blessed are those who hunger and thirst after righteousness, for they shall be filled.

Matthew 5:8 — Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.

The eight beatitudes fall into two halves, and verse 7 opens the second. The first four describe a man being emptied: bankrupt in spirit, mourning, meek, starving. Nothing in that list is an action. It is a man discovering he has nothing. Then verse 6 promises he will be filled — and the moment the promise lands, the sentences turn outward. Merciful. Pure in heart. Peacemaking. Persecuted for righteousness. The second four are what comes out of a man who has been fed.

Sequence is the argument here. Mercy is the fifth beatitude, not the first, and Jesus did not shuffle them. A man who has not been emptied has no mercy to give, because he still believes his own standing was earned and will judge everyone else on the same terms he imagines he passed. Put verse 7 first and you get moralism. Put it fifth and you get the gospel, because by the time the man gets here he has already been told that the righteousness he is starving for will be handed to him.

Widen out and the whole Sermon leans on this verse. Nine verses on, Jesus teaches the men to pray forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors — and then, out of seven petitions, doubles back to comment on that one alone. In the same sermon He warns that the measure you use will be measured back to you. Later in the Gospel He tells the parable of a servant forgiven ten thousand talents who would not forgive a hundred denarii, and the parable ends with the heavenly Father doing to him what he did to his brother. Matthew keeps returning to this and never softens it.

And Jesus twice quotes Hosea at the men who thought they had religion handled — I desire mercy, and not sacrifice — once over dinner with tax collectors and once over grain picked on a Sabbath. Both times the professionals were technically correct and merciless, and both times He told them their correctness had failed. That is the target of this beatitude. Not the openly wicked. The scrupulous.

  • James 2:13

    For judgment is without mercy to him who has shown no mercy. Mercy triumphs over judgment.

  • Luke 6:36

    “Therefore be merciful, even as your Father is also merciful.

  • Micah 6:8

    He has shown you, O man, what is good. What does the LORD require of you, but to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?

  • Psalm 18:25

    With the merciful you will show yourself merciful. With the perfect man, you will show yourself perfect.

  • Proverbs 11:17

    The merciful man does good to his own soul, but he who is cruel troubles his own flesh.

James is the sharpest of these and the most often misread. He does not say mercy earns acquittal. He says a merciless man will be judged without mercy — that the man who wants law for his neighbour will get law for himself, and that is a warning, not a price list.

3. What the Witnesses Saw

The Reformed reading of this verse has to answer two questions at once. First: what actually makes a man merciful, given that mercy toward the undeserving is not native to anybody. Second: how the promise can be real without turning grace into wages. Calvin takes both, and he takes the first one by moving the question off the man in front of you.

The Lord enjoins us to do good to all without exception, though the greater part, if estimated by their own merit, are most unworthy of it. But Scripture subjoins a most excellent reason, when it tells us that we are not to look to what men in themselves deserve, but to attend to the image of God, which exists in all, and to which we owe all honour and love.

Calvin, Institutes III.7

That is the mechanism. Mercy is impossible as long as you are pricing the recipient, because the recipient will not be worth it — that is what the word mercy already told you. So Calvin refuses the appraisal entirely and puts the image of God between you and the man’s record. He then works down the list of excuses one at a time, and he knows every one of them:

Therefore, whoever be the man that is presented to you as needing your assistance, you have no ground for declining to give it to him. … Say that he is unworthy of your least exertion on his account; but the image of God, by which he is recommended to you, is worthy of yourself and all your exertions.

Calvin, Institutes III.7

He is a stranger. He is nobody. You owe him nothing. He hurt you. Calvin answers the last one — the only one that ever really stops a man — with a question instead of an argument: He has deserved very differently from me, you will say. But what has the Lord deserved? There is no reply to that. It is the parable of the unforgiving servant in nine words.

Then Calvin does something most treatments of mercy never get to. He turns on the merciful man’s manner. It is not enough, he says, to do the thing; a man can discharge every duty on the list and still do it wrong, and he had seen plenty of it — men who would be thought very liberal, and who never gave alms without contempt in the look on their face.

First, they should put themselves in the place of him whom they see in need of their assistance, and pity his misfortune as if they felt and bore it, so that a feeling of pity and humanity should incline them to assist him just as they would themselves.

Calvin, Institutes III.7

That is eleēmōn in a sentence: feel it as if you bore it, then act. Not a cheque written from a height. And Calvin closes the loophole a generous man reaches for last — the idea that a donation buys him out of the rest of the obligation. Every one should rather consider, that however great he is, he owes himself to his neighbours, and that the only limit to his beneficence is the failure of his means.

On the second question — how the promise stays grace — Calvin is just as blunt. Writing on the petition this beatitude stands behind, he explains why our sins are called debts at all:

To sins he gives the name of debts, because we owe the punishment due to them, a debt which we could not possibly pay were we not discharged by this remission, the result of his free mercy, when he freely expunges the debt, accepting nothing in return; but of his own mercy receiving satisfaction in Christ, who gave himself a ransom for us.

Calvin, Institutes III.20

Accepting nothing in return. Which means the man who prays that petition while holding up his own record of mercy has misunderstood what he is asking for. Calvin says it exactly:

while they address God in this petition, do nothing more than subscribe their own accusation, and seal their condemnation by their own testimony … they do not implore his mercy, but appeal to his justice.

Calvin, Institutes III.20

Anyone who reads Matthew 5:7 as payment has just done that. He has walked into the courtroom with an invoice, and an invoice is an appeal to justice. Justice is the last thing he wants.

4. Confessional Anchor

The confession does not comment on this verse directly, but it settles the exact question the verse raises, and it settles it in two paragraphs that have to be held together. First, what a work of mercy is:

These good works, done in obedience to God's commandments, are the fruits and evidences of a true and lively faith; and by them believers manifest their thankfulness, strengthen their assurance, edify their brethren, adorn the profession of the gospel, stop the mouths of the adversaries, and glorify God.

1689 LBCF, ch. 16 — Of Good Works

Fruits and evidences. Not causes. A merciful man is not manufacturing his standing; he is displaying it. And note the second item on that list — strengthen their assurance. The confession grants that works do something for a man’s certainty, which is precisely what Matthew 5:7 is doing. When a man finds himself extending mercy at real cost to someone who has not earned it, he is looking at evidence that something happened to him he did not do himself.

Then, immediately, the fence:

We cannot by our best works merit pardon of sin or eternal life at the hand of God, by reason of the great disproportion that is between them and the glory to come, and the infinite distance that is between us and God, whom by them we can neither profit nor satisfy for the debt of our former sins.

1689 LBCF, ch. 16 — Of Good Works

Our best works. The confession is not talking about your worst day. It is saying that the most costly mercy you ever showed — the forgiveness that took you eleven years — cannot buy a pardon, because the disproportion is infinite and the arithmetic never closes. So mercy is evidence and it is not payment, and both of those are true of the same act at the same time.

The Baptist Catechism puts the two together in one line, on the petition Jesus attaches to this beatitude:

In the fifth petition, which is, And forgive us our debts as we forgive our debtors, we pray that God, for Christ's sake, would freely pardon all our sins, which we are the rather encouraged to ask because by his grace we are enabled from the heart to forgive others.

The Baptist Catechism, Question 112
Read the clause slowly

Freely pardon — the pardon is free, so nothing you did bought it. For Christ's sake — the ground is outside you entirely. Encouraged to ask — your forgiving is not the price, it is the nerve; it is why you dare open your mouth. By his grace we are enabled — and even that nerve was issued to you. Every door out of grace is nailed shut, and the verse still lands with full weight: a man who will not forgive has no business praying that prayer, because he is asking for something he has already announced he does not believe in.

5. For the Man Reading This

Start where it actually costs something. Mercy is cheap toward strangers and expensive toward the man who wronged you, and that second one is the only real test in the verse. You already know his name. You have thought about him this week. Everything below is about him.

Mercy is not saying it did not happen. This is where men get stuck, and it is a bad reason to stay stuck. Mercy requires the verdict. You looked at what he did, you called it what it is, and then you declined to make him pay. If you skip the verdict you have not shown mercy, you have shown avoidance, and avoidance rots. Name the debt honestly. Then release it, knowing exactly what you are releasing.

Mercy is not the same as trust. Forgiving a man does not mean handing him the keys again. You can cancel a debt and still not lend to that man a second time; Scripture is full of both wisdom and consequence, and mercy never asks you to be a fool with what you are responsible for. A father can forgive and still change the locks. Do not let a false definition of mercy make you a bad steward, and do not use stewardship as a costume for a grudge you are keeping.

Run the audit downward. Most men are merciful upward — to the boss, to the man who could hurt them, to the people whose opinion they need. Watch how you speak to the man behind the counter, to the subordinate who blew a deadline, to the wife who is wrong about something small, to the kid who broke it again. Mercy shown to someone who can do nothing for you is the only sample that is not contaminated. If you are gracious in the meeting and merciless in the truck on the way home, you already have your reading.

Watch the arithmetic in your own head. The unforgiving servant knew the exact figure his fellow servant owed him. He had it memorized. He had apparently stopped counting the ten thousand talents. That is what an unmerciful man looks like from the inside — precise about what is owed him, vague about what he was forgiven. If you can recite his offense in detail and could not tell me what your own pardon cost, you are the servant in the parable and you did not notice.

Then do one thing this week. Not a feeling. Cancel something. A debt, an owed apology you have been waiting on, a story you have been telling about someone. Pay for something quietly for a man who cannot repay you and will never know it was you — Calvin’s point about the manner is that mercy delivered with contempt is not mercy, and the surest way to strip the contempt out is to remove the audience. Mercy that costs nothing and is seen by everyone is a purchase, not a gift.

And carry the promise the way it is written. They shall obtain mercy. Future, passive, no invoice attached. You are not building a case. You are showing, in the only currency that proves it, that you know what was done for you — and being told that the God who started it will finish it, on the day when the only thing any of us will want is exactly this.

In the Sermon on the Mount memorize pack — carry it. Nine words. Say them the next time you catch yourself rehearsing a case against somebody.
