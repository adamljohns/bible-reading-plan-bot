Book 1 · Chapter 11 · USMC modern English

Impiety of Attributing a Visible Form to God — The Setting Up of Idols a Defection From the True God

There are three leading divisions in this chapter. The first contains a refutation of those who ascribe a visible form to God (s. 1 and 2), with an answer to the objection of those who, because it is said that God manifested his presence by certain symbols, use it as a defence of their error (s. 3 and 4). Various arguments are afterwards adduced, disposing of the trite objection from Gregory’s expression, that images are the books of the unlearned (s. 5-7). The second division of the chapter relates to the origin of idols or images, and the adoration of them, as approved by the Papists (s. 8-10). Their evasion refuted (s. 11). The third division treats of the use and abuse of images (s. 12). Whether it is expedient to have them in Christian Churches (s. 13). The concluding part contains a refutation of the second Council of Nice, which very absurdly contends for images in opposition to divine truth, and even to the disparagement of the Christian name.

1¶

Because Scripture, making allowance for the dull and clumsy understanding of mankind, usually speaks in ordinary terms, whenever its aim is to distinguish the true God from false gods it sets him over against idols in particular. It does this not because it approves of what the philosophers teach in a more elegant and subtle fashion, but so that it may the better expose the folly — indeed the madness — of the world in its search after God, so long as every person clings to his own speculations. This exclusive definition, which we meet with everywhere in Scripture, wipes out every deity that men frame for themselves of their own accord, since God himself is the only fit witness to himself. Meanwhile, since this brutish stupidity has spread over the whole globe, with people longing after visible forms of God and so shaping gods out of wood and stone, silver and gold, or any other dead and perishable material, we must hold it as a first principle that whenever any form is assigned to God, his glory is corrupted by an ungodly lie. In the Law, accordingly, after God had claimed the glory of deity for himself alone, when he comes to show what kind of worship he approves and what he rejects, he immediately adds, "Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image, or any likeness of any thing that is in heaven above, or in the earth beneath, or in the water under the earth" (Exod. 20:4). With these words he reins in any unrestrained attempt we might make to represent him by a visible shape, and he briefly lists all the forms by which superstition had already begun, long before, to turn his truth into a lie. For we know that the Sun was worshipped by the Persian. However many stars the foolish nations saw in the sky, that many gods they imagined them to be. And to the Egyptians, every animal was a figure of God — they went so far as onions and leeks.

2¶

This may easily be gathered from the reasons he attaches to his prohibition. First, it is said in the books of Moses (Deut. 4:15), "Take ye therefore good heed unto yourselves; for ye saw no manner of similitude in the day that the Lord spake unto you in Horeb, out of the midst of the fire, lest ye corrupt yourselves, and make you a graven image, the similitude of any figure," and so on. We see how plainly God declares himself against all figures, in order to make us aware that every longing after such visible shapes is rebellion against him. Among the prophets it will be enough to mention Isaiah, who is the fullest on this subject (Isaiah 40:18; 41:7, 29; 45:9; 46:5), so as to show how the majesty of God is defiled by an absurd and unseemly invention, when he who has no body is likened to bodily matter, he who is invisible to a visible image, he who is a spirit to a lifeless object, and he who fills all space to a scrap of cheap wood, or stone, or gold. Paul, too, reasons in the same way: "Forasmuch, then, as we are the offspring of God, we ought not to think that the Godhead is like unto gold, or silver, or stone, graven by art and man's device" (Acts 17:29). From this it is plain that whatever statues are set up or pictures painted to represent God are utterly displeasing to him, as a kind of insult to his majesty. And is it any wonder that the Holy Spirit thunders such answers from heaven, when he forces even blind and wretched idolaters to make a similar confession here on earth? Seneca's complaint, as Augustine gives it in De Civit. Dei, c. 10, is well known. He says that the sacred, immortal, and invisible gods they display in the cheapest and most ignoble materials, and dress them in the clothing of men and beasts; some confuse the sexes, and make a composite out of different bodies, giving the name of deities to objects which, if they were met alive, would be reckoned monsters. From this, again, it is obvious that the defenders of images take refuge in a shabby little quibble when they pretend that the Jews were forbidden to use images because they were so inclined to superstition — as if a prohibition which the Lord grounds upon his own eternal being and upon the unchanging course of nature could be confined to a single nation. Besides, when Paul refuted the error of giving a bodily shape to God, he was addressing not Jews, but Athenians.

3¶

It is true that the Lord did at times make his presence known by certain signs, so that he was said to be seen face to face; but every sign he ever used was fitly suited to the body of teaching he was giving, and at the same time gave plain notice of his incomprehensible essence. For the cloud, the smoke, and the flame, though they were symbols of heavenly glory (Deut. 4:11), held men's minds in check as with a bridle, so that they might not try to press any further. Therefore even Moses — the man to whom, of all men, God revealed himself most intimately — was not permitted, though he prayed for it, to behold that face, but received for answer that the brightness was too great for man (Exod. 33:20). The Holy Spirit appeared under the form of a dove, but since it vanished at once, who can fail to see that in this symbol of a moment the faithful were warned to regard the Spirit as invisible, to be content with his power and grace, and not to call for any outward figure? God sometimes appeared in the form of a man, but this was in anticipation of the revelation yet to come in Christ, and so it gave the Jews not the slightest pretext for setting up a symbol of deity in human form. The mercy-seat as well (Exod. 25:17, 18, 21), where under the Law God displayed the presence of his power, was so made as to signify that God is best seen when the mind rises in wonder above itself: the Cherubim overshadowed it with outstretched wings, the veil covered it, and the remoteness of the place was by itself concealment enough. It is therefore sheer folly to try to defend images of God and of the saints by the example of the Cherubim. For what, I ask, did those figures mean, if not that images are unfit to represent the mysteries of God, since they were shaped so as to cover the mercy-seat with their wings, hiding the sight of God not only from the eye but from every human sense, and holding presumption in check? We may add to this that the prophets portray the Seraphim, who are set before us in vision, as having their faces veiled; and so they signify that the brightness of the divine glory is so great that even the angels cannot gaze upon it directly, while the faint beams that sparkle in the faces of angels are shrouded from our view. Moreover, all men of sound judgment acknowledge that the Cherubim in question belonged to the old tutelage of the law. It is absurd, then, to bring them forward as an example for our own age. For that period of childhood, if I may put it that way, to which such first lessons were suited, has passed away. And surely it is disgraceful that heathen writers should be more skilful interpreters of Scripture than the Papists. Juvenal (Sat. 14) holds the Jews up to derision for worshipping the thin clouds and the firmament. In this he is perverse and impious; yet in denying that any visible shape of Deity existed among them, he speaks more accurately than the Papists, who prate about there having been some visible image. In the fact that the people again and again rushed forth in boiling haste after idols, like water bursting out with violence from an abundant spring, let us learn how prone our nature is to idolatry — so that we may not, by throwing the whole blame for a common vice upon the Jews, be lured away by vain and sinful enticements to sleep the sleep of death.

4¶

The words of the Psalmist point the same way (Psalms 115:4, 135:15): "Their idols are silver and gold, the works of men's hands." From the materials of which they are made he concludes that they are not gods, taking it for granted that every human invention concerning God is a lifeless fiction. He mentions silver and gold rather than clay or stone, so that neither splendour nor cost may win reverence for idols. He then draws a general conclusion, that nothing is more unlikely than that gods should be formed out of any kind of lifeless matter. Man is forced to confess that he is but the creature of a day (see Book 3 c. 9 s. 2), and yet he would have the metal which he has deified regarded as God. Where did idols get their origin, if not from the will of man? There was ground, then, for the sarcasm of the heathen poet (Hor. Sat. I. 8): "I was once the trunk of a fig-tree, a useless log, when the tradesman, uncertain whether he should make me a stool, and so on, chose rather that I should be a god." In other words, an earth-born creature, who breathes out his life almost every moment, is able by his own contrivance to confer the name and honour of deity on a lifeless trunk. But since that Epicurean poet, in indulging his wit, had no regard for religion, let us pay no attention to his jeers or those of his fellows, and let the prophet's rebuke sting us — indeed, cut us to the heart — when he speaks of the utter folly of those who take a piece of wood to kindle a fire to warm themselves, to bake bread, to roast or boil flesh, and out of what is left over make a god, before which they prostrate themselves as suppliants (Isaiah 44:16). And so the same prophet, in another place, not only charges idolaters as guilty in the eye of the law, but reproaches them for not learning from the foundations of the earth, since nothing is more incongruous than to shrink the immense and incomprehensible Deity down to the stature of a few feet. And yet experience shows that this monstrous practice, plainly as it contradicts the order of nature, comes naturally to man. It should further be noted that by the manner of expression employed here, every form of superstition is denounced. Being the works of men, idols have no authority from God (Isa. 2:8, 31:7; Hos. 14:3; Mic. 5:13); and therefore it must be held as a fixed principle that all modes of worship devised by man are detestable. The Psalmist sets this folly in a still stronger light (Psalm 115:8) when he shows how help is begged from dead and senseless objects by beings who were given intelligence for the very purpose of knowing that the whole universe is governed by divine energy alone. But since the corruption of our nature sweeps all mankind, together and one by one, into this madness, the Spirit at length thunders forth a dreadful imprecation: "They that make them are like unto them, so is every one that trusteth in them." Calvin renders the words of the Psalmist as an imprecation: let those who make them be like unto them.

7¶

Let the Papists, then, if they have any sense of shame, give up from now on the futile plea that images are the books of the unlearned — a plea so plainly refuted by countless passages of Scripture. And even were I to grant the plea, it would not be a valid defence of their own particular idols. It is well known what sort of monstrosities they press upon us as divine. For what are the pictures or statues to which they attach the names of saints, but displays of the most shameless luxury or obscenity? Were anyone to dress himself after their model, he would deserve the pillory. Indeed, brothels display their inmates more chastely and modestly dressed than churches do the images intended to represent virgins. The dress of the martyrs is in no respect more becoming. Let the Papists, then, show at least a little regard for decency in decking out their idols, if they would give the least plausibility to the false allegation that these are books of some kind of sanctity. But even then we shall answer that this is not the method by which Christian people should be taught in sacred places. Very different from these follies is the doctrine in which God would have them instructed there. His injunction is that the doctrine common to all should be set forth there by the preaching of the Word and the administration of the sacraments — a doctrine to which little heed can be paid by those whose eyes are carried to and fro gazing at idols. And who are these unlearned, whose rawness can be taught by images alone? Precisely those whom the Lord acknowledges for his disciples, those whom he honours with a revelation of his celestial philosophy and desires to have trained in the saving mysteries of his kingdom. I confess, indeed, that as matters now stand there are not a few in the present day who cannot do without such books. But I ask, where does this stupidity come from, if not from the fact that they have been defrauded of the only doctrine fit to instruct them? The simple reason why those who had the charge of churches resigned the office of teaching to idols was that they themselves were dumb. Paul declares that by the true preaching of the gospel Christ is portrayed and in a manner crucified before our eyes (Gal. 3:1). Of what use, then, was the erection in churches of so many crosses of wood and stone, silver and gold, if this doctrine were faithfully and honestly preached — namely, that Christ died that he might bear our curse upon the tree, that he might expiate our sins by the sacrifice of his body, wash them in his blood, and, in short, reconcile us to God the Father? From this one doctrine the people would learn more than from a thousand crosses of wood and stone. As for crosses of gold and silver, it may well be true that the avaricious give their eyes and minds to them more eagerly than to any heavenly instructor.

8¶

As to the origin of idols, the statement contained in the Book of Wisdom has been received with almost universal consent — namely, that they began with those who bestowed this honour on the dead out of a superstitious regard for their memory. I admit that this perverse practice is of very great antiquity, and I do not deny that it was a kind of torch by which mankind's infatuated proneness to idolatry was kindled into a greater blaze. I do not, however, admit that it was the first origin of the practice. That idols were in use before that ambitious consecration of the images of the dead became widespread — a practice frequently mentioned by profane writers — is evident from the words of Moses (Gen. 31:19). When he relates that Rachel stole her father's images, he speaks of the use of idols as a common vice. From this we may infer that the human mind is, so to speak, a perpetual forge of idols. There was a kind of renewal of the world at the deluge, yet before many years had passed men were forging gods at will. There is reason to believe that in the holy Patriarch's own lifetime his grandchildren were given to idolatry, so that he must have seen with his own eyes, and not without the deepest grief, the earth polluted with idols — that same earth whose iniquities God had so lately purged with so fearful a judgment. For Joshua testifies (Josh. 24:2) that Torah and Nachor, even before the birth of Abraham, were worshipers of false gods. If the progeny of Shem revolted so speedily, what are we to think of the posterity of Ham, who had been cursed long before in their father? So indeed it is. The human mind, stuffed as it is with presumptuous rashness, dares to imagine a god suited to its own capacity; and since it labours under dullness — indeed, is sunk in the grossest ignorance — it substitutes vanity and an empty phantom in the place of God. To these evils another is added. The god whom man has thus conceived inwardly he then attempts to embody outwardly. In this way the mind conceives the idol, and the hand gives it birth. That idolatry has its origin in the idea men have that God is not present with them unless his presence is carnally exhibited is apparent from the example of the Israelites: "Up," said they, "make us gods, which shall go before us; for as for this Moses, the man that brought us up out of the land of Egypt, we wot not what is become of him" (Exod. 22:1). They knew well enough that there was a God whose mighty power they had experienced in so many miracles, but they had no confidence that he was near to them unless they beheld with their eyes a corporeal symbol of his presence, as an attestation of his actual government. They desired, therefore, to be assured by the image which went before them that they were journeying under divine guidance. And daily experience shows that the flesh is always restless until it has obtained some figment like itself, with which it may vainly solace itself as a representation of God. Driven by this blind passion, men have in almost every age since the world began set up signs in which they imagined that God was visibly depicted to their eyes.

9¶

Once such a figment has been formed, adoration follows at once: for as soon as men imagined that they beheld God in images, they also worshipped him as being there. In the end, their eyes and minds wholly engrossed by these objects, they grew more and more brutish, gazing and wondering as though some divinity were actually before them. From this it appears that men do not fall away into the worship of images until they have taken in some notion of a grosser description — not that they actually believe the images to be gods, but that the power of divinity somehow or other resides in them. Therefore, whether it is God or a creature that is imaged, the moment you fall prostrate before it in veneration you are to that extent fascinated by superstition. For this reason the Lord forbade not only the erection of statues to himself, but also the consecration of titles and stones which might be set up for adoration. For the same reason, too, the second commandment has an additional part concerning adoration. For as soon as a visible form is given to God, his power also is supposed to be annexed to it. So stupid are men that wherever they figure God, there they fix him, and by necessary consequence proceed to adore him. It makes no difference whether they worship the idol simply or God in the idol; it is always idolatry when divine honours are paid to an idol, be the colour what it may. And because God wills not to be worshipped superstitiously, whatever is bestowed upon idols is so much robbed from him.

Let those attend to this who set about hunting for miserable pretexts in defence of the execrable idolatry under which, for many ages past, true religion has been buried and sunk. It is said that the images are not accounted gods. Neither were the Jews so utterly thoughtless as to forget that there was a God whose hand had led them out of Egypt before they made the calf. Indeed, when Aaron said that these were the gods which had brought them out of Egypt, they intimated, in no ambiguous terms, that they wished to retain God their deliverer, provided they saw him going before them in the calf. Nor are the heathen to be deemed so stupid as not to understand that God was something other than wood and stone. For they changed the images at pleasure, but always retained the same gods in their minds; nevertheless, they did not therefore say that the unity of God was divided.

Would only show his power under the form of images.

10¶

It is an impudent falsehood to deny that what was anciently done is also done in our own day. For why do men prostrate themselves before images? Why, in the very act of praying, do they turn toward them as toward the ears of God? It is indeed true, as Augustine says (in Ps. 113), that no one prays or worships in this way, looking at an image, without being impressed with the idea that he is heard by it, or without hoping that what he wishes will be performed by it. Why are such distinctions drawn between different images of the same God, so that one is passed by, or receives only common honour, while another is worshipped with the highest solemnities? Why do they fatigue themselves with votive pilgrimages to images while they have many just like them at home? For there, passing by a crucifix, or an image of what they call "Our Lady," or making no great account of them, they pay their devotion to another. Why is it that they trot so far on a pilgrimage to see a marmoset, when they have one like it at their door?

11¶

I am not unaware, however, and I have no wish to disguise the fact, that they endeavour to evade the charge by means of a more subtle distinction, which shall afterwards be fully considered (see infra, s. 16, and chap. 12 s. 2). The worship which they pay to their images they cloak with the name of εἰδωλοδυλεία (ιδολοδυλια), and deny to be εἰδωλολατρεία (ιδολατρια). So they speak, holding that the worship which they call δυλια may, without insult to God, be paid to statues and pictures. Hence they think themselves blameless if they are only the servants, and not the worshippers, of idols — as if it were not a lighter matter to worship than to serve. And yet, while they take refuge in a Greek term, they contradict themselves in the most childish way. For the Greek word λατρεύειν has no other meaning than to worship, so that what they say amounts to a confession that they worship their images without worshipping them. They cannot object that I am quibbling upon words. The fact is that they only betray their own ignorance while they attempt to throw dust in the eyes of the simple. But however eloquent they may be, they will never prove by their eloquence that one and the same thing makes two. Let them show how the two differ, if they would be thought different from ancient idolaters. For just as a murderer or an adulterer will not escape conviction by giving his crime some adventitious name, so it is absurd for them to expect that the subtle device of a name will exculpate them, if in fact they differ in nothing from the idolaters whom they themselves are forced to condemn. But so far are they from proving that their case is different, that the source of the whole evil consists in a preposterous rivalship with those very idolaters, since they too devise symbolical shapes of God with their minds and execute them with their hands.

12¶

I am not, however, so superstitious as to think that all visible representations of every kind are unlawful. But since sculpture and painting are gifts of God, what I insist upon is that both be used purely and lawfully — that gifts which the Lord has bestowed upon us, for his glory and our good, should not be preposterously abused, and still less perverted to our own destruction. We think it unlawful to give a visible shape to God, because God himself has forbidden it, and because it cannot be done without tarnishing his glory in some degree. And lest anyone should suppose that we are alone in this opinion, those acquainted with the productions of sound divines will find that they have always disapproved of it. If it is unlawful to make any corporeal representation of God, it must be still more unlawful to worship such a representation instead of God, or to worship God in it. The only things, therefore, which ought to be painted or sculptured are things which can be presented to the eye; the majesty of God, which is far beyond the reach of any eye, must not be dishonoured by unbecoming representations. Visible representations fall into two classes — historical, which give a representation of events, and pictorial, which merely exhibit bodily shapes and figures. The former are of some use for instruction or admonition. The latter, so far as I can see, are fitted only for amusement. And yet it is certain that the latter are almost the only kind which have hitherto been exhibited in churches. From this we may infer that the exhibition was not the result of judicious selection, but of a foolish and inconsiderate longing. I say nothing as to the improper and unbecoming form in which they are presented, or the wanton license in which sculptors and painters have here indulged (a point to which I alluded a little ago, supra, s. 7). I say only that even were they otherwise faultless, they could be of no utility in teaching.

13¶

But setting that distinction aside, let us here consider whether it is expedient that churches should contain representations of any kind, whether of events or of human forms. First, then, if we attach any weight to the authority of the ancient Church, let us remember that for five hundred years, during which religion was in a more prosperous condition and a purer doctrine flourished, Christian churches were completely free of visible representations (see Preface, and Book 4, c. 9 s. 9). Hence their first admission as an ornament to churches took place only after the purity of the ministry had somewhat degenerated. I will not dispute how rational the grounds were on which they were first introduced; but if you compare the two periods, you will find that the latter had greatly declined from the purity of the times when images were unknown. What then? Are we to suppose that those holy fathers, if they had judged the thing to be useful and salutary, would have allowed the Church to go so long without it? Undoubtedly, because they saw very little or no advantage in it and the greatest danger, they rather rejected it intentionally and on rational grounds than omitted it through ignorance or carelessness. This is clearly attested by Augustine in these words (Ep. 49. See also De Civit. Dei, lib 4 c. 31): "When images are thus placed aloft in seats of honour, to be beheld by those who are praying or sacrificing, then though they have neither sense nor life, yet from appearing as if they had both, they affect weak minds just as if they lived and breathed," and so on. And again, in another passage (in Ps. 112), he says, "The effect produced, and in a manner extorted, by the bodily shape is that the mind, being itself in a body, imagines that a body which is so like its own must be similarly affected," and so on. A little farther on he says, "Images are more capable of giving a wrong bent to an unhappy soul, from having mouth, eyes, ears, and feet, than of correcting it, as they neither speak, nor see, nor hear, nor walk." This undoubtedly is the reason why John (1 John 5:21) enjoins us to beware not only of the worship of idols, but also of idols themselves. And from the fearful infatuation under which the world has hitherto laboured, almost to the entire destruction of piety, we know too well from experience that the moment images appear in churches, idolatry has as it were raised its banner; for the folly of mankind cannot moderate itself, but forthwith falls away to superstitious worship. Even were the danger less imminent, still, when I consider the proper end for which churches are erected, it appears to me more unbecoming their sacredness than I well can tell to admit any other images than those living symbols which the Lord has consecrated by his own word: I mean Baptism and the Lord's Supper, together with the other ceremonies. On these our eyes ought to be more steadily fixed, and by these our minds more vividly impressed, than to require the aid of any images which the wit of man may devise. Such, then, is the incomparable blessing of images — a blessing the want of which, if we believe the Papists, cannot possibly be compensated: no compensation can equal the value of a marmoset looking askance and twisting its face.

Enough, I believe, would have been said on this subject, were I not in a manner arrested by the Council of Nice — not the celebrated Council which Constantine the Great assembled, but one which was held eight hundred years ago by the orders and under the auspices of the Empress Irene, that wicked Proserpine.

15¶

When they treat of adoration, great stress is laid on the worship of Pharaoh, the staff of Joseph, and the inscription which Jacob set up. In this last case they not only pervert the meaning of Scripture, but quote what is nowhere to be found. Then the passages "Worship at his footstool" — "Worship in his holy mountain" — "The rulers of the people will worship before thy face" seem to them very solid and apposite proofs. Were someone, with a view to turning the defenders of images into ridicule, to put words into their mouths, could they be made to utter greater or grosser absurdities? But to put an end to all doubt on the subject of images, Theodosius Bishop of Mira confirms the propriety of worshipping them by the dreams of his archdeacon, which he adduces with as much gravity as if he held in his hand a response from heaven. Let the patrons of images now go and press us with the decree of this Synod, as if those venerable Fathers did not bring themselves into utter discredit by handling Scripture so childishly, or by wresting it so shamefully and profanely.

Calvin's burden here is plain: the moment we assign God a visible form, we exchange his glory for a lie, because the infinite, invisible, spiritual God cannot be shrunk into wood, stone, or canvas without insult to his majesty. The human heart, he warns, is a perpetual forge of idols. In 2026 our idols rarely wear gilded frames; they glow on screens, they trade in algorithms and curated images, they promise a God conformed to our preferences and our feelings. The old plea that images are the books of the unlearned has simply migrated to new media that catechize our children for hours each day. Calvin's remedy stands undiminished: God trains his Church not by spectacle but by the faithful preaching of his Word and the sacraments he himself ordained. Let the Christian household, then, guard its eyes and its appetites, set the Word above the image, and worship the God who has given the only sure portrait of himself in Scripture and in his Son.
