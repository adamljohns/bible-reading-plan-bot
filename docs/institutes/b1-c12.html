Book 1 · Chapter 12 · USMC modern English

God Distinguished From Idols, That He May Be the Exclusive Object of Worship

Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. Scripture, in teaching that there is but one God, does not make a dispute about words, but attributes all honour and religious worship to him alone. This proved, 1st, By the etymology of the term. 2d, By the testimony of God himself, when he declares that he is a jealous God, and will not allow himself to be confounded with any fictitious Deity.
  2. 2. The Papists in opposing this pure doctrine, gain nothing by their distinction of δυλια and λατρια.
  3. 3. Passages of Scripture subversive of the Papistical distinction, and proving that religious worship is due to God alone. Perversions of Divine worship.
1¶

We said at the beginning of this work (chapter 2) that the knowledge of God does not consist in cold speculation, but carries worship along with it; and we touched in passing (chapter 5, sections 6, 9, 10) on what will be treated more fully in other places (Book 2, chapter 8) — namely, how God is rightly worshiped. For now I will only repeat briefly that whenever Scripture asserts the oneness of God, it is not contending for a mere name, but also commands that nothing belonging to deity be assigned to any other; and in this way it makes plain in what respect pure religion differs from superstition. The Greek word εὐσέβεια means "right worship," for the Greeks, though they were groping in darkness, were always aware that a certain rule had to be kept, so that God might not be worshiped absurdly. Cicero truly and shrewdly traces the word religion back to relego, and yet the reason he gives for it is strained and far-fetched — namely, that honest worshipers read and read again, and ponder what is true. Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods, book 2, chapter 28. See also Lactantius, Divine Institutes, book 4, chapter 28.

But God, in asserting his own right, first proclaims that he is a jealous God, and that he will be a stern avenger if he is confused with any false god; and then he defines what true worship is, so that the human race may be kept in obedience. He gathers up both of these in his Law, where he first binds the faithful in allegiance to himself as their only Lawgiver, and then lays down a rule for worshiping him according to his will. The Law, with its many uses and purposes, I will consider in its own place; for the present I point only to this one, that it is meant as a bridle to curb us and keep us from turning aside to counterfeit worship. But it is necessary to hold on to the observation I set out with — that unless everything peculiar to deity is confined to God alone, he is robbed of his honor, and his worship is violated.

It may be well here to look more closely at the subtleties that superstition employs. When it revolts to strange gods, it avoids the appearance of abandoning the Supreme God, or of reducing him to the same rank as the others. It gives him the highest place, yet at the same time it surrounds him with a tribe of lesser deities, among whom it parcels out the offices that belong to him alone. In this way, though by dissembling and craft, the glory of the Godhead is cut into pieces and not allowed to remain whole. In the same way the people of old, both Jews and Gentiles, set an immense crowd of beings in subordination to the father and ruler of the gods, and gave them, each according to his rank, a share with the supreme God in the government of heaven and earth. In the same way, too, for some ages past, departed saints have been raised to a partnership with God, to be worshiped, invoked, and praised in his place. And yet we do not even imagine that the majesty of God is dimmed by this abomination, when in fact it is largely suppressed and put out — all that we keep of it being a cold opinion of his supreme power. And all the while, deceived by these entanglements, we go astray after a variety of gods.

2¶

The distinction between what is called δυλια and λατρια was invented for the very purpose of allowing divine honors to be paid to angels and to dead men with apparent impunity. For it is plain that the worship which Papists pay to saints differs in no respect from the worship of God: this worship is paid without any distinction at all, and only when they are pressed do they fall back on the evasion that what belongs to God is kept untouched, because they leave him λατρια. But since the question concerns not the word but the thing itself, how can they be allowed to toy at will with a matter of the highest importance? Yet even setting that aside, the most they will gain by their distinction is that they give worship to God and service to the others. For λατρεὶα in Greek has the same meaning as worship in Latin, whereas δουλεὶα properly means service, though in Scripture the words are sometimes used interchangeably. But suppose the distinction were always kept: the thing to be examined is what each word means. Δουλεὶα unquestionably means service, and λατρεὶα worship. But no one doubts that to serve is something higher than to worship. For it would often be a hard thing to serve the man whom you would not refuse to honor. It is therefore an unjust division to assign the greater thing to the saints and leave the lesser to God. But, it is said, several of the ancient fathers observed this distinction. What of it, when everyone can see that it is not only improper but utterly frivolous?

3¶

Laying the subtleties aside, let us examine the thing itself. When Paul reminds the Galatians of what they were before they came to the knowledge of God, he says that they "did service unto them which by nature are no gods," (Galatians 4:8). Was their superstition excusable because he does not say λατρια? This superstition, which he calls δυλια, he condemns just as fully as if he had called it λατρια. When Christ turns back Satan's insulting proposal with the words, "It is written, Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve," (Matthew 4:10), there was no question of λατρια. For all that Satan asked was προσκὺνεσις (obeisance). In the same way, when John is rebuked by the angel for falling on his knees before him (Revelation 19:10; 22:8, 9), we ought not to suppose that John had so far forgotten himself as to intend to transfer to an angel the honor due to God alone. But because it was impossible that a worship bound up with religion should not carry some flavor of divine worship, he could not προσκὺνει̑ν (do obeisance to) the angel without taking something away from the glory of God. It is true that we often read of men being given worship; but that was, if I may put it so, a civil honor. Religious honor is another matter altogether, and the moment it is joined to worship it carries with it the profaning of the honor due to God. The same thing may be seen in the case of Cornelius (Acts 10:25). He had not made so little progress in godliness as to fail to reserve supreme worship for God alone. Therefore, when he throws himself down before Peter, he certainly does not do it with the intention of adoring him in God's place. Yet Peter sternly forbids him. And why, except that we never draw the line so precisely between the worship of God and the worship of creatures as to keep from handing over to the creature, without discrimination, what belongs to God alone. Therefore, if we would have one God, let us remember that we can never claim the smallest portion of his glory without holding back what is rightly his. Accordingly, when Zechariah speaks of the restoring of the Church, he says plainly not only that there would be one God, but that he would have only one name — the reason being that he might have nothing in common with idols. The nature of the worship God requires will be seen in its own place (Book 2, chapters 7 and 8). He has been pleased to prescribe in his Law what is lawful and right, and so to hold us to a fixed rule, in case anyone should take it upon himself to invent a worship of his own. But since it is unwise to burden the reader by mixing up a variety of topics, I will not dwell on that one now. Let it be enough to remember that whatever offices of piety are given anywhere else than to God alone are of the nature of sacrilege. First, superstition attached divine honors to the sun and the stars, or to idols; afterward ambition followed — ambition which, dressing man in the spoils of God, dared to profane everything sacred. And though the principle of worshiping a supreme Deity continued to be held, still the practice was to sacrifice indiscriminately to spirits and lesser gods, or to departed heroes: so prone are we to slide into this vice of handing out to a crowd what God strictly claims as his own peculiar right!

Calvin presses one searching demand: worship belongs to God alone, and whatever offices of piety are bestowed anywhere else than on him are, in his stark word, sacrilege. The clever distinction between serving and worshiping changes nothing—a soul that prostrates itself before any rival has already robbed God of what is his. Superstition, he observes, rarely denies the Most High outright; it simply crowds his throne with lesser claimants and parcels out his honor. The same crafty dividing of devotion runs through 2026, where few profess atheism yet many give God the highest place in name while reserving their real trust for self, success, the state, or the saints of the age. The watchful believer learns to test where his confidence actually rests when fear comes and prayer rises. Keep the worship of your home undivided—one God, one name, one undiminished allegiance—for he is a jealous God, and he will not share his glory with another.
