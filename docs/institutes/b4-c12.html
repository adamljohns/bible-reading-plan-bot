Book 4 · Chapter 12 · USMC modern English

Of the Discipline of the Church, and Its Principal Use in Censures and Excommunication

This chapter consists of two parts:—I. The first part of ecclesiastical discipline, which respects the people, and is called common, consists of two parts, the former depending on the power of the keys, which is considered, sec. 1-14; the latter consisting in the appointment of times for fasting and prayer, sec. 14-21. II. The second part of ecclesiastical discipline relating to the clergy, sec. 22-28. 1. Of the power of the keys, or the common discipline of the Church. Necessity and very great utility of this discipline. 2. Its various degrees. 1. Private admonition. 2. Rebukes before witnesses. 3. Excommunication. 3. Different degrees of delinquency. Modes of procedure in both kinds of chastisement. 4. Delicts to be distinguished from flagitious wickedness. The last to be more severely punished. 5. Ends of this discipline. 1. That the wicked may not, by being admitted to the Lord’s Table, put insult on Christ. 2. That they may not corrupt others. 3. That they themselves may repent. 6. In what way sins public as well as secret are to be corrected. Trivial and grave offences. 7. No person, not even the sovereign, exempted from this discipline. By whom and in what way it ought to be exercised. 8. In what spirit discipline is to be exercised. In what respect some of the ancient Christians exercised it too rigorously. This done more from custom than in accordance with their own sentiments. This shown from Cyprian, Chrysostom, and Augustine. 9. Moderation to be used, not only by the whole Church, but by each individual member. 10. Our Saviour’s words concerning binding and loosing wrested if otherwise understood. Difference between anathema and excommunication. Anathema rarely if ever to be used. 11. Excessive rigour to be avoided, as well by private individuals as by pastors. 12. In this respect the Donatists erred most grievously, as do also the Anabaptists in the present day. Portraiture by Augustine. 13. Moderation especially to be used when not a few individuals, but the great body of the people, have gone astray. 14. A second part of common discipline relating to fastings, prayer, and other holy exercises. These used by believers under both dispensations. To what purposes applied. Of Fasting. 15. Three ends of fasting. The first refers more especially to private fasting. Second and third ends. 16. Public fasting and prayer appointed by pastors on any great emergency. 17. Examples of this under the Law. 18. Fasting consists chiefly in three things—viz. time, the quality, and sparing use of food. 19. To prevent superstition, three things to be inculcated. 1. The heart to be rent, not the garments. 2. Fasting not to be regarded as a meritorious work or kind of divine worship. 3. Abstinence must not be immoderately extolled. 20. Owing to an excess of this kind the observance of Lent was established. This superstitious observance refuted by three arguments. It was indeed used by the ancients, but on different grounds. 21. Laws afterwards made to regulate the choice of food. Various abuses even in the time of Jerome. Practically there is no common ecclesiastical discipline in the Papacy. 22. The second part of discipline having reference to the clergy. What its nature, and how strict it formerly was. How miserably neglected in the present day. An example which may suit the Papists. 23. Of the celibacy of priests, in which Papists place the whole force of ecclesiastical discipline. This impious tyranny refuted from Scripture. An objection of the Papists disposed of. 24. An argument for the celibacy of priests answered. 25. Another argument answered. 26. Another argument answered. 27. An argument drawn from the commendation of virginity as superior to marriage. Answer. 28. The subject of celibacy concluded. This error not favoured by all ancient writers.

1¶

The discipline of the Church, the consideration of which has been put off until now, must be briefly explained, so that we may be able to pass on to other matters. Now discipline depends in very large measure on the power of the keys and on spiritual jurisdiction. So that this may be more easily understood, let us divide the Church into two principal classes — namely, clergy and people. The term clergy I use in its common sense for those who perform a public ministry in the Church. French, "J'use de ce mot de Cleres pource qu'il est commun, combien qu'il soit impropre; par lequel j'entens ceux qui ont office et ministere en l'Eglise." — I use this word Clergy because it is common, though it is improper; by it I mean those who have an office and ministry in the Church.

2¶

The first foundation of discipline is to make provision for private admonition; that is, if anyone does not do his duty of his own accord, or behaves insolently, or does not live quite honestly, or commits something worthy of blame, he must allow himself to be admonished; and everyone must make it his concern to admonish his brother when the case requires it. Here especially there is occasion for the vigilance of pastors and presbyters, whose duty is not only to preach to the people, but to exhort and admonish from house to house, whenever their hearers have not profited enough from general teaching. So Paul shows, when he relates that he taught "publicly, and from house to house," and testifies that he is "pure from the blood of all men," because he had not shunned to declare "all the counsel of God" (Acts 20:20, 26, 27). Then does doctrine gain force and authority: not only when the minister publicly expounds to all what they owe to Christ, but when he has the right and the means of requiring this from those whom he may observe to be sluggish or disobedient to his doctrine. Should anyone either perversely reject such admonitions, or show by persisting in his faults that he despises them, the command of Christ is that, after he has been admonished a second time before witnesses, he is to be summoned to the bar of the Church, which is the consistory of elders, and there admonished more sharply, as by public authority, so that if he reveres the Church he may submit and obey (Matthew 18:15, 17). If even in this way he is not subdued, but persists in his iniquity, he is then, as a despiser of the Church, to be shut out from the fellowship of believers.

3¶

But since our Savior is not speaking there of secret faults only, we must pay attention to the distinction that some sins are private, while others are public or openly manifest. Of the former, Christ says to every private individual, "go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone" (Matthew 18:15). Of open sins Paul says to Timothy, "Those that sin rebuke before all, that others also may fear" (1 Timothy 5:20). Our Savior had previously used the words, "If thy brother shall trespass against thee." Unless you mean to be captious, you cannot understand this clause otherwise than: if this happens in a way known to yourself, with others not privy to it. The command which Paul gave to Timothy, to rebuke openly those who sin openly, he himself followed in the case of Peter (Galatians 2:14). For when Peter sinned so as to give public offense, Paul did not admonish him privately, but brought him forward in the face of the Church. The lawful course, therefore, will be to proceed in correcting secret faults by the steps Christ mentioned, and in open sins accompanied by public scandal to proceed at once to the solemn correction of the Church.

4¶

Another distinction to be noted is that some sins are mere failings, while others are crimes and flagrant iniquities. In correcting the latter, it is necessary to use not only admonition or rebuke but a sharper remedy, as Paul shows when he not only rebukes the incestuous Corinthian in words, but punishes him with excommunication as soon as he was informed of his crime (1 Corinthians 5:4). Now, then, we begin to see more clearly how the spiritual jurisdiction of the Church, which takes notice of sins according to the word of the Lord, is at once the best help to sound doctrine, the best foundation of order, and the best bond of unity. Therefore, when the Church banishes from its fellowship open adulterers, fornicators, thieves, robbers, the seditious, the perjured, false witnesses, and others of that kind, and likewise the obstinate, who, when duly admonished for lighter faults, hold God and his tribunal in derision, it does not claim for itself anything unreasonable, but exercises a jurisdiction which it has received from the Lord. Moreover, lest anyone should despise the judgment of the Church, or count it a small matter to be condemned by the votes of the faithful, the Lord has declared that it is nothing else than the publishing of his own sentence, and that what they do on earth is ratified in heaven. For they act by the word of the Lord in condemning the perverse, and by the word of the Lord in taking the penitent back into favor (John 20:23). Those, I say, who trust that churches can stand for long without this bond of discipline are mistaken, unless indeed we can safely do without a help which the Lord foresaw would be necessary. And indeed, the greatness of the necessity will be better seen from its many uses.

5¶

There are three ends which the Church has in view in correcting and excommunicating in this way. The first is that God may not be insulted by giving the name of Christians to those who lead shameful and wicked lives, as if his holy Church were a league of the wicked and abandoned. For since the Church is the body of Christ, she cannot be defiled by such foul and rotten members without bringing some disgrace on her Head. Therefore, so that there may be nothing in the Church to bring disgrace on his sacred name, those whose vileness might bring infamy on that name must be expelled from his family. And here, too, regard must be had to the Lord's Supper, which might be profaned by indiscriminate admission. See Cyril on John, chapter 50, and Luther, On the Communion of the People, volume 2.

6¶

These being the ends in view, it remains to see in what way the Church is to carry out this part of discipline, which consists in jurisdiction. And first, let us remember the division laid down above, that some sins are public, others private or secret. Public sins are those which are done not before one or two witnesses, but openly, and to the offense of the whole Church. By secret sins I mean not those which are altogether hidden from men, such as the sins of hypocrites (for these do not fall under the judgment of the Church), but those of an intermediate kind, which are not without witnesses and yet are not public. The former class does not require the different steps which Christ lists; but whenever anything of that kind occurs, the Church ought to do her duty by summoning the offender and correcting him according to his fault. In the second class, the matter does not come before the Church unless there is obstinacy, according to the rule of Christ. In taking notice of offenses, it is necessary to observe the distinction between failings and flagrant iniquities. In lighter offenses there is not so much occasion for severity; verbal correction is enough, and that gentle and fatherly, so as not to exasperate or overwhelm the offender, but to bring him back to himself, so that he may rejoice in the correction rather than be grieved by it. Flagrant iniquities require a sharper remedy. It is not enough to rebuke in words the man who, by some open act of evil example, has grievously offended the Church; he ought for a time to be denied the communion of the Supper, until he gives proof of repentance. Paul does not merely give the Corinthian a verbal rebuke, but casts him out of the Church, and reproves the Corinthians for having put up with him so long (1 Corinthians 5:5). This was the method observed by the ancient and purer Church, when lawful government was in force. When anyone was guilty of some flagrant iniquity, and so caused scandal, he was first ordered to abstain from sharing in the sacred Supper, and afterward to humble himself before God and testify his repentance before the Church. There were, moreover, solemn rites which were customarily prescribed to those who had lapsed, as signs of repentance. When the penitent had in this way made satisfaction to the Church, he was received into favor by the laying on of hands. Cyprian often calls this admission peace, and briefly describes its form. Cyprian, book 1, letter 2; book 3, letters 14, 26.

7¶

So far was anyone from being exempt from this discipline that even princes submitted to it along with their subjects; and rightly so, since it is the discipline of Christ, to whom all scepters and crowns should be subject. Thus Theodosius. Ambrose, book 1, letter 3; and the Oration delivered at the Funeral of Theodosius.

8¶

It ought not, however, to be left out that the Church, in exercising severity, ought to accompany it with the spirit of meekness. For, as Paul commands, we must always take care that the one on whom discipline is exercised is not "swallowed up with overmuch sorrow" (2 Corinthians 2:7); for in that way, instead of a cure there would be destruction. The rule of moderation will best be drawn from the end in view. For the object of excommunication is to bring the sinner to repentance and to remove bad examples, so that the name of Christ may not be evil spoken of, nor others tempted to the same evil courses. If we consider this, we shall easily understand how far severity should be carried, and at what point it ought to stop. Therefore, when the sinner gives the Church evidence of his repentance, and by this evidence does all that lies in him to blot out the offense, he ought not on any account to be pressed further. If he is pressed, the rigor now exceeds due measure. In this respect it is impossible to excuse the excessive austerity of the ancients, which was altogether at odds with the command of our Lord, and strangely dangerous. For when they imposed a formal penance, and excluded from communion for three, or four, or seven years, or for life, what could the result be but either great hypocrisy or very great despair? In the same way, when anyone who had lapsed again was not admitted to a second repentance, but was cast out of the Church to the end of his life (Augustine, Letter 54), this was neither useful nor agreeable to reason. Whoever, therefore, looks at the matter with sound judgment will regret here a want of prudence. Here, however, I disapprove of the public custom rather than blame those who complied with it. Some of them certainly disapproved of it, but submitted to what they were unable to correct. Cyprian, indeed, declares that it was not by his own will that he was so rigorous. "Our patience, readiness, and kindness," he says (book 1, letter 3), "are ready for all who come. I wish all to be brought back into the Church: I wish all our fellow soldiers to be gathered within the camp of Christ and the dwellings of God the Father. I forgive all; I overlook much; from an earnest desire of gathering the brotherhood, I do not minutely examine all the faults which have been committed against God. I myself often err by forgiving offenses more than I ought. Those who return in repentance, and those who confess their sins with simple and humble satisfaction, I embrace with prompt and full delight." Chrysostom, who is somewhat more severe, still speaks this way: "If God is so kind, why should his priest wish to appear austere?" We know, moreover, how indulgently Augustine treated the Donatists, not hesitating to admit to their bishoprics any who returned from schism, as soon as they declared their repentance. But since a contrary method had prevailed, they were compelled to follow it and give up their own judgment.

9¶

But just as the whole body of the Church is required to act this mildly, and not to carry its rigor against those who have lapsed to an extreme, but rather to act charitably toward them, according to the precept of Paul, so every private individual ought in due proportion to conform himself to this clemency and kindness. It is not for us, therefore, to strike those who have been expelled from the Church out of the number of the elect, or to despair of them as if they were already lost. We may lawfully judge them strangers to the Church, and so strangers to Christ, but only during the time of their excommunication. If even then they give more evidence of insolence than of humility, let us still commit them to the judgment of the Lord, hoping better of them for the future than we see at present, and not ceasing to pray to God for them. And, to sum it up in one word, let us not consign to destruction their person, which is in the hand and subject to the decision of the Lord alone; but let us only weigh the character of each man's acts according to the law of the Lord. In following this rule, we abide by the divine judgment rather than give any judgment of our own. Let us not claim for ourselves greater liberty in judging, if we do not wish to limit the power of God and lay down the law to his mercy. Whenever it seems good to him, the worst are changed into the best; aliens are grafted in, and strangers are adopted into the Church. This the Lord does to frustrate the thoughts of men and confound their rashness, a rashness which, if it were not curbed, would seize a power of judging to which it has no claim.

10¶

For when our Savior promises that what his servants bound on earth would be bound in heaven (Matthew 18:18), he confines the power of binding to the censure of the Church. This censure does not consign those who are excommunicated to perpetual ruin and damnation, but assures them, when they hear their life and conduct condemned, that perpetual damnation will follow if they do not repent. Excommunication differs from anathema in this: the latter, completely shutting out pardon, dooms and devotes the person to eternal destruction, whereas the former rather rebukes and censures his conduct; and although it also punishes, it does so to bring him to salvation, by warning him in advance of his future doom. If it succeeds, reconciliation and restoration to communion are ready to be given. Moreover, anathema is to be used rarely, if ever. Hence, though ecclesiastical discipline does not allow us to be on familiar and intimate terms with excommunicated persons, we still ought to strive by every possible means to bring them to a better mind and recover them to the fellowship and unity of the Church, as the apostle also says, "Yet count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother" (2 Thessalonians 3:15). If this kindness is not observed in private as well as in public, the danger is that our discipline will degenerate into destruction. French, "Il y a danger, que de discipline nous ne tombions en une maniere de gehene, et que de correcteurs nous ne devenions bourreaux." — There is a danger that instead of discipline we fall into a kind of gehenna, and instead of correctors become executioners.

11¶

Another special requirement for moderation in discipline is, as Augustine argues against the Donatists, that private individuals must not immediately separate themselves from the Church when they see vices corrected less carefully by the Council of Elders; nor must pastors themselves, when they are unable to reform all that needs correction to the extent they could wish, throw up their ministry, or throw the whole Church into confusion by unusual severity. What Augustine says is perfectly true: "Whoever corrects what he can by rebuking it, or, without violating the bond of peace, excludes what he cannot correct, or unjustly condemns while he patiently tolerates what he is unable to exclude without violating the bond of peace, is free and exempt from the curse" (Augustine, Against Parmenian, book 2, chapter 4). Elsewhere he gives the reason. "Every godly method and manner of ecclesiastical discipline ought always to have regard to the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. This the apostle commands us to keep by bearing with one another. If it is not kept, the medicine of discipline begins to be not only superfluous but even harmful, and therefore ceases to be medicine" (ibid., book 3, chapter 1). "He who carefully considers these things neither neglects strictness of discipline in preserving unity, nor bursts the bond of fellowship by intemperate correction" (ibid., chapter 2). He confesses, indeed, that not only ought pastors to exert themselves in removing every defect from the Church, but every individual ought to do so to the utmost; nor does he hide the fact that he who neglects to admonish, accuse, and correct the wicked, although he neither favors them nor sins with them, is guilty before the Lord; and if he conducts himself so that, though he can exclude them from partaking of the Supper, he does not do it, then the sin is no longer that of other men, but his own. Only he would have that prudence used which our Lord also requires, "lest while ye gather up the tares, ye root up also the wheat with them" (Matthew 13:29). Hence he draws this from Cyprian: "Let a man, then, mercifully correct what he can; what he cannot correct, let him bear patiently, and in love bewail and lament."

12¶

This he says on account of the sullen harshness of the Donatists, who, when they saw faults in the Church which the bishops rebuked in words but did not punish with excommunication (because they did not think that anything would be gained in that way), bitterly attacked the bishops as traitors to discipline, and by an impious schism separated themselves from the flock of Christ. Similar in our own day is the conduct of the Anabaptists, who, acknowledging no assembly of Christ unless it is conspicuous in every respect for angelic perfection, overthrow under pretense of zeal everything that tends to edification. See a lengthy refutation in Calvin, Instruction against the Anabaptists, article 2. See also Calvin, On the Lord's Supper.

13¶

One thing Augustine especially commends — namely, that if the contagion of sin has seized the multitude, mercy must accompany living discipline. "For counsels of separation are vain, sacrilegious, and harmful, because they are impious and proud, and do more to disturb the weak good than to correct the wicked proud" (Augustine, Letter 64). What he urges on others he himself faithfully practiced. For, writing to Aurelius, Bishop of Carthage, he complains that drunkenness, which is so severely condemned in Scripture, prevails in Africa without punishment, and he advises that a council of bishops be called for the purpose of providing a remedy. He immediately adds, "In my opinion, such things are not removed by rough, harsh, and imperious measures, but more by teaching than by commanding, more by admonishing than by threatening. For this is how we ought to act with a multitude of offenders. Severity is to be exercised against the sins of a few" (Augustine, Letter 64). He does not mean, however, that the bishops were to wink at public offenses or be silent because they are unable to punish them severely, as he himself afterward explains. But he wishes to temper the manner of correction, so as to give soundness to the body rather than cause its destruction. And accordingly he concludes in this way: "Therefore we must on no account neglect the command of the apostle to separate from the wicked, when it can be done without the risk of violating peace, because he did not wish it to be done otherwise (1 Corinthians 5:13); we must also endeavor, by bearing with one another, to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace" (Ephesians 4:2).

14¶

The remaining part of discipline, which is not, strictly speaking, included in the power of the keys, is when pastors, according to the necessity of the times, exhort the people either to fasting and solemn prayer, or to other exercises of humiliation, repentance, and faith; the time, manner, and form of these are not prescribed by the Word of God, but left to the judgment of the Church. As the observance of this part of discipline is useful, so it was always used in the Church, even from the days of the apostles. Indeed, the apostles themselves were not its first authors, but borrowed the example from the Law and the Prophets. For we see there, See a striking instance in Ezra 8:21, on the appointment of a fast at the river Ahava, on the return of the people from the Babylonian captivity.

French "Quand il advient quelque different en Chretienté, qui tire grande consequence." — When some dispute on a matter of great consequence arises in Christendom.

15¶

A holy and lawful fast has three ends in view. We use it either to mortify and subdue the flesh, so that it may not run riot; or to prepare ourselves better for prayer and holy meditation; or to give evidence of humbling ourselves before God when we wish to confess our guilt before him. The first end is not very often in view in public fasting, because not all have the same bodily constitution or the same state of health, and so it applies more to private fasting. The second end is common to both, for this preparation for prayer is needed by the whole Church as well as by each individual member. The same may be said of the third. For it sometimes happens that God strikes a nation with war or pestilence or some other kind of calamity. Under this common chastisement it is fitting for the whole people to plead guilty and confess their guilt. Should the hand of the Lord strike anyone privately, then the same thing is to be done by him alone, or by his family. The thing itself, indeed, is properly a disposition of the mind. But when the mind is affected as it ought to be, it cannot help giving vent to itself in outward expression, especially when this tends to the common edification, so that all, by openly confessing their sin, may give praise to the divine justice and by their example encourage one another.

16¶

Hence fasting, as a sign of humiliation, has a more frequent use in public than among private individuals, although, as we have said, it is common to both. As regards, then, the discipline of which we are now treating, whenever supplication is to be made to God on any important occasion, it is fitting to appoint a time for fasting and prayer. Thus when the Christians of Antioch laid hands on Barnabas and Paul, in order to commend more fully their ministry, which was of such great importance, they joined fasting with prayer (Acts 13:3). So these two apostles afterward, when they appointed ministers to churches, were accustomed to use prayer and fasting (Acts 14:23). In general, the only object they had in fasting was to make themselves more alert and unencumbered for prayer. We certainly find by experience that after a full meal the mind does not rise toward God in such a way as to be carried along by an earnest and fervent longing for prayer and perseverance in prayer. In this sense we are to understand the saying of Luke concerning Anna, that she "served God with fastings and prayers, night and day" (Luke 2:37). For he does not place the worship of God in fasting, but indicates that in this way the holy woman trained herself to constancy in prayer. Such was the fast of Nehemiah, when with more intense zeal he prayed to God for the deliverance of his people (Nehemiah 1:4). For this reason Paul says that married believers do well to abstain for a season (1 Corinthians 7:5), so that they may have greater freedom for prayer and fasting; and by joining fasting to prayer as a help, he reminds us that it is of no importance in itself, except insofar as it serves this end. Again, when in the same place he commands spouses to render due benevolence to one another, it is clear that he is not referring to daily prayer, but to prayers which require more than ordinary attention.

17¶

On the other hand, when pestilence begins to stalk abroad, or famine or war, or when any other disaster seems to hang over a province and people (Esther 4:16), then also it is the duty of pastors to exhort the Church to fasting, so that she may humbly entreat the Lord to turn away his anger. For when he makes danger appear, he declares that he is ready and, as it were, armed for vengeance. In the same way, therefore, as accused persons in ancient times were accustomed, in order to stir the pity of the judge, to humble themselves as suppliants with long beard, disheveled hair, and coarse garments, so when we are charged before the divine tribunal, to plead against his severity in humble clothing is equally for his glory and the public edification, and useful and wholesome for ourselves. And that this was common among the Israelites we may infer from the words of Joel. For when he says, "Blow the trumpet in Zion, sanctify a fast, call a solemn assembly," and so on (Joel 2:15), he speaks of these as things received by common custom. A little before, he had said that the people were to be tried for their wickedness and that the day of judgment was at hand, and he had summoned them as criminals to plead their cause; then he cries out that they should hasten to sackcloth and ashes, to weeping and fasting; that is, that they should humble themselves before God with outward expressions. The sackcloth and ashes, indeed, were perhaps more suited to those times, but the assembly, and weeping and fasting, and the like, undoubtedly belong equally to our age, whenever the state of our affairs requires it. For since it is a holy exercise both for men to humble themselves and to confess their humility, why should we, in similar need, use it less than those of old did? We read not only that the Israelite Church, formed and established by the word of God, fasted in token of sorrow, but the Ninevites also, whose only teaching had been the preaching of Jonah. 1 Samuel 7:6; 31:13; 2 Kings 1:12; Jonah 3:5.

18¶

But so that there may be no error about the name, let us define what fasting is; for we do not understand by it simply a restrained and sparing use of food, but something else. The life of the godly should be tempered with frugality and sobriety, so as to display, as far as possible, a kind of fasting throughout the whole course of life. But there is another, temporary fast, when we cut back somewhat from our accustomed way of living, either for one day or for a certain period, and prescribe for ourselves a stricter and more severe restraint in the use of ordinary food. This consists in three things — namely, the time, the quality of the food, and the sparing use of it. By the time I mean that while fasting we are to perform those actions for the sake of which the fast is appointed. For example, when a man fasts on account of solemn prayer, he should engage in it without having taken food. The quality consists in putting all luxury aside and being content with common and plainer food, so as not to excite our palate with delicacies. As regards quantity, we must eat more lightly and sparingly, only for necessity and not for pleasure.

19¶

But the first thing always to be guarded against is the creeping in of superstition, as happened in former times, to the great injury of the Church. It would have been much better to have no fasting at all than to have it carefully observed but at the same time corrupted by false and harmful opinions, into which the world is continually falling unless pastors prevent them with the greatest faithfulness and prudence. The first thing is constantly to press the command of Joel, "Rend your heart, and not your garments" (Joel 2:13); that is, to remind the people that fasting in itself is of no great value in the sight of God unless it is accompanied by the inward affection of the heart, true displeasure with sin and with oneself, true humiliation, and true grief arising from the fear of God; indeed, that fasting is useful for no other reason than because it is added to these as a lesser help. There is nothing God abhors more than when men try to cloak themselves by putting signs and outward appearance in the place of integrity of heart. Accordingly, Isaiah inveighs most bitterly against the hypocrisy of the Jews in thinking that they had satisfied God when they had merely fasted, whatever impiety and impure thoughts they cherished in their hearts. "Is it such a fast that I have chosen?" (Isaiah 58:5). See also what follows. The fast of hypocrites, therefore, is not only useless and superfluous fatigue, but the greatest abomination. Another evil akin to this, and greatly to be avoided, is to regard fasting as a meritorious work and a kind of divine worship. For since it is a thing indifferent in itself, and has no importance except on account of the ends it ought to serve, it is a most harmful superstition to confuse it with the works commanded by God, which are necessary in themselves without reference to anything else. Such was the dream of the Manichees in ancient times, in refuting whom Augustine clearly shows, Augustine, On the Morals of the Manichees, book 2, chapter 13; and Against Faustus, book 30.

See Chrysostom, Homily at the beginning of Lent, where he calls fasting a cure of souls and a washing away of sins.

20¶

Then the superstitious observance of Lent had prevailed everywhere: for the common people imagined that by it they were performing some excellent service to God, and pastors commended it as a holy imitation of Christ; though it is plain that Christ did not fast to set an example for others, but, by beginning the preaching of the gospel in this way, meant to prove that his doctrine was not of men but had come from heaven. And it is strange how men of keen judgment could fall into this gross delusion, which so many clear reasons refute. For Christ did not fast repeatedly (which he must have done had he meant to lay down a law for an annual fast), but once only, when preparing for the proclamation of the gospel. Nor does he fast in the manner of men, as he would have done had he meant to invite men to imitate him; rather, he gives an example by which he may move all to admire him rather than strive to imitate him. In short, the nature of his fast is no different from that which Moses kept when he received the law from the hand of the Lord (Exodus 24:18; 34:28). For since that miracle was performed in Moses to establish the law, it was fitting that it should not be left out in Christ, lest the gospel should seem inferior to the law. But from that day it never occurred to anyone, under pretense of imitating Moses, to set up a similar form of fast among the Israelites. Nor did any of the holy prophets and fathers follow it, though they had inclination and zeal enough for all godly exercises; for though it is said of Elijah that he went forty days without food and drink (1 Kings 19:8), this was only so that the people might recognize that he was raised up to uphold the law, from which almost the whole of Israel had revolted. It was, therefore, merely false zeal, full of superstition, which set up a fast under the title and pretext of imitating Christ; although there was then a strange diversity in the manner of the fast, as Cassiodorus relates in the ninth book of the History of Socrates: "The Romans," he says, "had only three weeks, but their fast was continuous, except on the Lord's day and the Sabbath. The Greeks and Illyrians had, some six weeks, others seven, but the fast was at intervals. Nor did they differ less in the kind of food: some used only bread and water, others added vegetables; others had no objection to fish and fowl; others made no difference in their food." Augustine also mentions this difference in his latter letter to Januarius.

21¶

Worse times followed. To the absurd zeal of the common people were added crudeness and ignorance in the bishops, lust for power, and tyrannical rigor. Impious laws were passed, binding the conscience in deadly chains. The eating of meat was forbidden, as if a man were defiled by it. Sacrilegious opinions were added one after another, until the whole became an abyss of error. And so that no kind of depravity might be left out, they began, under a most absurd pretense of abstinence, to make a mockery of God; Bernard, in Sermon 1 on Easter Day, censures among others princes also, for longing during the season of Lent for the approaching festival of our Lord's resurrection, so that they might indulge themselves more freely.

22¶

We come now to the second part of discipline, which relates especially to the clergy. It is contained in the canons which the ancient bishops framed for themselves and their order: for instance, let no clergyman spend his time in hunting, in gambling, or in feasting; let none engage in usury or in trade; let none be present at lewd dances; and the like. Penalties were also added to give force to the authority of the canons, so that none might violate them with impunity. With this in view, each bishop was entrusted with the oversight of his own clergy, so that he might govern them according to the canons and keep them to their duty. For this purpose certain annual visitations and synods were appointed, so that if anyone was negligent in his office he might be admonished, and if anyone sinned he might be punished according to his fault. The bishops also had their provincial synods once a year, and in ancient times twice, by which they were tried if they had done anything contrary to their duty. For if any bishop had been too harsh or violent with his clergy, there was an appeal to the synod, even if only one person complained. The severest punishment was deposition from office and exclusion from communion for a time. And since this was the settled arrangement, no synod rose without fixing the time and place of the next meeting. To call a universal council belonged to the emperor alone, as all the ancient summonses testify. As long as this strictness was in force, the clergy required no more of the people in word than they themselves performed in deed and by example; indeed, they were stricter with themselves than with the common people. And truly it is fitting that the people should be ruled by a gentler and, if I may so speak, looser discipline, while the clergy should be stricter in their censures and less indulgent to themselves than to others. How this whole procedure fell into disuse it is needless to relate, since in our day nothing can be imagined more lawless and dissolute than this order, whose license is so extreme that the whole world is crying out. I admit that, so as not to seem to have lost all sight of antiquity, they deceive the eyes of the simple with certain shadows; but these no more resemble the ancient customs than the mimicry of an ape resembles what men do by reason and counsel. There is a memorable passage in Xenophon, in which he relates that when the Persians had shamefully degenerated from the customs of their ancestors, and had fallen away from an austere way of life into luxury and effeminacy, they still, to hide the disgrace, were careful to observe the ancient rites (Cyropaedia, book 8). For while in the time of Cyrus sobriety and temperance so flourished that no Persian needed to wipe his nose, and it was even thought disgraceful to do so, it remained with their descendants as a point of religion not to remove the mucus from the nostril, though they were allowed to nourish within, even to rottenness, the foul humors they had contracted by gluttony. In the same way, by the ancient custom it was unlawful to use cups at table; but it was quite tolerable to gulp down wine until one had to be carried off drunk. It was prescribed to take only one meal a day; this their good successors did not abolish, but they kept up their gorging from midday to midnight. To finish the day's march fasting, as the law required, was the settled custom; but to avoid weariness, the permitted and usual practice was to limit the march to two hours. As often as the degenerate Papists thrust forward their rules to show their resemblance to the holy fathers, this example will serve to expose their ridiculous imitation. Indeed, no painter could portray them more to the life.

23¶

In one thing they are more than rigid and unbending — in not permitting priests to marry. There is no need to mention with what impunity whoredom prevails among them, and how, trusting to their vile celibacy, they have become hardened to every kind of iniquity. The prohibition, however, clearly shows how pestilent all traditions are, since this one has not only robbed the Church of fit and honest pastors, but has brought in a fearful sink of iniquity and plunged many souls into the gulf of despair. Certainly, when marriage was forbidden to priests, it was done with impious tyranny, not only contrary to the word of God but contrary to all justice. First, men had no right whatever to forbid what God had left free; second, it is too clear to need any lengthy proof that God has expressly provided in his Word that this liberty shall not be infringed. I pass over Paul's command, in numerous passages, that a bishop be the husband of one wife; but what could be stronger than his declaration that in the latter days there would be impious men "forbidding to marry"? (1 Timothy 4:3). Such persons he calls not only impostors but devils. We have, therefore, a prophecy, a sacred oracle of the Holy Spirit, intended to warn the Church from the beginning against dangers, and declaring that the prohibition of marriage is a doctrine of devils. They think they escape very neatly when they twist this passage and apply it to Montanus, the Tatians, the Encratites, and other ancient heretics. These alone, they say, condemned marriage; we by no means condemn it, but only deny it to the ecclesiastical order, for whom we think it unfitting. As if, even granting that this prophecy was fulfilled first in those heretics, it did not also apply to themselves; or as if anyone could listen to the childish quibble that they do not forbid marriage because they do not forbid it to all. This is just as if a tyrant were to argue that a law is not unjust because its injustice presses on only a part of the state.

24¶

They object that there ought to be some distinguishing mark between the clergy and the people; as if the Lord had not provided the ornaments in which priests ought to excel. In this way they charge the apostle with having disturbed the ecclesiastical order and destroyed its ornament when, in drawing the picture of a perfect bishop, he dared to set down marriage among the other qualities he required of them. I am aware of how they explain this — namely, that no one was to be made a bishop who had a second wife. This interpretation, I admit, is not new; but its unsoundness is plain from the immediate context, which prescribes what kind of wives bishops and deacons ought to have. Paul lists marriage among the qualities of a bishop; these men declare that in the ecclesiastical order marriage is an intolerable vice; and indeed, not content with this general abuse, they call it in their canons the uncleanness and pollution of the flesh (Siricius, to the Bishops of Spain). Let everyone consider for himself from what forge these things have come. Christ condescends to honor marriage so highly as to make it an image of his sacred union with the Church. What greater praise could be spoken of the dignity of marriage? How, then, do they have the effrontery to call unclean and polluted that which furnishes a bright representation of the spiritual grace of Christ?

25¶

Though their prohibition is so clearly contrary to the word of God, they nevertheless find something in the Scriptures to defend it. The Levitical priests, as often as their turn of ministry came round, had to keep apart from their wives, so that they might be pure and spotless in handling sacred things; and it would therefore be very unseemly, they say, that our sacred things, which are nobler and are ministered every day, should be handled by those who are married: as if the evangelical ministry were of the same character as the Levitical priesthood. Those priests, as types, represented Christ, who, as Mediator between God and men, was by his own spotless purity to reconcile us to the Father. But since sinners could not in every respect display a type of his holiness, they were commanded, so that they might at least shadow it forth by certain outlines, to purify themselves beyond the ordinary manner of men when they approached the sanctuary, inasmuch as they then properly prefigured Christ appearing in the tabernacle, an image of the heavenly tribunal, as peacemakers to reconcile men to God. Since ecclesiastical pastors do not bear this character in our day, the comparison is made in vain. Therefore the apostle declares plainly, without reservation, "Marriage is honorable in all, and the bed undefiled; but whoremongers and adulterers God will judge" (Hebrews 13:4). And the apostles showed by their own example that marriage is not unfitting for the holiness of any office, however excellent; for Paul declares that they not only kept their wives but took them about with them (1 Corinthians 9:5).

26¶

How great, then, is the effrontery when, in holding up this ornament of chastity as a matter of necessity, they cast the greatest reproach on the primitive Church, which, while it abounded in admirable divine learning, excelled still more in holiness. For if they pay no regard to the apostles (they are sometimes accustomed to despise them boldly), what, I ask, will they make of all the ancient fathers, who, it is certain, not only tolerated marriage in the episcopal order but also approved it? These fathers, no doubt, encouraged a foul profanation of sacred things when the mysteries of the Lord were performed so irregularly by them! In the Council of Nicaea, indeed, there was some question of decreeing celibacy, as there are never lacking little men of superstitious minds who are always devising some novelty as a means of winning admiration for themselves. What was resolved? The opinion of Paphnutius was adopted, who declared lawful conjugal intercourse to be chastity (Tripartite History, book 2, chapter 14). The marriage of priests, therefore, remained sacred, and was neither regarded as a disgrace nor thought to cast any stain on their ministry.

27¶

In the times that followed, a too superstitious admiration of celibacy prevailed. Hence boundless praises were again and again pronounced on virginity, so that it became the common belief that scarcely any virtue could be compared to it. And although marriage was not condemned as impurity, its dignity was lessened and its sanctity obscured, so that he who did not refrain from it was thought not to have a mind strong enough to aspire to perfection. Hence came those canons which enacted, first, that those who had attained the priesthood should not contract marriage; and second, that none should be admitted to that order but the unmarried, or those who, with the consent of their wives, renounced the marriage bed. These enactments, since they seemed to win reverence for the priesthood, were, I admit, received with great applause even in ancient times. But if my opponents plead antiquity, my first answer is that both under the apostles and for several ages afterward bishops were free to have wives; that the apostles themselves, and other pastors of primitive authority who succeeded them, had no hesitation in using this liberty; and that the example of the primitive Church ought rightly to carry too much weight to allow us to think that what was then received and used with approval is either unlawful or unbecoming. My second answer is that the age which, from an immoderate affection for virginity, began to be less favorable to marriage did not bind a law of celibacy on the priests as if the thing were necessary in itself, but gave a preference to the unmarried over the married. My last answer is that they did not require this so rigidly as to make continence necessary and compulsory for those who were unfit for it. For while the strictest laws were made against fornication, the only enactment regarding those who contracted marriage was that they should be removed from their office.

28¶

Therefore, as often as the defenders of this new tyranny appeal to antiquity in defense of their celibacy, so often should we call on them to restore the ancient chastity of their priests; to put away adulterers and whoremongers; not to allow those to whom they deny an honorable and chaste use of marriage to rush with impunity into every kind of lust; to bring back that obsolete discipline by which all license is restrained; and to free the Church from the shameful vileness by which it has long been disfigured. When they have granted this, they will next need to be reminded not to represent as necessary that which, being free in itself, depends on the usefulness of the Church. I do not, however, speak this way as if I thought that on any condition whatever effect should be given to those canons which lay a bond of celibacy on the ecclesiastical order, but so that the better-minded may understand the effrontery of our enemies in using the name of antiquity to defame the holy marriage of priests. As for the Fathers whose writings are extant, none of them, when they spoke their own mind, with the exception of Jerome, so spitefully detracted from the honor of marriage. We will be content with a single passage from Chrysostom, because he, being a special admirer of virginity, cannot be thought more lavish than others in praising matrimony. Chrysostom speaks this way: "The first degree of chastity is pure virginity; the second, faithful marriage. Therefore a chaste love of matrimony is the second kind of virginity" (Chrysostom, Homily on the Finding of the Cross).

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Discipline, Calvin says, is the sinew that holds the body together—not cruelty but the wound of a friend, moving by gradual steps from private admonition to public rebuke to excommunication, always aiming at the sinner's repentance and never at his despair, and never sparing even the prince. He warns with equal force against the rigor of the Donatist who abandons a flock for its imperfections and the laxity that lets the leaven spread unchecked. For families and churches in 2026, where accountability is mistaken for unkindness and tolerance for love, this is a steadying word: discipline that flows from meekness, that bears patiently with the weak while refusing to bless what God condemns, is one of the surest marks that a people takes Christ seriously. Fast, pray, humble yourselves when his hand grows heavy, and correct one another as those who must one day give account—for a house that will not be admonished is already coming apart, however peaceful it looks.
