Refutation of the Calumnies by Which This Doctrine Is Always Unjustly Assailed

This chapter consists of four parts, which refute the principal objections to this doctrine, and the various pleas and exceptions founded on these objections. These are preceded by a refutation of those who hold election but deny reprobation, sec. 1. Then follows, I. A refutation of the first objection to the doctrine of reprobation and election, sec. 2-5. II. An answer to the second objection, sec. 6-9. III. A refutation of the third objection. IV. A refutation of the fourth objection; to which is added a useful and necessary caution, sec. 12-14. 1. Error of those who deny reprobation. 1. Election opposed to reprobation. 2. Those who deny reprobation presumptuously plead with God, whose counsels even angels adore. 3. They murmur against God when disclosing his counsels by the Apostle. Exception and answer. Passage of Augustine. 2. First objection—viz. that God is unjustly offended with those whom he dooms to destruction without their own desert. First answer, from the consideration of the divine will. The nature of this will, and how to be considered. 3. Second answer. God owes nothing to man. His hatred against those who are corrupted by sin is most just. The reprobate convinced in their own consciences of the just judgment of God. 4. Exception—viz. that the reprobate seem to have been preordained to sin. Answer. Passage of the Apostle vindicated from calumny. 5. Answer, confirmed by the authority of Augustine. Illustration. Passage of Augustine. 6. Objection, that God ought not to impute the sins rendered necessary by his predestination. First answer, by ancient writers. This not valid. Second answer also defective. Third answer, proposed by Valla, well founded. 7. Objection, that God did not decree that Adam should perish by his fall, refuted by a variety of reasons. A noble passage of Augustine. 8. Objection, that the wicked perish by the permission, not by the will of God. Answer. A pious exhortation. 9. Objection and answer. 10. Objection, that, according to the doctrine of predestination, God is a respecter of persons. Answer. 11. Objection, that sinners are to be punished equally, or the justice of God is unequal. Answer. Confirmed by passages of Augustine. 12. Objection, that the doctrine of predestination produces overweening confidence and impiety. Different answers. 13. Another objection, depending on the former. Answer. The doctrine of predestination to be preached, not passed over in silence. 14. How it is to be preached and delivered to the people. Summary of the orthodox doctrine of predestination, from Augustine.

1¶

When the human mind hears this doctrine, it cannot hold back its outrage. It boils and rages as though stirred up by the blast of a trumpet. Many people claim they want to defend God against a hateful charge, and so they accept the doctrine of election but deny that anyone is reprobated. They do this out of ignorance, like children, because there could be no election at all without its opposite, reprobation. God is said to set apart those whom he adopts for salvation. It would be utterly absurd to say that he admits others merely by chance, or that those others somehow earn by their own effort what election alone grants to a few. So then, those whom God passes by, he reprobates, and for no other reason than that he chooses to shut them out from the inheritance he predestines for his children. We must not put up with people who refuse to be restrained by the Word of God when it speaks of his unsearchable plan, a plan that even the angels worship. We have already been shown that hardening comes just as directly from the hand of God as mercy does. Paul does not, like the men I mentioned, anxiously try to defend God by calling in the help of falsehood. He simply reminds us that it is not lawful for the creature to argue with its Creator. So how will those who refuse to admit that anyone is reprobated by God explain these words of Christ: 'Every plant which my heavenly Father has not planted shall be rooted up' (Matthew 15:13)? They are told plainly that all whom the heavenly Father has not been pleased to plant as sacred trees in his garden are destined and given over to destruction. If they deny that this is a sign of reprobation, then nothing, however clear, can ever be proved to them. But if they still want to grumble, let us, in the soberness of faith, rest content with Paul's instruction: there can be no ground for complaint that God, 'willing to show his wrath, and to make his power known, endured with much long-suffering the vessels of wrath fitted for destruction; and that he might make known the riches of his glory on the vessels of mercy, which he had afore prepared unto glory' (Romans 9:22, 23). Notice that Paul, in order to cut off every excuse for grumbling and slander, attributes supreme sovereignty to the wrath and power of God. For it would be unjust to subject those deep judgments, which lie far beyond all our power to discern, to our own reckoning. Our opponents reply, weakly, that God does not utterly reject those whom he merely tolerates for a while, but holds back in case they might repent, as if Paul were picturing God patiently waiting for the conversion of the very people he describes as fitted for destruction. But Augustine, rightly explaining this passage, says that where power is joined to endurance, God does not merely permit; he rules (Augustine, Against Julian, Book 5, ch. 5). They add as well that it is not without reason that the vessels of wrath are said to be fitted for destruction, while God is said to have prepared the vessels of mercy. In this way, they argue, the praise of salvation is given to God, while the blame for ruin is laid on those who bring it on themselves of their own accord. But even if I were to grant that Paul, by using these different forms of expression, softens the harshness of the earlier clause, it does not follow at all that he traces the preparation for destruction to any cause other than the secret counsel of God. In fact, this is asserted in the verses just before, where God is said to have raised up Pharaoh and to harden whomever he wills. It follows, then, that the hidden counsel of God is the cause of the hardening. With Augustine, I hold at least this much: when God makes sheep out of wolves, he forms them anew by the powerful working of grace, so that their hardness is subdued. And he does not convert the stubborn, because he does not bring to bear that more powerful grace which he has at his command and could use if he chose (Augustine, On the Predestination of the Saints, Book 1, ch. 2).

2¶

These remarks would be more than enough for those who are devout and humble, and who remember that they are only men. But because there are so many forms of blasphemy that these vicious dogs spit out against God, we will, as far as the subject allows, give an answer to each one. Foolish men raise many grounds for quarreling with God, as though they could hold him answerable to their accusations. First, they ask why God is offended with creatures who have not provoked him by any earlier offense. To hand over to destruction whomever he pleases, they say, looks more like the whim of a tyrant than the lawful sentence of a judge. And so, they think, there is good reason to take God to task if, simply because it pleases him, men are predestined to eternal death without having deserved it at all. If thoughts like this ever cross the minds of the godly, they will be well enough armed to put them down once they consider how sinful it is to insist on knowing the reasons behind the divine will. For that will is itself the cause of everything that exists, and rightly so. If his will had some cause, there would have to be something prior to it and binding upon it, which it would be wicked even to imagine. The will of God is the supreme rule of righteousness. (This is drawn from Augustine, On Genesis Against the Manichees, Book 1, ch. 3.)

(Translator's note from the French: 'Even so, in speaking this way, we do not approve of the daydream of the Popish theologians concerning the absolute power of God.')

3¶

God could simply silence his enemies and say nothing. But so that we will not let them mock his holy name without consequence, he gives us weapons against them from his Word. So when we are confronted with words like these, 'Why did God from the very first predestine some to death, when they did not yet even exist and so could not have deserved a sentence of death?', let us answer with a question of our own: What do you suppose God owes to man, if he chooses to weigh him by his own nature? Since we are all corrupted by sin, we cannot help but be hateful to God, and that not because of any tyrannical cruelty in him, but by the strictest justice. But if all whom the Lord predestines to death are by their nature already liable to a sentence of death, then what injustice, I ask, do they have to complain about? Suppose all the sons of Adam were to come and argue and contend with their Creator, because by his eternal providence they were doomed to everlasting destruction before they were ever born. When God comes to settle accounts with them, what will they be able to mutter against this defense? If all are taken from a corrupt mass, it is no surprise that all are subject to condemnation. So let them not charge God with injustice if, by his eternal judgment, they are sentenced to a death toward which they themselves feel they are drawn willingly, by their own nature, whether they like it or not. This shows how twisted their habit of grumbling is, when they deliberately suppress the cause of their condemnation, which they are forced to recognize in themselves, just so they can pin the blame on God. But even if I were to admit a hundred times over that God is the author of it, and it is most certainly true that he is, that still does not wipe away their own guilt, which is carved into their consciences and keeps presenting itself before their eyes again and again.

4¶

They raise another objection: Were men not predestined, by the ordaining will of God, to that corruption which is now held up as the cause of their condemnation? If so, then when they perish in their corruption, they are doing nothing more than suffering punishment for the disaster into which Adam fell by God's predestination, dragging all his descendants headlong with him. Is God not therefore unjust in mocking his creatures so cruelly? I admit that, by the will of God, all the sons of Adam fell into the state of misery in which they are now caught up. This is exactly what I said at the start: we must always come back to the mere good pleasure of the divine will, whose cause is hidden in God himself. But it does not immediately follow that God is open to this charge. For we will answer with Paul in these words: 'Nay but, O man, who art thou that repliest against God? Shall the thing formed say to him that formed it, Why hast thou made me thus? Hath not the potter power over the clay, of the same lump to make one vessel unto honour, and another unto dishonour?' (Romans 9:20, 21). They will deny that God's justice is really defended this way. They will claim we are looking for a dodge, the kind that people use when they have no good excuse. For, they ask, what more is being said here than that God's power is such that nothing can stop it, so that he can do whatever he pleases? But the truth is far otherwise. For what stronger reason could be given than to be told to consider who God is? How could the one who is the Judge of the whole world commit any unrighteousness? If doing judgment belongs to the very nature of God, then he must by nature love justice and hate injustice. So the Apostle did not, like a man caught in a tight spot, resort to a dodge. He only meant that the way divine justice works is too high to be measured by human standards or grasped by the weakness of human understanding. The Apostle does confess that in the judgments of God there is a depth in which the minds of all men must be swallowed up if they try to plunge into it. But he also shows how unfitting it is to reduce the works of God to a standard by which we presume to condemn them the moment they fail to line up with our reasoning. There is a well-known saying of Solomon, though few understand it properly: 'The great God that formed all things both rewardeth the fool, and rewardeth transgressors' (Proverbs 26:10). For Solomon is speaking of the greatness of God, who is pleased to inflict punishment on fools and wrongdoers, while he is not pleased to give them his Spirit. It is a monstrous folly for men to try to confine within the little measure of their reason something that has no limits at all. Paul gives the name 'elect' to the angels who kept their integrity. If their steadfastness was due to the good pleasure of God, then the rebellion of the others proves that they were abandoned. (Translator's note from the French: 'If their constancy and firmness was founded on the good pleasure of God, the revolt of the devils shows that they were not held back, but rather abandoned.')

6¶

Ungodliness raises yet another objection. This one does not so much accuse God as it tries to excuse the sinner, though anyone whom God condemns as a sinner cannot in the end be acquitted without calling the Judge himself into question. Here, then, is the scoffing language that profane tongues use: Why should God blame men for things he himself has made necessary by his own predestination? What could they do? Could they fight against his decrees? It would be useless for them to try, since they could not possibly win. Therefore, they say, it is not just to punish them for things whose main cause lies in God's predestination. Here I will set aside the defense that church writers usually fall back on, namely, that there is nothing in God's foreknowledge to keep him from regarding man as a sinner, since the evils he foresees are man's, not his own. This would not stop the scoffer, who would still insist that God could have prevented the evils he foresaw if he had wished, and that, since he did not, he must have deliberately created man for the very purpose of acting as he does on the earth. But if, by the providence of God, man was created on the condition that he would afterward do whatever he in fact does, then what he cannot escape, and what he is compelled by the will of God to do, cannot be charged against him as a crime. So let us see the proper way to resolve the difficulty. First, everyone must grant what Solomon says: 'The Lord hath made all things for himself: yea, even the wicked for the day of evil' (Proverbs 16:4). Now, since the ordering of all things is in the hand of God, and since to him belongs the disposal of life and death, he arranges everything by his sovereign counsel, in such a way that some individuals are born who are doomed from the womb to certain death, and who are to glorify him by their destruction. If anyone claims that no necessity is laid on them by the providence of God, but rather that they are created in that condition because he foresaw their future depravity, that person says something, but not enough. The ancient writers do sometimes use this solution, though with some hesitation. The Schoolmen, on the other hand, rest in it as if it could not be challenged. For my part, I am willing to admit that mere foreknowledge lays no necessity on creatures, though some do not agree with this and hold that foreknowledge is itself the cause of things. But Valla, though otherwise not deeply skilled in sacred matters, seems to me to have taken a sharper and shrewder view when he shows that the whole dispute is beside the point, since life and death are acts of the divine will rather than of foreknowledge. If God merely foresaw human events, and did not also arrange and order them as he pleased, there might be room to debate how far his foreknowledge amounts to necessity. But since he foresees the things that are to happen simply because he has decreed that they will happen, it is pointless to argue about foreknowledge when it is clear that all events take place by his sovereign appointment.

They deny that it is ever stated in plain terms that God decreed Adam should perish by his rebellion. (See Calvin, On Predestination.)

8¶

At this point they fall back on the distinction between will and permission, the aim being to prove that the wicked perish only by God's permission and not by his will. But why do we say that he permits, except that he wills it? And there is really no likelihood in the idea itself, that man brought death on himself merely by God's permission and not by his ordaining will, as if God had not determined what he wanted the condition of the chief of his creatures to be. So I will not hesitate to confess plainly, with Augustine, that the will of God is necessity, and that everything is necessary which he has willed, just as those things will certainly happen which he has foreseen (Augustine, On Genesis, Book 6, ch. 15). Now suppose that, in order to excuse themselves and other ungodly people, the Pelagians, the Manichees, the Anabaptists, or the Epicureans (for it is with these four sects that we have to argue this matter) should point to the necessity that constrains them as a result of divine predestination. They are doing nothing that bears on the case. For if predestination is nothing but a dispensing of divine justice, secret indeed, but blameless, since it is certain that those predestined to that condition were not undeserving of it, then it is equally certain that the destruction which follows from predestination is also entirely just. Moreover, although their ruin depends on the predestination of God, the cause and the substance of it lie within themselves. The first man fell because the Lord judged it fitting that he should. Why he judged it fitting, we do not know. Yet it is certain that it was just, because he saw that his own glory would thereby be displayed. When you hear the glory of God mentioned, understand that his justice is included in it, for whatever deserves praise must be just. Man therefore falls, with divine providence so ordaining it, but he falls by his own fault. A little earlier the Lord had declared that everything he had made was very good (Genesis 1:31). Where, then, did man's depravity come from, the depravity that made him revolt from God? So that we would not suppose it came from his creation, God expressly approved what came forth from himself. Therefore man's own wickedness corrupted the pure nature he had received from God, and his ruin dragged down all his descendants with it. So let us look, in the corruption of human nature, for the plain cause of condemnation, a cause that lies closer to home for us, rather than searching for a cause hidden and almost beyond understanding in the predestination of God. And let us not refuse to submit our judgment to the boundless wisdom of God, so far as to confess that it is unable to grasp many of his secrets. To be ignorant of things we are not able to know, or are not permitted to know, is real learning, while the craving to know them is a kind of madness.

9¶

Someone may perhaps say that I have not yet said enough to refute this blasphemous excuse. I admit that it is impossible to stop ungodliness from grumbling and objecting. But I think I have said enough to remove not only the ground but even the pretext for laying blame on God. The reprobate would excuse their sins by claiming that they cannot escape the necessity of sinning, especially because a necessity of this kind is laid on them by the ordaining will of God. We deny that they can be excused in this way, since the ordaining will of God, by which they complain they are doomed to destruction, is fully consistent with justice, a justice unknown to us, indeed, but absolutely certain. So we conclude that every evil they bear is inflicted by the most just judgment of God. Next, we have shown that they act backward when, in searching for the origin of their condemnation, they turn their gaze to the hidden depths of the divine counsel and shut their eyes to the corruption of their nature, which is the true source. They cannot blame this corruption on God, because he bears witness to the goodness of his creation. For although, by the eternal providence of God, man was formed for the calamity under which he now lies, he took the substance of that calamity from himself, not from God, since the only cause of his destruction was his own falling away from the purity of his creation into a state of vice and impurity.

10¶

There is a third supposed absurdity by which the opponents of predestination try to discredit it. Because we attribute it entirely to the counsel of the divine will that those whom God adopts as heirs of his kingdom are spared from the universal destruction, they conclude that he shows favoritism toward particular persons. But Scripture consistently denies this. And so, they say, either Scripture contradicts itself, or merit must be taken into account in election. First, the sense in which Scripture declares that God shows no favoritism toward persons is different from what they suppose. The term 'person' here does not mean a man, but rather those traits which, when they stand out in a man, either win favor, grace, and standing, or, on the other hand, breed hatred, contempt, and disgrace. Among these traits are, on the one side, riches, wealth, power, rank, office, nationality, beauty, and the like, and, on the other side, poverty, need, low birth, shabbiness, contempt, and so on. So Peter and Paul say that the Lord shows no favoritism toward persons, because he makes no distinction between Jew and Greek; he does not make the mere fact of nationality a reason for rejecting the one or embracing the other (Acts 10:34; Romans 2:10; Galatians 3:28). James uses the same words when he wants to declare that God has no regard for riches in his judgment (James 2:5). And Paul says elsewhere that in judging, God has no regard for slavery or freedom (Ephesians 6:9; Colossians 3:25). There is nothing inconsistent with this when we say that God, according to the good pleasure of his will and without any regard to merit, elects those whom he chooses as sons, while he rejects and reprobates others. For fuller satisfaction, the matter may be explained this way (see Augustine, Letter 115, and to Boniface, Book 2, ch. 7). The question is asked: How does it happen that, of two men between whom there is no difference of merit, God in his election adopts one and passes by the other? I, in turn, ask: Is there anything in the man who is adopted to incline God toward him? If it must be admitted that there is nothing, then it follows that God does not look to the man at all, but is moved entirely by his own goodness to do him good. Therefore, when God elects one and rejects another, it is owing not to any regard for the individual, but entirely to his own mercy, which is free to display and exert itself whenever and wherever he pleases. For we have seen elsewhere that, in order to humble the pride of the flesh, 'not many wise men after the flesh, not many mighty, not many noble, are called' (1 Corinthians 1:26). So far is God, in showing his favor, from having any regard for persons.

11¶

Therefore it is false, and utterly wicked, to charge God with handing out justice unequally because, in this predestination, he does not follow the same course toward everyone. If, they say, he finds all guilty, let him punish all alike; and if he finds them innocent, let him relieve all of them from the severity of judgment. But they argue with God as though he were either forbidden to show mercy, or were obliged, if he does show mercy, to give up judgment entirely. What is it they are demanding? That if all are guilty, all should receive the same punishment. We admit that the guilt is shared by all, but we say that God in his mercy comes to the aid of some. Let him aid all, they say. We answer that it is right for him to show, by punishing, that he is a just judge. When they cannot stand this, what are they really trying to do but to strip God of the power to show mercy, or at least to allow it to him only on the condition that he wholly give up judgment? Here the words of Augustine apply most fittingly: 'Since in the first man the whole human race fell under condemnation, those vessels which are made from it unto honor are not vessels of self-righteousness, but of divine mercy. And when other vessels are made unto dishonor, this must be charged not to injustice, but to judgment' (Augustine, Letter 106, On Predestination and Grace; On the Gift of Perseverance, ch. 12). Since God inflicts the punishment that is due on those whom he reprobates, and grants unearned favor to those whom he calls, he is free from every accusation, just as it belongs to a creditor to forgive the debt of one and to demand it from another. The Lord, therefore, may show favor to whomever he wills, because he is merciful; and he may withhold it from others, because he is a just judge. In giving to some what they do not deserve, he displays his free favor; in not giving to all, he declares what all deserve. For when Paul says, 'God hath concluded them all in unbelief, that he might have mercy upon all,' it should also be added that he is debtor to no one; for 'who hath first given to him, and it shall be recompensed unto him again?' (Romans 11:32, 33).

12¶

Another argument they use to overthrow predestination is that, if it stands, then all care and effort to do good must cease. For what man, they ask, can hear that life and death are fixed by an eternal and unchangeable decree of God without immediately concluding that it makes no difference how he acts, since none of his works can either hinder or advance the predestination of God? And so everyone will rush ahead, and like desperate men plunge headlong wherever their cravings lead. It is true that this is not entirely made up, for there are crowds of people with a swinish nature who defile the doctrine of predestination with their profane blasphemies and use it as a cover to escape every warning and rebuke. 'God knows what he has decided to do with us. If he has decreed our salvation, he will bring us to it in his own time; and if he has doomed us to death, it is useless for us to fight against it.' But Scripture, while it commands us to think of this high mystery with far greater reverence and devotion, gives very different instruction to the godly, and rightly condemns the cursed license of the ungodly. For it does not remind us of predestination in order to increase our boldness, tempting us to pry with wicked presumption into the unsearchable counsels of God, but rather to humble and lower us, so that we may tremble at his judgment and learn to look up to his mercy. This is the goal at which believers will aim. The grunting of these filthy swine is fully silenced by Paul. They say they feel secure in their vices because, if they are among the elect, their vices will be no obstacle to finally reaching life. But Paul reminds us that the goal for which we are elected is 'that we should be holy and without blame before him' (Ephesians 1:4). If the goal of election is holiness of life, then it ought to rouse and spur us to strive after it with all our strength, not serve as an excuse for laziness. How vast is the difference between these two things: between ceasing to do good because election is enough for salvation, and recognizing that the very goal of election is that we should give ourselves to the pursuit of good works. Have done, then, with blasphemies that wickedly turn the whole order of election upside down. And when they push their blasphemies even further and say that anyone reprobated by God will waste his effort if he tries to win God's approval by an innocent and upright life, they are caught in the most shameless falsehood. For where could any such effort come from except from election? Just as all who are among the reprobate are vessels formed for dishonor, so they never stop, by their endless crimes, provoking the anger of God against themselves, and giving plain signs of the judgment God has already passed upon them. So far is it from being true that they vainly struggle against it.

13¶

Another shameless and malicious slander against this doctrine is that it destroys all exhortation to a godly life. The great hostility Augustine once faced on this point he wiped away in his treatise On Rebuke and Grace, addressed to Valentinus, which anyone godly and teachable can read and be satisfied by. Here, however, I may touch on a few points that will, I hope, be enough for those who are honest and not quarrelsome. We have already seen how plainly and clearly Paul preaches the doctrine of free election. Is he therefore cold in admonishing and exhorting? Let those self-styled zealots compare his intensity with their own, and they will find that they are ice while he is all fire. And surely every doubt on this subject should be removed by the principles he lays down: that God has not called us to uncleanness; that everyone should keep his vessel in honor; that we are God's workmanship, 'created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them' (1 Thessalonians 4:4, 7; Ephesians 2:10). In a word, anyone with even a fair acquaintance with the writings of Paul will see, without a long demonstration, how well he reconciles the two things these men pretend are contradictory. Christ commands us to believe in him, and yet there is nothing false or contrary to this command in what he says afterward: 'No man can come unto me, except it were given unto him of my Father' (John 6:65). So let preaching have its free course, that it may lead men to faith and dispose them to persevere with unbroken progress. And at the same time, let there be no obstacle to the knowledge of predestination, so that those who obey may not congratulate themselves on anything of their own, but may glory only in the Lord. It is not without reason that our Savior says, 'Who hath ears to hear, let him hear' (Matthew 13:9). Therefore, while we exhort and preach, those who have ears willingly obey; while in those again who have no ears, what is written is fulfilled: 'Hear ye indeed, but understand not' (Isaiah 6:9). 'But why,' says Augustine, 'do some have ears and others not? Who has known the mind of the Lord? Are we, then, to deny what is plain because we cannot comprehend what is hidden?' This is a faithful quotation from Augustine. But because his words will perhaps carry more weight than mine, let us bring forward the following passage from his treatise On the Gift of Perseverance, ch. 15.

'Suppose that some, on hearing this, turn to idleness and laziness, and leaving off all effort, rush headlong into lust. Are we therefore to suppose that what has been said about the foreknowledge of God is untrue? If God foreknew that they would be good, will they not be good, however great their present wickedness? And if God foreknew that they would be wicked, will they not be wicked, however great the goodness now seen in them? For reasons of this kind, must the truth that has been stated about divine foreknowledge be denied, or left unmentioned? And especially when, if it is not stated, other errors will arise?' In the sixteenth chapter he says: 'The reason for not mentioning the truth is one thing; the necessity of telling the truth is another. It would take too long to inquire into all the reasons for silence. One of them, however, is this: that those who do not understand may not become worse, while we are trying to make those who do understand better informed. Now such people, when we say anything of this kind, do not in fact become better informed, but neither do they become worse. But when the truth is of such a kind that the one who cannot grasp it becomes worse because we tell it, and the one who can grasp it becomes worse because we do not tell it, what do you think we ought to do? Are we not to tell the truth, so that the one who can grasp it may grasp it, rather than to leave it untold and thereby keep both from grasping it, and even make the more intelligent of the two become worse, when, if he heard and grasped it, others might learn through him? And we are unwilling to say what, on the witness of Scripture, it is lawful to say. For we are afraid that, when we speak, the one who cannot grasp it may be offended; but we have no fear that, while we are silent, the one who can grasp the truth may be left entangled in falsehood.' In the twentieth chapter, returning again to the same point, he confirms it still more clearly: 'Therefore, if the apostles and the teachers of the Church who came after them did both, if they spoke devoutly of the eternal election of God and at the same time kept believers under the discipline of a godly life, how can these men of our own day, when cornered by the unconquerable force of truth, think they are right to say that what is taught about predestination, though true, must not be preached to the people? On the contrary, it certainly ought to be preached, so that whoever has ears to hear may hear. And who has ears, if he has not received them from the one who has promised to give them? Let the one who does not receive, by all means, reject it. Let the one who receives, take and drink, drink and live. For just as godliness is to be preached, so that God may be rightly worshiped, so predestination also is to be preached, so that the one who has ears to hear may, in the matter of divine grace, glory not in himself, but in God.'

14¶

And yet, because that holy man had a singular desire to build others up, he so governed his way of teaching as to take care, as far as he could, not to give offense. For he reminds us that things which are true should also be spoken fittingly. If anyone were to address the people like this, 'If you do not believe, it is because God has already doomed you to destruction,' he would not only encourage laziness but also give cover to wickedness. And if anyone were to put it in the future tense and say that those who hear will not believe because they are reprobates, that would be a curse rather than teaching. So Augustine rightly orders such men, as foolish teachers or ministers and prophets of ill omen, to withdraw from the Church. Indeed, he truly argues elsewhere that 'a man profits from correction only when he who causes whomever he pleases to profit even without correction takes pity and helps. But why is it this way with some, and otherwise with others? Far be it from us to say that this belongs to the clay to decide, and not to the potter.' He afterward says, 'When men, through correction, either come or return to the way of righteousness, who is it that works salvation in their hearts but the one who gives the increase, whoever it may be that plants and waters? When he is pleased to save, there is no free will in man to resist. Therefore it cannot be doubted that the will of God, who has done whatever he pleased in heaven and on earth, and who has even done the things that are yet to be, cannot be resisted by the human will, nor kept from doing what he pleases, since he works even with the very wills of men.' Again he says, 'When he means to draw men to himself, does he bind them with bodily chains? He acts inwardly, holds them inwardly, inwardly moves their hearts, and draws them by the will, which he himself has worked in them.' What he adds right after this must not be left out: 'Because we do not know who belongs to the number of the predestined and who does not, our desire ought to be that all may be saved. And so, with everyone we meet, we will long for them to share peace with us. But our peace will rest upon the sons of peace. Therefore, for our part, let correction be applied as a harsh but healing medicine to all, so that they may neither perish themselves nor destroy others. It will belong to God to make it effective in those whom he has foreknown and predestined.'

A Word for 2026 · U.S.M.C. Ministries

Calvin here takes up the hardest doctrine and refuses to soften it: there can be no election without reprobation, for those whom God passes by he passes by according to his righteous and inscrutable will, while the cause of their condemnation lies wholly within themselves. The flesh boils against this, demanding that the clay sit in judgment on the potter — but Calvin answers that the will of God is the supreme rule of righteousness, and that men who perish do so by their own fault, drawn spontaneously by their corrupt nature. For the household in 2026, two snares loom: the carnal who twist election into license to sin, and the timid who would bury the doctrine to spare weak minds. Calvin, with Augustine, charts the narrow road — preach it, but preach it for humility and adoration, never for presumption. The end of election is holiness of life. So labor in good works not to secure what is already sure, but because that very labor is the fruit and proof of being his.
