Necessity of Contemplating the Judgment-Seat of God, in Order to Be Seriously Convinced of the Doctrine of Gratuitous Justification

The divisions of this chapter are,—I. A consideration of the righteousness of God overturns the righteousness of works, as is plain from passages of Scripture, and the confession and example of the saints, sec. 1-3. II. The same effect produced by a serious examination of the conscience, and a constant citation to the divine tribunal, sec. 4 and 5. III. Hence arises, in the hearts of the godly, not hypocrisy, or a vain opinion of merit, but true humility. This illustrated by the authority of Scripture and the example of the Publican, sec. 6, 7. IV. Conclusion—arrogance and security must be discarded, every man throwing an impediment in the way of the divine goodness in proportion as he trusts to himself. 1. Source of error on the subject of Justification. Sophists speak as if the question were to be discussed before some human tribunal. It relates to the majesty and justice of God. Hence nothing accepted without absolute perfection. Passages confirming this doctrine. If we descend to the righteousness of the Law, the curse immediately appears. 2. Source of hypocritical confidence. Illustrated by a simile. Exhortation. Testimony of Job, David, and Paul. 3. Confession of Augustine and Bernard. 4. Another engine overthrowing the righteousness of works—viz. A serious examination of the conscience, and a comparison between the perfection of God and the imperfection of man. 5. How it is that we so indulge this imaginary opinion of our own works. The proper remedy to be found in a consideration of the majesty of God and our own misery. A description of this misery. 6. Christian humility consists in laying aside the imaginary idea of our own righteousness, and trusting entirely to the mercy of God, apprehended by faith in Christ. This humility described. Proved by passages of Scripture. 7. The parable of the Publican explained. 8. Arrogance, security, and self-confidence, must be renounced. General rule, or summary of the above doctrine.

1¶

Although the perfect truth of the doctrine above is proved by clear passages of Scripture, we cannot clearly see how necessary it is until we bring distinctly into view the foundations on which the whole discussion ought to rest. First, then, let us remember that the righteousness we are considering belongs not to a human tribunal but to a heavenly one; and so let us beware of using our own little standard to measure the perfection that is to satisfy the justice of God. It is strange with what rashness and presumption this is commonly defined. Indeed, we see that none talk more confidently, or, so to speak, more blusteringly, of the righteousness of works than those whose diseases are most obvious and whose blemishes are most apparent. They do this because they do not reflect on the righteousness of Christ, which, if they had the slightest perception of it, they would never treat with such insult. It is certainly undervalued if it is not recognized to be so perfect that nothing can be accepted that is not in every respect whole and complete, and tainted by no impurity; such as indeed never has been, and never will be, found in man. It is easy for anyone, within the walls of the schools, to talk of the sufficiency of works for justification; but when we come into the presence of God, there must be an end to such talk. There the matter is dealt with in earnest, and is no longer a theatrical battle of words. This is where we must turn our minds if we would inquire to any purpose about true righteousness; the question must be: How shall we answer the heavenly Judge when he calls us to account? Let us contemplate that Judge, not as our own unaided intellect conceives of him, but as he is portrayed to us in Scripture (see especially the Book of Job): with a brightness that darkens the stars, a strength that melts the mountains, an anger that shakes the earth, a wisdom that takes the wise in their own craftiness, a purity before which all things become impure, a righteousness to which not even the angels are equal (so far is it from making the guilty innocent), a vengeance that, once kindled, burns to the lowest hell (Exodus 34:7; Nahum 1:3; Deuteronomy 32:22). Let Him, I say, sit in judgment on the actions of men, and who will feel secure in presenting himself before his throne? "Who among us," says the prophet, "shall dwell with the devouring fire? who among us shall dwell with everlasting burnings? He that walketh righteously, and speaketh uprightly," etc. (Isaiah 33:14, 15). Let whoever will come forward. But the answer shows that no man can. For, on the other side, we hear the dreadful voice: "If thou, Lord, shouldst mark our iniquities, O Lord, who shall stand?" (Psalm 130:3). All must immediately perish, as Job declares: "Shall mortal man be more just than God? shall a man be more pure than his Maker? Behold, he put no trust in his servants; and his angels he charged with folly: How much less in them that dwell in houses of clay, whose foundation is in the dust, which are crushed before the moth? They are destroyed from morning to evening" (Job 4:17-20). Again: "Behold, he putteth no trust in his saints; yea, the heavens are not clean in his sight. How much more abominable and filthy is man, which drinketh iniquity like water?" (Job 15:15, 16). I confess, indeed, that in the Book of Job reference is made to a righteousness of a higher kind than the keeping of the Law. It is important to attend to this distinction; for even if a man could satisfy the Law, he could not stand the scrutiny of that righteousness which surpasses all our thoughts. Hence, although Job was not conscious of offending, he is still struck dumb with astonishment, because he sees that God could not be appeased even by the holiness of angels, if their works were weighed in that supreme balance. But to say no more of this righteousness, which is incomprehensible, I only say that if our life is measured by the standard of the written law, we are lethargic indeed if we are not filled with dread at the many curses which God has used to rouse us, and among others this general one: "Cursed be he that confirmeth not all the words of this law to do them" (Deuteronomy 27:26). In short, the whole discussion of this subject will be tasteless and frivolous unless we present ourselves before the heavenly Judge and, anxious for our acquittal, willingly humble ourselves, confessing our nothingness.

2¶

This, then, is how we must lift our eyes, so that we may learn to tremble instead of vainly exulting. It is easy, indeed, when the comparison is made among men, for everyone to pride himself on some quality that others ought not to despise; but when we rise to God, that confidence instantly falls and dies away. The case of the soul with regard to God is very much like that of the body with regard to the visible sky. The bodily eye, while it surveys nearby objects, is pleased with its own sharpness; but when it is turned to the sun, dazzled and overwhelmed by its brilliance, it becomes no less convinced of its weakness than it was before of its power in viewing lesser objects. Therefore, so that we do not deceive ourselves with vain confidence, let us remember that even if we consider ourselves equal or superior to other men, this is nothing to God, by whose judgment the decision must be given. But if our presumption cannot be tamed by these considerations, he will answer us as he did the Pharisees: "Ye are they which justify yourselves before men; but God knoweth your hearts: for that which is highly esteemed among men is abomination in the sight of God" (Luke 16:15). Go now and make a proud boast of your righteousness among men, while God in heaven abhors it. But what are the feelings of the servants of God, of those who are truly taught by his Spirit? "Enter not into judgment with thy servant; for in thy sight shall no man living be justified" (Psalm 143:2). Another, though in a somewhat different sense, says, "How should man be just with God? If he will contend with him he cannot answer him one of a thousand" (Job 9:2, 3). Here we are plainly told what the righteousness of God is, namely, a righteousness that no human works can satisfy, that charges us with a thousand sins while not one sin can be excused. Paul, that chosen vessel of God, had formed a right idea of this righteousness when he declared, "I know nothing by myself, yet am I not hereby justified" (1 Corinthians 4:4).

3¶

Such examples are found not in the sacred volume only; all pious writers show that their view was the same. Thus Augustine says, "Of all pious men groaning under this burden of corruptible flesh and the infirmities of this life, the only hope is that we have one Mediator, Jesus Christ the righteous, and that he intercedes for our sins" (Augustine, Ad Bonifacium, book 3, chapter 5). What do we hear? If this is their only hope, where is their confidence in works? When he says only, he leaves room for no other. Bernard says, "And, indeed, where do the weak have firm security and safe rest, but in the wounds of the Savior? Hold to it, then, all the more securely, the more powerful he is to save. The world frowns, the body presses, the devil lays snares: I do not fall, because I am founded on a firm rock. I have sinned a grievous sin: my conscience is troubled, but it shall not be overwhelmed, for I will remember the wounds of the Lord." He afterward concludes, "My merit, therefore, is the compassion of the Lord; plainly I am not without merit so long as he is not without pity. And if the mercies of the Lord are many, my merits are equally many. Shall I sing of my own righteousness? O Lord, I will make mention of thy righteousness alone. That righteousness is mine also, being made mine by God" (Bernard, Sermon 61 on the Song of Songs). Again, in another passage: "Man's whole merit is to place his whole hope in him who saves the whole man" (on the Psalm Qui Habitat, Sermon 15). In the same way, keeping peace for himself, he leaves the glory to God: "Let thy glory remain undiminished: it is well with me if I have peace. I utterly renounce boasting, lest, if I should usurp what is not mine, I lose also what is offered" (Sermon 13 on the Song of Songs). He says still more plainly in another place: "Why is the Church anxious about merits? God purposely supplies her with a firmer and more secure ground of boasting. There is no reason to ask by what merits we may hope for blessings, especially when you hear in the prophet, 'Thus saith the Lord God, I do not this for your sakes, O house of Israel, but for mine holy name's sake' (Ezekiel 36:22, 32). It is enough for merit to know that merits are not enough; but as it is enough for merit not to presume on merit, so to be without merits is enough for condemnation" (Bernard, Sermon 68). The loose use of the term merits for good works must be pardoned as a matter of custom. Bernard's purpose was to alarm hypocrites, who turned the grace of God into licentiousness, as he explains shortly after: "Happy the church which lacks neither merit without presumption, nor presumption without merit. It has ground to presume, but not merit. It has merit, merit to deserve, not to presume. Is not the absence of presumption itself a merit? He, therefore, to whom the many mercies of the Lord furnish ample grounds for boasting, presumes the more securely because he does not presume" (Bernard, Sermon 68).

4¶

So indeed it is. Awakened consciences, when they have to do with God, feel this to be the only refuge in which they can breathe safely. For if the stars that shine most brightly by night lose their brightness when the sun appears, what do we think will become of the highest purity of man when set against the purity of God? For the scrutiny will be most strict, reaching to the most hidden thoughts of the heart. As Paul says, it "will bring to light the hidden things of darkness and will make manifest the counsels of the heart" (1 Corinthians 4:5); it will compel the reluctant and dissembling conscience to bring everything forward, even things that have now slipped from our memory. The devil, aware of all the iniquities he has led us to commit, will appear as accuser; the outward show of good works, the only thing now considered, will then be of no use; the only thing demanded will be the true intent of the will. Hence hypocrisy will fall confounded, however much it may now swell with pride and presumption: not only the hypocrisy by which a man, though conscious of guilt before God, strives to make an ostentatious display before men, but also that by which each one deceives himself before God (so prone are we to soothe and flatter ourselves). Those who do not turn their thoughts to this scene may be able for the moment to build up a righteousness for themselves calmly and complacently; but the judgment of God will immediately overthrow it, just as great wealth heaped up in a dream vanishes the moment we wake. Those who, as in the presence of God, inquire seriously into the true standard of righteousness will certainly find that all the works of men, if judged by their own worth, are nothing but vileness and pollution; that what is commonly considered justice is mere iniquity with God; what is considered integrity is pollution; what is considered glory is disgrace.

5¶

Let us not refuse to come down from this contemplation of the divine perfection to look into ourselves without flattery or blind self-love. It is no wonder that we are so deceived in this matter, since none of us can escape that pestilent self-indulgence which, as Scripture proclaims, is naturally inborn in all: "Every way of a man is right in his own eyes," says Solomon (Proverbs 21:2). And again, "All the ways of a man are clean in his own eyes" (Proverbs 16:2). What then? Does this delusion excuse him? No indeed, for Solomon immediately adds, "The Lord weigheth the spirits"; that is, while man flatters himself by wearing an outward mask of righteousness, the Lord weighs the hidden impurity of the heart in his balance. Since, therefore, nothing is gained by such flattery, let us not willingly deceive ourselves to our own destruction. To examine ourselves properly, our conscience must be called before the judgment-seat of God. His light is needed to lay bare the secret recesses of wickedness, which otherwise lie too deeply hidden. Only then shall we clearly perceive what our works are worth: that man, so far from being just before God, is but rottenness and a worm, abominable and vain, drinking in "iniquity like water." For "who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? not one" (Job 14:5). Then we shall experience the truth of what Job said of himself: "If I justify myself, mine own mouth shall condemn me: if I say I am perfect, it shall prove me perverse" (Job 9:20). Nor does the complaint which the prophet made concerning Israel apply to one age only. It is true of every age that "all we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way" (Isaiah 53:6). Indeed, he includes there all to whom the gift of redemption was to come. And the strictness of the examination ought to be kept up until it has thoroughly alarmed us, and in that way prepared us to receive the grace of Christ. For he is deceived who thinks himself capable of enjoying it before he has laid aside all loftiness of mind. There is a well-known declaration: "God resisteth the proud, but giveth grace to the humble" (1 Peter 5:5).

6¶

But what means is there of humbling us, if we do not make way for the mercy of God by our utter need and destitution? For I do not call it humility so long as we think there is any good left in us. Those who have joined together these two things, to think humbly of ourselves before God and yet to hold our own righteousness in some esteem, have until now taught a pernicious hypocrisy. For if we confess to God contrary to what we feel, we wickedly lie to him; but we cannot feel as we ought without seeing that everything resembling a ground for boasting is completely crushed. Therefore, when you hear from the prophets, "thou wilt save the afflicted people; but wilt bring down high looks" (Psalm 18:27), consider, first, that there is no access to salvation unless all pride is laid aside and true humility embraced; second, that this humility is not a kind of moderation by which you yield to God some portion of your right (as men are called humble toward one another when they neither behave haughtily nor insult others, though they may still hold some sense of their own excellence), but that it is the unfeigned submission of a mind overwhelmed by a serious conviction of its need and misery. Such is the description given everywhere in the word of God. When in Zephaniah the Lord speaks thus, "I will take away out of the midst of thee them that rejoice in thy pride, and thou shalt no more be haughty because of my holy mountain. I will also leave in the midst of thee an afflicted and poor people, and they shall trust in the name of the Lord" (Zephaniah 3:11, 12), does he not plainly show who the humble are, namely, those who lie afflicted by a knowledge of their poverty? On the other hand, he describes the proud as rejoicing (exultantes), this being the way in which men usually express their delight in prosperity. To the humble, whom he means to save, he leaves nothing but hope in the Lord. So also in Isaiah: "To this man will I look, even to him that is poor and of a contrite spirit, and trembleth at my word" (Isaiah 66:2). And again: "Thus saith the high and lofty One that inhabiteth eternity, whose name is Holy; I dwell in the high and holy place, with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit, to revive the spirit of the humble, and to revive the heart of the contrite ones" (Isaiah 57:15). By the term contrition, which you hear so often, understand a wounded heart, which humbles a man to the earth and does not allow him to rise. With such contrition your heart must be wounded if you would, according to the declaration of God, be exalted with the humble. If this is not your case, you shall be humbled by the mighty hand of God to your shame and disgrace.

7¶

Our divine Master, not confining himself to words, has set before us in a parable, as in a picture, a representation of true humility. He brings forward a publican who, standing far off and not daring to lift up his eyes to heaven, beats his breast, laments aloud, and cries, "God be merciful to me a sinner" (Luke 18:13). Let us not suppose that he is showing the signs of a pretended modesty when he dares not come near or lift his eyes to heaven, but, beating his breast, confesses himself a sinner; let us understand that these are the evidences of what he feels within. With him our Lord contrasts the Pharisee, who thanks God: "I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican. I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess." In this public confession he admits that the righteousness he possesses is the gift of God; but because of his confidence that he is righteous, he departs from the presence of God unaccepted and abhorred. The publican, acknowledging his iniquity, is justified. From this we may see how highly our humility is valued by the Lord: our breast cannot receive his mercy until it is completely emptied of all opinion of its own worth. When such an opinion is held, the door of mercy is shut. So that there might be no doubt on this matter, the mission on which Christ was sent into the world by his Father was "to preach good tidings to the meek," "to bind up the broken-hearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives, and the opening of the prison to them that are bound; to proclaim the acceptable year of the Lord, and the day of vengeance of our God; to comfort all that mourn; to appoint unto them that mourn in Zion to give unto them beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness" (Isaiah 61:1-3). In fulfillment of that mission, the only persons he invites to share in his kindness are the "weary and heavy laden." In another passage he says, "I am not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance" (Matthew 11:28; 9:13).

8¶

Therefore, if we would make way for the call of Christ, we must put far from us all arrogance and confidence. The former is produced by a foolish persuasion of self-righteousness, when a man thinks he has something in himself that deservedly commends him to God; the latter may exist without any confidence in works. French, "Par arrogance j'enten l'orgueil qui s'engendre d'une fole persuasion de justice, quand l'homme pense avoir quelque chose, dont il merite d'estre agreable à Dieu; par presomption j'enten une nonchalance charnelle, qui peut estre sans aucune fiance des œuvres;" by arrogance I mean the pride that is born of a foolish persuasion of righteousness, when man thinks he has something for which he deserves to be pleasing to God. By presumption I understand a carnal indifference, which may exist without any confidence in works.

Calvin will not let us debate righteousness in the comfortable courtroom of human comparison; he hauls the soul before the heavenly Judge, whose purity makes even the angels unclean, that we might be stripped of every boast and driven, contrite and empty-handed, to the mercy of God in Christ. The publican, not the Pharisee, went home justified. This is medicine for a self-justifying age — and for our own hearts, so quick to measure ourselves against the neighbor and call it integrity. Fathers and mothers, do not raise children skilled in defending themselves; raise them able to smite the breast and say, God be merciful to me a sinner. Bring your conscience often to that tribunal, in private prayer and at the Lord's Table, that the dream of your own goodness might vanish like waking from sleep. True humility is not modesty about ourselves; it is the abandonment of self into the wounds of the Savior, where alone the troubled soul finds rest.
