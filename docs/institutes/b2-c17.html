Book 2 · Chapter 17 · USMC modern English

Christ Rightly and Properly Said to Have Merited Grace and Salvation for Us

The three leading divisions of this chapter are,—I. A proof from reason and from Scripture that the grace of God and the merit of Christ (the prince and author of our salvation) are perfectly compatible, sec. 1 and 2. II. Christ, by his obedience, even to the death of the cross (which was the price of our redemption), merited divine favour for us, sec. 3–5. III. The presumptuous rashness of the Schoolmen in treating this branch of doctrine.

1¶

A question must be considered here by way of supplement. Certain men who are too much given to subtlety, while they admit that we obtain salvation through Christ, will not hear of the term merit, by which they imagine the grace of God is obscured; and so they insist that Christ was only the instrument or minister, not the author or leader or prince of life, as Peter calls him (Acts 3:15). I admit that if Christ were set simply and by himself against the justice of God, there could be no room for merit, because no worth can be found in man that could make God a debtor; indeed, as Augustine says most truly, Augustine, De Praedestinatione Sanctorum, Lib. 1 c. 15; De Bono Perseverantia, last chapter. See above, chapter 14, section 7.

2¶

This distinction is found in many passages of Scripture: "God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him might not perish" (John 3:16). We see that the first place is given to the love of God as the chief cause or origin, and that faith in Christ follows as the second and nearer cause. Should anyone object that Christ is only the formal cause, The French adds, "C'est a dire, qui n'emporte en soy vrai effect" — that is to say, a cause which in itself produces no real effect.

1 John 2:2; Colossians 1:19, 20; 2 Corinthians 5:19; Ephesians 1:6; 2:16.

3¶

That Christ by his obedience truly purchased and merited grace for us with the Father is rightly inferred from several passages of Scripture. I take it as granted that if Christ made satisfaction for our sins, if he paid the penalty we owed, if he appeased God by his obedience, in short, if he suffered, the just for the unjust, then salvation was obtained for us by his righteousness; and this is exactly the same as meriting it. Now Paul's testimony is that we were reconciled, and received reconciliation, through his death (Romans 5:11). But there is no room for reconciliation except where offense French, "Offense, haine, divorce" — offense, hatred, divorce.

4¶

When we say that grace was obtained for us by the merit of Christ, we mean that we were cleansed by his blood, and that his death was an expiation for sin: "His blood cleanses us from all sin." "This is my blood, which is shed for the remission of sins" (1 John 1:7; Luke 22:20). If the effect of his shed blood is that our sins are not imputed to us, it follows that by that price the justice of God was satisfied. To the same effect are the Baptist's words, "Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world" (John 1:29). For he contrasts Christ with all the sacrifices of the Law, showing that in him alone was fulfilled what those figures foreshadowed. But we know the common expression in Moses: iniquity shall be expiated, sin shall be wiped away and forgiven. In short, we are admirably taught by the ancient figures what power and efficacy there is in Christ's death. And the Apostle, skillfully proceeding from this principle, explains the whole matter in the Epistle to the Hebrews, showing that without shedding of blood there is no remission (Hebrews 9:22). From this he infers that Christ appeared once for all to take away sin by the sacrifice of himself; and again, that he was offered to bear the sins of many (Hebrews 9:12). He had said earlier that it was not by the blood of goats or of calves, but by his own blood, that he had entered once into the holy of holies, having obtained eternal redemption for us. Now when he reasons in this way, "If the blood of bulls and of goats, and the ashes of an heifer sprinkling the unclean, sanctifieth to the purifying of the flesh: how much more shall the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself to God, purge your consciences from dead works to serve the living God?" (Hebrews 9:13, 14), it is plain that too little is made of the grace of Christ unless we grant to his sacrifice the power of expiating, appeasing, and satisfying; as he adds shortly afterward, "For this cause he is the mediator of the new testament, that by means of his death, for the redemption of the transgressions that were under the first testament, they which are called might receive the promise of eternal inheritance" (Hebrews 9:15). But it is especially necessary to attend to the analogy Paul draws when he says that Christ was made a curse for us (Galatians 3:13). It would have been superfluous, and therefore absurd, for Christ to be burdened with a curse, had it not been in order that by paying what others owed he might acquire righteousness for them. There is no ambiguity in Isaiah's testimony: "He was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was laid upon him; and with his stripes we are healed" (Isaiah 53:5). For had Christ not made satisfaction for our sins, he could not be said to have appeased God by taking upon himself the penalty which we had incurred. To this corresponds what follows in the same place: "for the transgression of my people was he stricken" (Isaiah 53:8). We may add the interpretation of Peter, who declares without any ambiguity that he "bare our sins in his own body on the tree" (1 Peter 2:24), meaning that the whole burden of condemnation, from which we have been relieved, was laid upon him.

5¶

The Apostles also plainly declare that he paid a price to ransom us from death: "Being justified freely by his grace, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus: whom God has set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood" (Romans 3:24, 25). Paul commends the grace of God in that he gave the price of redemption in the death of Christ; and he urges us to flee to his blood, so that, having obtained righteousness, we may stand with confidence before the judgment seat of God. To the same effect are the words of Peter: "Forasmuch as ye know that ye were not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold," "but with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot" (1 Peter 1:18, 19). The contrast would be pointless if he had not made satisfaction for sins by this price. For this reason Paul says, "Ye are bought with a price." Nor could it be said elsewhere that there is "one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus; who gave himself a ransom for all" (1 Timothy 2:5, 6), had the punishment which we deserved not been laid upon him. Accordingly, the same Apostle declares that "we have redemption through his blood, even the forgiveness of sins" (Colossians 1:14); as if he had said that we are justified, or acquitted, before God because that blood serves as satisfaction. With this another passage agrees — namely, that he blotted out "the handwriting of ordinances which was against us, which was contrary to us" (Colossians 2:14). These words denote the payment or compensation which acquits us of guilt. There is great weight also in these words of Paul: "If righteousness come by the law, then Christ is dead in vain" (Galatians 2:21). For we infer from this that we must seek from Christ what the Law would confer on anyone who fulfilled it; or, what is the same thing, that by the grace of Christ we obtain what God promised in the Law to our works: "If a man do, he shall live in them" (Leviticus 18:5). This is taught no less clearly in the sermon at Antioch, when Paul declares, "That through this man is preached unto you the forgiveness of sins; and by him all that believe are justified from all things, from which ye could not be justified by the law of Moses" (Acts 13:38, 39). For if the keeping of the Law is righteousness, who can deny that Christ, by taking this burden upon himself and reconciling us to God as if we were keepers of the Law, merited favor for us? Of the same kind is what he later says to the Galatians: "God sent forth his Son, made of a woman, made under the law, to redeem them that were under the law" (Galatians 4:4, 5). For to what end was that subjection, unless he obtained justification for us by undertaking to pay what we were unable to pay? Hence comes that imputation of righteousness without works of which Paul treats (Romans 4:5), the righteousness found in Christ alone being accepted as if it were ours. And certainly the only reason Christ is called our "meat" (John 6:55) is that we find in him the substance of life. And the source of this efficacy is simply that the Son of God was crucified as the price of our justification; as Paul says, Christ "has given himself for us an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling savour" (Ephesians 5:2); and elsewhere, he "was delivered for our offences, and was raised again for our justification" (Romans 4:25). Hence it is proved not only that salvation was given to us by Christ, but that on his account the Father is now propitious to us. For there can be no doubt that what God declares by Isaiah under a figure is completely fulfilled in him: "I will defend this city to save it for mine own sake, and for my servant David's sake" (Isaiah 37:35). Of this the Apostle is the best witness when he says, "Your sins are forgiven you for his name's sake" (1 John 2:12). For although the name of Christ is not expressed, John, in his usual way, designates him by the pronoun "He" (aujtov"). In the same sense our Lord also declares, "As the living Father has sent me, and I live by the Father: so he that eateth me, even he shall live by me" (John 6:57). To this corresponds the passage of Paul, "Unto you it is given in the behalf of Christ, not only to believe in him, but also to suffer for his sake" (Philippians 1:29).

6¶

To ask, as Lombard and the Schoolmen do (Sentences, Lib. 3 Dist. 18), whether he merited anything for himself is foolish curiosity. Equally rash is their decision when they answer yes. How could it be necessary for the only Son of God to come down in order to acquire some new quality for himself? The explanation which God gives of his own purpose removes all doubt. The Father is not said to have looked to the advantage of his Son in his services, but to have given him up to death and not spared him, because he loved the world (Romans 8). The prophetic expressions should be noted: "To us a Son is born"; "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion: shout, O daughter of Jerusalem: behold, thy King cometh unto thee" (Isaiah 9:6; Zechariah 9:9). Otherwise the commendation of love which Paul describes would be a cold one, when he says, "God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). Hence again we infer that Christ had no regard for himself; and he plainly affirms this when he says, "For their sakes I sanctify myself" (John 17:19). He who transfers the benefit of his holiness to others testifies that he acquires nothing for himself. And surely it is most worthy of notice that Christ, in devoting himself entirely to our salvation, in a sense forgot himself. It is absurd to twist the testimony of Paul to a different meaning: "Wherefore God has highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name" (Philippians 2:9). The sentence reads this way in the French: "Les Sorbonnistes pervertissent le passage de S. Paul, l'appliquans a ce propos c'est que pource que Jesus Christ s'est humilié, le Pere l'a exalté et lui donné un nom souverain:" — The Sorbonnists pervert the passage of St. Paul and apply it in this way: that because Christ humbled himself, the Father exalted him and gave him a sovereign name.

END OF THE SECOND BOOK.

Calvin guards a precious balance: Christ truly merited grace and salvation for us — paying our penalty, satisfying divine justice, being made a curse in our place — and yet this merit takes nothing from the free mercy of God, for it flows from that mercy and was ordained by it. Grace is the fountain; the cross is the channel; they are no rivals. In an age that flattens the atonement into mere example, or recoils from any talk of a price paid and wrath appeased, hold both truths with steady hands. God so loved that he gave; and the Son so obeyed, even unto the death of the cross, that nothing now stands against us. Teach this carefully in your home and church — that salvation cost the Son of God everything, and that he sought nothing for himself in the bearing of it, but for our sakes sanctified himself. To know what our redemption cost is to be drawn out of trembling into worship, and to spend our lives, as he spent his, not for ourselves.
