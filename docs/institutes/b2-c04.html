How God Works in the Hearts of Men

The leading points discussed in this chapter are, I. Whether in bad actions anything is to be attributed to God; if anything, how much. Also, what is to be attributed to the devil and to man, sec. 1–5. II. In indifferent matters, how much is to be attributed to God, and how much is left to man, sec. 6. III. Two objections refuted, sec. 7, 8.

1¶

That man is so enslaved by the yoke of sin that he cannot, by his own nature, aim at good either in desire or in actual pursuit has, I think, been sufficiently proved. Moreover, a distinction has been drawn between compulsion and necessity, making it clear that man, though he sins necessarily, nevertheless sins voluntarily. But since, from the fact that he has been brought into bondage to the devil, it would seem that he is moved more by the devil's will than by his own, it is necessary first to explain what the agency of each is, and then to resolve the question. The French adds, "dont on doute communement" — about which doubts are commonly raised.

2¶

The nature of the divine agency in such cases is very different. For the sake of illustration, let us turn to the calamities brought upon holy Job by the Chaldeans. Having killed his shepherds, they carry off his flocks. The wickedness of their deed is plain. The French adds, "Car quand nous voyons des voleurs, qui ont commis quelque meurtre ou larrecin, nous ne doutons point de leur imputer la faute, et de les condamner." — For when we see robbers who have committed some murder or theft, we do not hesitate to lay the blame on them and condemn them.

3¶

Ancient writers sometimes show a superstitious fear of making a simple confession of the truth in this matter, because they are afraid of giving ungodliness an occasion for speaking irreverently of the works of God. While I embrace such sobriety with all my heart, I cannot see the least danger in simply holding what Scripture teaches. Augustine was not always free from this superstition, as when he says that blinding and hardening refer not to the operation of God, but to his foreknowledge (On Predestination and Grace). But this subtlety is refuted by many passages of Scripture, which clearly show that the divine involvement amounts to something more than foreknowledge. And Augustine himself, in his book against Julian — the French adds, "se retractant de l'autre sentence," retracting his other view.

4¶

In keeping with the former methods it seems to be said: Ezekiel 7:26; Psalm 107:40; Job 12:20, 24; Isaiah 63:17; Exodus 4:21; 7:3; 10:1; 3:19.

Isaiah 5:26; 7:18; Ezekiel 12:13; 17:20; Jeremiah 2:23; Isaiah 10:15.

5¶

Moreover, that the ministry of Satan is used to stir up the reprobate whenever the Lord, in the course of his providence, has some purpose to accomplish in them will be sufficiently clear from a single passage. It is said repeatedly in the First Book of Samuel that an evil spirit from the Lord came upon Saul and troubled him (1 Samuel 16:14; 18:10; 19:9). It would be impious to apply this to the Holy Spirit. An impure spirit must therefore be called a spirit from the Lord because it is completely subservient to his purpose, being more an instrument in the action than a proper agent. We should also add what Paul says: "God shall send them strong delusion, that they should believe a lie: that they all might be damned who believed not the truth" (2 Thessalonians 2:11, 12). But in the same event there is always a wide difference between what the Lord does and what Satan and the ungodly intend to do. The wicked instruments that he has under his hand, and can turn as he pleases, he makes serve his own justice. They, being wicked, carry out the iniquity conceived in their wicked minds. Everything necessary to vindicate the majesty of God from slander, and to cut off every evasion on the part of the ungodly, has already been set out in the chapters on providence (Book 1, chapters 16–18). Here I meant only to show, in a few words, how Satan reigns in the reprobate, and how God works in both.

6¶

In those actions which in themselves are neither good nor bad, and which concern the bodily rather than the spiritual life, the liberty that man possesses has not yet been explained, although we touched on it above (chapter 2, sections 13–17). Some have granted man a free choice in such actions, more, I suppose, because they were unwilling to argue over a matter of no great importance than because they wished to assert positively what they were prepared to grant. While I admit that those who hold that man has no ability in himself to do righteousness hold what is most necessary to be known for salvation, I think it ought not to be overlooked that we owe it to the special grace of God whenever, on the one hand, we choose what is to our advantage and our will inclines in that direction, and whenever, on the other, we shun with heart and soul what would otherwise harm us. And the involvement of divine providence extends not only to making events turn out as was foreseen to be fitting, but to giving the wills of men the same direction. If we look at the management of human affairs with the eye of sense, we will have no doubt that, to this extent, they are placed at man's disposal; but if we listen to the many passages of Scripture which proclaim that even in these matters the minds of men are ruled by God, they will compel us to place human choice under his special influence. Who gave the Israelites such favor in the eyes of the Egyptians that they lent them all their most valuable goods (Exodus 11:3)? They would never have been so inclined of their own accord. Their inclinations, therefore, were overruled by God more than they were governed by themselves. And surely, had Jacob not been persuaded that God inspires men with various affections as seems good to him, he would not have said of his son Joseph (whom he thought to be some heathen Egyptian), "God Almighty give you mercy before the man" (Genesis 43:14). In the same way, the whole Church confesses that when the Lord was pleased to have compassion on his people, he made them also to be pitied by all who carried them captive (Psalm 106:46). In the same way, when Saul's anger was kindled so that he prepared himself for battle, the cause is stated to have been that a spirit from God fell upon him (1 Samuel 11:6). Who turned Absalom away from adopting the counsel of Ahithophel, which used to be regarded as an oracle (2 Samuel 17:14)? Who inclined Rehoboam to adopt the counsel of the young men (1 Kings 12:10)? Who caused the approach of the Israelites to strike terror into nations formerly renowned for valor? Even the harlot Rahab recognized the hand of the Lord. Who, on the other hand, filled the hearts of the Israelites with fear and dread (Leviticus 26:36), but He who threatened in the Law that he would give them a "trembling heart" (Deuteronomy 28:65)?

7¶

It may be objected that these are special examples which cannot be regarded as a general rule. They are sufficient, at any rate, to prove the point for which I contend — namely, that whenever God is pleased to make way for his providence, he turns and bends the wills of men even in external matters, so that whatever the freedom of their choice may be, it is still subject to the disposal of God. Daily experience teaches that your mind depends more on the working of God than on the freedom of your own choice. Your judgment often fails, and in matters of no great difficulty your courage flags; at other times, in matters of the greatest obscurity, the way to explain them at once suggests itself, while in matters of importance and danger your mind rises above every difficulty. The French adds, "D'où procede cela sinon que Dieu besongne tant d'une part que d'autre?" — Where does this come from, if not from God's working in this way in either case?

8¶

Let the reader remember here that the power of the human will is not to be judged by the outcome, as some unskilled people absurdly tend to do. They think it an elegant and clever proof of the bondage of the human will that even the greatest monarchs are sometimes thwarted in their wishes. But the ability of which we speak must be considered as being within the man, not measured by outward success. In discussing the subject of free will, the question is not whether external obstacles will allow a man to carry out what he has inwardly resolved, but whether, in any matter whatever, he has a free power of judging and of willing. If men possess both of these, Attilius Regulus, shut up in a barrel studded with sharp nails, will have a will no less free than Augustus Caesar ruling with imperial power over a large portion of the globe. The French is simply, "Car si cela pouvoit etre en l'homme, il ne seroit par moins libre enfermé en un prison que dominant par toute la terre." If that could be in man, he would be no less free shut up in a prison than ruling all the earth.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

How God works in human hearts is among the hardest questions Calvin handles, and he refuses the easy escapes — that God merely permits, or merely foresees, what the wicked do. Scripture, he shows from Job and elsewhere, attributes the same event to God, to Satan, and to man, each acting according to his own nature: God justly, Satan and man wickedly. In matters indifferent, too, our will is not so free as we imagine, for God inclines hearts as he pleases. For the church in 2026 this guards us from a deistic God who only watches from a distance; he is at work in every heart, even where we cannot trace him. The pastoral fruit is deep humility — we cannot fully chart his hidden operation, so we adore rather than dissect. Steadfastness grows where we stop demanding to understand the mechanism and simply trust that the God who moves all hearts moves ours toward himself, and our enemies' wherever he wills.
