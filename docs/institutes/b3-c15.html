Book 3 · Chapter 15 · USMC modern English

The Boasted Merit of Works Subversive Both of the Glory of God, in Bestowing Righteousness, and of the Certainty of Salvation

The divisions of this chapter are,—I. To the doctrine of free justification is opposed the question, Whether or not works merit favor with God, sec. 1. This question answered, sec. 2 and 3. II. An exposition of certain passages of Scripture produced in support of the erroneous doctrine of merit, sec. 4 and 5. III. Sophisms of Semipelagian Schoolmen refuted, sec. 6 and 7. IV. Conclusion, proving the sufficiency of the orthodox doctrine, sec. 8. 1. After a brief recapitulation, the question, Whether or not good works merit favor with God, considered. 2. First answer, fixing the meaning of the term Merit. This term improperly applied to works, but used in a good sense, as by Augustine, Chrysostom, Bernard. 3. A second answer to the question. First by a negative, then by a concession. In the rewarding of works what to be attributed to God, and what to man. Why good works please God, and are advantageous to those who do them. The ingratitude of seeking righteousness by works. This shown by a double similitude. 4. First objection taken from Ecclesiasticus. Second objection from the Epistle to the Hebrews. Two answers to both objections. A weak distinction refuted. 5. A third and most complete answer, calling us back to Christ as the only foundation of salvation. How Christ is our righteousness. Whence it is manifest that we have all things in Christ and he nothing in us. 6. We must abhor the sophistry which destroys the merit of Christ, in order to establish that of man. This impiety refuted by clear passages of Scripture. 7. Errors, of the younger Sophists extracted from Lombard. Refuted by Augustine. Also by Scripture. 8. Conclusion, showing that the foundation which has been laid is sufficient for doctrine, exhortation, and comfort. Summery of the orthodox doctrine of Justification.

1¶

The principal point of this subject has now been explained: since justification, if it depended on works, could not possibly stand in the sight of God, it must depend solely on the mercy of God and communion with Christ, and therefore on faith alone. But let us carefully attend to the point on which the whole subject turns, lest we become entangled in the common delusion, not only of ordinary people, but of the learned. For the moment the question is raised whether justification is by faith or by works, they run off to those passages which seem to ascribe some merit to works in the sight of God, just as if justification by works were proved whenever it is proved that works have any value with God. Above we have clearly shown that justification by works consists only in a perfect and absolute fulfillment of the law, and that therefore no man is justified by works unless he has reached the summit of perfection and cannot be convicted of even the smallest transgression. But there is another and separate question: though works are by no means sufficient to justify, do they not merit favor with God?

2¶

First, I must say at the outset regarding the term merit that whoever first applied it to human works, viewed in relation to the divine tribunal, did very poorly by the purity of the faith. I gladly refrain from disputes about words, but I could wish that Christian writers had always observed this restraint: that when there was no need for it, they had never thought of using terms foreign to the Scriptures, terms which might produce much offense but very little fruit. I ask, what need was there to introduce the word merit, when the value of works might have been fully expressed by another term, and without offense? How much offense it contains the world shows to its great loss. It is certain that, being a high-sounding term, it can only obscure the grace of God and inspire men with pernicious pride. I admit that it was used by ancient church writers, and I wish they had not, by the misuse of one term, furnished posterity with material for heresy, although in some passages they themselves show that they had no wish to injure the truth. For Augustine says, "Let human merits, which perished by Adam, here be silent, and let the grace of God reign through Jesus Christ" (Augustine, On the Predestination of the Saints). Again, "The saints ascribe nothing to their merits; everything they will ascribe solely to your mercy, O God" (Augustine, on Psalm 139). Again, "And when a man sees that whatever good he has, he has not from himself but from his God, he sees that everything in him which is praised comes not from his own merits but from the divine mercy" (Augustine, on Psalm 88). You see how he denies man the power of acting rightly, and so lays merit low. Chrysostom says, "If any works of ours follow the free calling of God, they are repayment and debt; but the gifts of God are grace, and kindness, and great generosity." But to say nothing more of the name, let us attend to the thing. I quoted earlier a passage from Bernard: "As it is sufficient for merit not to presume on merit, so to be without merit is sufficient for condemnation" (Bernard, Sermons on the Song of Songs, 98). He immediately adds an explanation which softens the harshness of the expression, when he says, "Therefore be careful to have merits; when you have them, know that they were given; hope for fruit from the divine mercy, and you have escaped all the perils of poverty, ingratitude, and presumption. Happy the Church which neither lacks merit without presumption, nor presumption without merit." A little earlier he had shown abundantly that he used the words in a sound sense, saying, "Why is the Church anxious about merits? God has furnished her with a firmer and surer ground of boasting. God cannot deny himself; he will do what he has promised. So there is no reason to ask by what merits we may hope for blessings, especially when you hear, 'Thus says the Lord God; I do not this for your sakes, O house of Israel, but for mine holy name's sake' (Ezek. 36:22). It suffices for merit to know that merits do not suffice."

3¶

What all our works can merit, Scripture shows when it declares that they cannot bear the gaze of God because they are full of impurity; it next shows what the perfect keeping of the law (if it can be found anywhere) will merit when it commands, "So likewise ye, when ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do" (Luke 17:10); for we make no free offering to God, but only perform the service we owe, by which no favor is earned. And yet those good works which the Lord has bestowed on us he counts as ours also, and declares that they are not only acceptable to him but that he will reward them. It is our part in return to be stirred by this great promise and to keep up our courage, so that we do not grow weary in doing good, but are rightly grateful for the great kindness of God. There can be no doubt that everything in our works which deserves praise is owed to divine grace, and that there is not a particle of it which we can properly ascribe to ourselves. If we truly and seriously acknowledge this, not only confidence but every idea of merit vanishes. I say that we do not, like the Sophists, divide the praise of works between God and man, but keep it whole and undiminished for the Lord. All that we assign to man is that by his impurity he pollutes and defiles the very works which were good. The most perfect thing that comes from man is always polluted by some stain. If the Lord, therefore, were to bring the best of human works into judgment, he would indeed see his own righteousness in them, but he would also see man's dishonor and disgrace. Thus good works please God and are not without fruit for those who do them, since by way of reward they obtain fuller blessings from God, not because they deserve it, but because the divine kindness is pleased of itself to set this value on them. Such, however, is our perversity that, not content with this generosity on God's part, which bestows rewards on works that in no way deserve them, we maintain with profane ambition that what is due entirely to the divine bounty is paid to the merit of works. Here I appeal to every man's common sense. If one who holds the use and fruits of a field by another's generosity sets up a claim to own it, does he not by his ingratitude deserve to lose the possession formerly granted? In the same way, if a slave who has been set free conceals his humble condition as a freedman and gives out that he was born free, does he not deserve to be reduced to his original slavery? A benefit can be rightly enjoyed only when we neither claim for ourselves more than has been given nor rob its author of his due praise; indeed, only when we so conduct ourselves as to make it plain that the benefit conferred still, in a sense, remains with the one who conferred it. But if this is the restraint to be observed toward men, let everyone reflect and consider for himself what is due to God.

4¶

I know that the Sophists misuse some passages in order to prove that the Scriptures use the term merit with reference to God. They quote a passage from Ecclesiasticus: "Mercy will give place to every man according to the merit of his works" (Ecclesiasticus 16:14); and from the Epistle to the Hebrews: "To do good and to communicate forget not; for with such sacrifices God is well pleased" (Heb. 13:16). I here waive my right to reject the authority of Ecclesiasticus; but I deny that the words of Ecclesiasticus, whoever the writer may have been, are faithfully quoted. The Greek reads as follows: Πάσῃ ἑλεημοσύνῃ ποιήσει τόπον̀ ἕκαστος γάρ κατὰ τὰ ἔργα αὑτου̑ εὑρήσει. "He will make room for all mercy: for each shall find according to his works." That this is the genuine reading, and that it has been corrupted in the Latin version, is plain both from the very structure of the sentence and from the preceding context. In the Epistle to the Hebrews there is no room for their quibbling over one little word, for in the Greek the apostle simply says that such sacrifices are pleasing and acceptable to God. This alone should be more than enough to quell and beat down the insolence of our pride and to keep us from setting a value on works beyond the rule of Scripture. It is the teaching of Scripture, moreover, that our good works are constantly covered with many stains by which God is justly offended and made angry with us, so far are they from being able to win him over and call forth his favor toward us; and yet, because in his forbearance he does not examine them with the utmost strictness, he accepts them just as if they were perfectly pure, and therefore rewards them, though they are undeserving, with countless blessings, both present and future. For I do not accept the distinction laid down by men otherwise learned and godly, that good works merit the favors conferred on us in this life, whereas eternal life is the reward of faith alone. Our Lord almost always places the reward of our labors and the crown of our contest in heaven. On the other hand, to ascribe to the merit of works, so as to deny it to grace, the fact that we are loaded with other gifts from the Lord is contrary to the teaching of Scripture. For though Christ says, "Unto every one that hath shall be given," and "thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things" (Matt. 25:29, 21), he at the same time shows that all additional gifts to believers come from his free kindness: "Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters, and he that hath no money; come ye, buy, and eat; yea, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price" (Isaiah 55:1). Therefore every aid to salvation bestowed on believers, and blessedness itself, are entirely the gift of God; and yet in both the Lord testifies that he takes account of works, since, to show the greatness of his love toward us, he so highly honors not only ourselves but the gifts which he has bestowed on us.

5¶

Had these points been properly handled and digested in past ages, so many tumults and dissensions could never have arisen. Paul says that in the building of Christian doctrine it is necessary to keep the foundation which he had laid among the Corinthians: "Other foundation can no man lay than that is laid, which is Jesus Christ" (1 Cor. 3:11). What, then, is our foundation in Christ? Is it that he begins salvation and leaves us to complete it? Is it that he only opened the way and left us to follow it in our own strength? By no means; but, as Paul had declared a little before, it is to acknowledge that he has been given to us for righteousness. No man, therefore, is well founded in Christ who does not have complete righteousness in him, since the apostle does not say that he was sent to help us obtain righteousness, but that he himself was to be our righteousness. Thus it is said that God "has chosen us in him before the foundation of the world," not according to our merit, but "according to the good pleasure of his will"; that in him "we have redemption through his blood, even the forgiveness of sins"; that peace has been made "through the blood of his cross"; that we are reconciled by his blood; that, placed under his protection, we are delivered from the danger of finally perishing; and that, being thus grafted into him, we are made partakers of eternal life and hope for admission into the kingdom of God. 1 Cor. 1:30; Eph. 1:3-5; Col. 1:14, 20; John 1:12; 10:28.

6¶

For a long time the world has been taught very differently. A kind of good works called moral has been invented, by which men are made agreeable to God before they are grafted into Christ; as if Scripture spoke falsely when it says, "He that hath the Son hath life; and he that hath not the Son of God hath not life" (1 John 5:12). How can they produce the materials of life if they are dead? Is there no meaning in its being said that "whatsoever is not of faith is sin" (Rom. 14:23)? Or can good fruit be produced from a bad tree? What have these most pestilent Sophists left to Christ on which to exercise his power? They say that he merited for us the first grace, that is, the opportunity of meriting, and that it is our part not to let slip the opportunity thus offered. O the daring effrontery of ungodliness! Who would have thought that men professing the name of Christ would strip him of his power in this way and all but trample him underfoot? The testimony consistently borne to him in Scripture is that whoever believes in him is justified; the doctrine of these men is that the only benefit which comes from him is to open a way for each man to justify himself. I wish they could get a taste of what is meant by these passages: "He that hath the Son hath life." "He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me," "is passed from death unto life." Whoever believes in him "is passed from death unto life." "Being justified freely by his grace, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus." "He that keepeth his commandments dwelleth in him, and he in him." God "has raised us up together, and made us sit together in heavenly places in Christ." "Who has delivered us from the power of darkness, and has translated us into the kingdom of his dear Son." John 5:12; John 5:24; Rom. 3:24; John 3:24; Eph. 2:6; Col. 1:13.

7¶

In this way the schools of the Sorbonne, the parents of all heresies, have robbed us of justification by faith, which lies at the root of all godliness. They confess in word, indeed, that men are justified by a formed faith, but they afterward explain this to mean that from faith they have good works which avail for justification, so that they almost seem to use the term faith in mockery, because they could not pass it over in silence without incurring great reproach, seeing that Scripture repeats it so often. And yet, not content with this, by their praise of good works they transfer to man what they steal from God. And since good works give little ground for exultation, and are not even properly called merits if they are regarded as the fruits of divine grace, they derive them from the power of free will; in other words, they extract oil from a stone. They do not deny that the principal cause lies in grace; but they contend that this does not exclude free will, through which all merit comes. This is the teaching not only of the later Sophists, but of Lombard, their Pythagoras (Sentences, book 2, distinction 28), who in comparison with them may be called sound and sober. It was surely a strange blindness that, while he had Augustine so often on his lips, he did not see how carefully Augustine guarded against ascribing a single particle of praise to man on account of good works. Above, when treating of free will, we quoted some passages from him to this effect, and similar passages occur frequently in his writings (see on Psalm 104; Letter 105), as when he forbids us ever to boast of our merits, because they too are the gifts of God, and when he says that all our merits come only from grace, are not supplied by our own sufficiency, but are entirely the product of grace, and so on. It is less strange that Lombard was blind to the light of Scripture, in which it is obvious that he had not been a very successful student. French, "d'autant qu'il n'y estoit gueres exercité" — inasmuch as he was little versed in it.

8¶

Therefore let us never on any account allow ourselves to be drawn away one nail's breadth French, "ne fust ce que de la pointe d'une sepingle" — were it only a pin's point.

Calvin presses one last guard against the boasted merit of works: it subverts both the glory of God in giving righteousness and the certainty of our salvation, for whatever good is found in our doing is itself the gift of grace, and the most perfect work we offer is still stained by the impurity of the one who offers it. To make Christ the mere opener of a way we finish in our own strength is to trample him underfoot; he was given to be our righteousness entire, not our assistant. Hold this fast in a faithless age that flatters human achievement and prices every soul by its accomplishments. In the home, teach the children that the rewards God graciously crowns upon works are his liberality, never our wages — lest gratitude curdle into pride. Steadfast under this conviction, the believer ceases the exhausting labor of self-justification and rests where the whole of salvation is found: outside himself, in the finished work of Christ, to the praise of the divine goodness alone.
