Of Vows — The Miserable Entanglements Caused by Vowing Rashly

This chapter consists of two parts,—I. Of vows in general, sec. 1-8. II. Of monastic vows, and specially of the vow of celibacy, sec. 8-21. 1. Some general principles with regard to the nature of vows. Superstitious errors not only of the heathen, but of Christians, in regard to vows. 2. Three points to be considered with regard to vows. First, to whom the vow is made—viz. to God. Nothing to be vowed to him but what he himself requires. 3. Second, Who we are that vow. We must measure our strength, and have regard to our calling. Fearful errors of the Popish clergy by not attending to this. Their vow of celibacy. 4. Third point to be attended to—viz. the intention with which the vow is made. Four ends in vowing. Two of them refer to the past, and two to the future. Examples and use of the former class. 5. End of vows which refer to the future. 6. The doctrine of vows in general. Common vow of Christians in Baptism, &c. This vow sacred and salutary. Particular vows how to be tested. 7. Great prevalence of superstition with regard to vows. 8. Vows of monks. Contrast between ancient and modern monasticism. 9. Portraiture of the ancient monks by Augustine. 10. Degeneracy of modern monks. 1. Inconsiderate rigour. 2. Idleness. 3. False boast of perfection. 11. This idea of monastic perfection refuted. 12. Arguments for monastic perfection. First argument answered. 13. Second argument answered. 14. Absurdity of representing the monastic profession as a second baptism. 15. Corrupt manners of monks. 16. Some defects in ancient monasticism. 17. General refutation of monastic vows. 18. Refutation continued. 19. Refutation continued. 20. Do such vows of celibacy bind the conscience? This question answered. 21. Those who abandon the monastic profession for an honest living, unjustly accused of breaking their faith.

1¶

It is indeed deplorable that the Church, whose freedom was purchased by the priceless blood of Christ, should have been so oppressed by a cruel tyranny, and almost buried under a huge mass of traditions; but at the same time the private folly of each individual shows that it is not without just cause that so much power has been given from above to Satan and his ministers. It was not enough to neglect the command of Christ and bear whatever burdens false teachers might please to impose; each individual had to have his own peculiar burdens, and so sink deeper by digging his own pit. This has been the result when men set about devising vows, by which a stronger and closer obligation might be added to the common ties. Having already shown that the worship of God was corrupted by the audacity of those who, under the name of pastors, domineered in the Church when they ensnared miserable souls by their wicked laws, it will not be out of place here to turn to a kindred evil, in order to make it plain that the world, in keeping with its depraved disposition, has always thrown every possible obstacle in the way of the helps by which it ought to have been brought to God. Moreover, so that the very grievous harm introduced by such vows may be more apparent, let the reader attend to the principles laid down earlier. First, we showed (Book 2, chapter 8, section 5) that everything required for ordering a godly and holy life is contained in the law. Second, we showed that the Lord, the better to dissuade us from devising new works, included the whole of righteousness in simple obedience to his will. If these positions are true, it is easy to see that all invented worship, which we ourselves devise for the purpose of serving God, is not in the least acceptable to him, however pleasing it may be to us. And unquestionably, in many passages the Lord not only openly rejects such worship but deeply abhors it. Hence a doubt arises with regard to vows which are made without any express authority from the word of God: in what light are they to be viewed? Can they be rightly made by Christian men, and to what extent are they binding? What is called a promise among men is a vow when it is made to God. Now, we promise to men either things which we think will be acceptable to them, or things which we owe them as a duty. Much more careful, therefore, ought we to be in vows which are directed to God, with whom we ought to deal with the greatest seriousness. Here superstition has in all ages strangely prevailed, men at once, without judgment and without discrimination, vowing to God whatever came into their minds or even rose to their lips. Hence the foolish vows, indeed the monstrous absurdities, by which the heathen insolently made sport of their gods. Would that Christians had not imitated them in this audacity of theirs! Nothing, indeed, could be less fitting; but it is obvious that for some ages nothing has been more common than this misconduct — the whole body of the people everywhere despising the Law of God, See Psalm 119:106. "I have sworn, and I will perform it, that I will keep thy righteous judgments." Calvin observes on these words that the vow and oath to keep the law cannot be charged with rashness, because it trusted in the promises of God concerning the forgiveness of sins, and in the spirit of regeneration.

2¶

If we would avoid error in deciding which vows are lawful and which are perverse, three things must be considered — namely, who it is to whom the vow is made; who we are who make it; and lastly, with what intention we make it. As regards the first, we should consider that we have to do with God, who so delights in our obedience that he abhors all will-worship, however attractive and splendid it may be in the eyes of men (Colossians 2:23). If all will-worship, which we devise without authority, is an abomination to God, it follows that no worship can be acceptable to him except that which is approved by his word. Therefore we must not claim such license for ourselves as to presume to vow anything to God without evidence of the value he places on it. For the doctrine of Paul, that whatever is not of faith is sin (Romans 14:23), while it extends to all actions of every kind, certainly applies with special force where the thought is turned directly toward God. Indeed, if in the smallest matters (Paul was then speaking of the distinction of foods) we err or fall where the sure light of faith does not shine before us, how much more modesty ought we to use when we undertake a matter of the greatest weight? For in nothing ought we to be more serious than in the duties of religion. In vows, then, our first precaution must be never to proceed to make any vow without having first resolved in our conscience to attempt nothing rashly. And we shall be safe from the danger of rashness when we have God going before us and, as it were, dictating from his word what is good and what is useless.

3¶

The second point which we mentioned as requiring consideration implies that we measure our strength and pay attention to our calling, so as not to neglect the blessing of liberty which God has conferred on us. For he who vows what is beyond his means, or is at odds with his calling, is rash, while he who despises the kindness of God in making him lord of all things is ungrateful. When I speak this way, I do not mean that anything is so placed in our hand that, leaning on our own strength, we may promise it to God. For in the Council of Orange (chapter 11) it was most truly decreed that nothing is rightly vowed to God except what we have received from his hand, since all things offered to him are merely his gifts. But since some things are given to us by the goodness of God and others withheld by his justice, every man should have regard to the measure of grace bestowed on him, as Paul commands (Romans 12:3; 1 Corinthians 12:11). All I mean here, then, is that your vows should be fitted to the measure which God prescribes to you by his gifts, lest by attempting more than he permits you claim too much for yourself and fall headlong. For example, when the assassins mentioned in Acts vowed "that they would neither eat nor drink till they had killed Paul" (Acts 23:12), even if it had not been an impious conspiracy, it would still have been intolerably presumptuous, since it subjected the life and death of a man to their own power. So Jephthah suffered for his folly when, with headlong fervor, he made a rash vow (Judges 11:30). In this class, the first place of insane audacity belongs to celibacy. Priests, monks, and nuns, forgetting their weakness, are confident of their fitness for celibacy. On the vow of celibacy, see Calvin, On Fleeing Unlawful Rites; Against the Theologians of Paris; On the Necessity of Reforming the Church; Preface to the Antidote to the Council of Trent; The True Method of Reforming the Church; On Scandals.

Bernard, On Conversion, to the Clergy, chapter 29, inveighing against the crimes of the clergy, says, "Would that those who cannot contain themselves would fear to take the vow of celibacy! For it is a weighty saying that not all can receive it. Many are either unable to hide it from the multitude, or do not try to. They abstain from the remedy of marriage, and afterward give themselves up to every wickedness."

4¶

I come now to my third position — namely, that if you would have your vow approved by God, the mind in which you undertake it is of great importance. For since God looks not on the outward appearance but on the heart, it follows that, according to the purpose the mind has in view, the same thing may at one time please him and be acceptable to him, and at another be most displeasing. If you vow abstinence from wine as if there were any holiness in doing so, you are superstitious; but if you have some end in view which is not perverse, no one can disapprove. Now, as far as I can see, there are four ends toward which our vows may properly be directed; two of these, for the sake of order, I refer to the past, and two to the future. To the past belong vows by which we either testify our gratitude toward God for favors received, or, in order to turn away his wrath, inflict punishment on ourselves for faults committed. The former, if you please, let us call acts of thanksgiving; the latter, acts of repentance. Of the former class we have an example in the tithes which Jacob vowed (Genesis 28:20) if the Lord would bring him safely home from exile; and also in the ancient peace offerings which godly kings and commanders, when about to engage in a just war, vowed that they would give if they were victorious, or at least if the Lord would deliver them when pressed by some greater difficulty. In this way are to be understood all the passages in the Psalms which speak of vows (Psalm 22:26; 56:13; 116:14, 18). Similar vows may also be used by us in our day, whenever the Lord has rescued us from some disaster, or dangerous disease, or other peril. For it is not foreign to the duty of a godly man to consecrate a votive offering to God in this way, as a formal sign of acknowledgment, so that he may not seem ungrateful for his kindness. The nature of the second class it will be enough to illustrate by just one familiar example. Should anyone, through gluttonous indulgence, have fallen into some iniquity, there is nothing to prevent him, with a view to chastising his intemperance, from renouncing all luxuries for a certain time, and in doing so from using a vow to bind himself more firmly. And yet I do not lay this down as an invariable law for all who have offended in a similar way; I merely show what may lawfully be done by those who think that such a vow will be useful to them. Thus, while I hold it lawful to vow in this way, I at the same time leave it free.

5¶

The vows which have reference to the future tend partly, as we have said, to make us more cautious, and partly to act as a kind of spur to the performance of duty. A man sees that he is so prone to a certain vice that, in a thing which is otherwise not bad, he cannot keep himself from falling straightway into evil: he will not act absurdly in cutting off the use of that thing for some time by a vow. If, for instance, someone should see that this or that bodily ornament brings him into danger, and yet, drawn on by desire, he eagerly longs for it, what can he do better than to put a curb on himself, that is, to impose on himself the necessity of abstinence, and so free himself from all doubt? In the same way, should someone be forgetful or sluggish in the necessary duties of godliness, why should he not, by making a vow, both awaken his memory and shake off his sloth? In both cases, I confess, there is a kind of tutelage; but inasmuch as they are helps to weakness, they are used not without profit by the ignorant and imperfect. Hence we hold that vows which have one of these ends in view, especially in outward things, are lawful, provided they are supported by the approval of God, are suited to our calling, and are limited to the measure of grace bestowed on us.

6¶

It is not now difficult to infer what view, on the whole, ought to be taken of vows. There is one vow common to all believers, which, taken in baptism, we confirm and, as it were, ratify by our Catechism, Latin, "Catechism." — French, "En faisant protestation de notre foy;" — in making profession of our faith.

7¶

It is now easy to see under how much superstition the world has labored in this respect for several ages. One vowed that he would abstain from wine, as if abstinence from wine were in itself an acceptable service to God. Another bound himself to fast, another to abstain from meat on certain days, which he had vainly imagined to be holier than other days. Things far more childish were vowed, though not by children. For it was counted great wisdom to undertake votive pilgrimages to holy places, and sometimes to make the journey on foot, or with the body half naked, so that greater merit might be gained by greater fatigue. These and similar things, for which the world has long bustled about with incredible zeal, if they are tested by the rules we laid down earlier, will be found to be not only empty and trifling, but full of manifest impiety. Whatever the judgment of the flesh may be, there is nothing God abhors more than invented worship. To these are added harmful and damnable notions: hypocrites, after performing such trifles, think they have acquired no ordinary righteousness, place the substance of godliness in outward observances, and despise all others who seem less careful about them.

8¶

It is of no use to list all the separate forms. But since monastic vows are held in great veneration, because they seem to be approved by the public judgment of the Church, I will say a few words about them. And first, lest anyone defend the monasticism of our day on the ground of long-standing prescription, it should be observed that the ancient way of living in monasteries was very different. Those who withdrew to them were men who wished to train themselves to the greatest austerity and endurance. The discipline the monks practiced then resembled that which the Lacedaemonians are said to have used under the laws of Lycurgus, and was even much more rigorous. They slept on the ground; their drink was water; their food bread, herbs, and roots; their chief luxuries oil and pulse. They abstained from more delicate food and from pampering the body. These things might seem exaggerated were they not vouched for by experienced eyewitnesses, such as Gregory Nazianzen, Basil, and Chrysostom. By such elementary training they prepared themselves for greater offices. For that the monastic colleges were then a kind of seminary for the ecclesiastical order, those whom we have just named are very competent witnesses (they were all brought up in monasteries and called from there to the episcopal office), as are several other great and excellent men of their age. Augustine also shows that in his time the monasteries were accustomed to supply the Church with clergy. For he addresses the monks of the island of Capraria in this way: "We exhort you, brethren, in the Lord, to keep your purpose and persevere to the end; and if at any time our mother the Church requires your labor, you will neither undertake it with eager elation nor reject it out of the allurement of sloth, but with meek hearts obey God. You will not prefer your own ease to the needs of the Church. Had no good men been willing to minister to her when she was in labor, it would have been impossible for you to be born." In the same place he admirably says, "Dearly beloved, love ease, but with a view to restraining yourselves from all worldly delight, and remember that there is no place where he who dreads our return to God is not able to lay his snares."

9¶

Augustine, in two passages in particular, gives a portrait of the form of ancient monasticism. The one is in his book De Moribus Ecclesiœ Catholicœ (On the Manners of the Catholic Church), where he defends the holiness of that profession against the slanders of the Manichees; the other is in a treatise entitled De Opere Monachorum (On the Work of Monks), where he inveighs against certain degenerate monks who had begun to corrupt that institution. I will give here a summary of what he says there, and as far as I can in his own words: "Despising the allurements of this world, and gathered together for a most chaste and most holy life, they spend their lives together, passing their time in prayer, reading, and conversation, not swollen with pride, not turbulent through insolence, not livid with envy. No one possesses anything of his own; no one is a burden to any man. They labor with their hands at things by which the body may be fed and the mind not withdrawn from God. The fruit of their labor they hand over to those whom they call deans. These deans, disposing of the whole with great care, render an account to one whom they call father. These fathers, who are not only of the purest morals but most distinguished for divine learning, and noble in all things, without any pride consult those whom they call their sons, though the former have full authority to command and the latter a great readiness to obey. At the close of the day they come together, each from his cell, and without having broken their fast, to hear their father; and they gather under each father to the number of three thousand at least" (he is speaking of Egypt and the East). "Then the body is refreshed as far as suffices for safety and health, everyone curbing his desire so as not to be excessive even in the scanty and very plain diet that is provided. Thus they abstain not only from meat and wine, for the purpose of subduing lust, but also from those things which provoke the appetite of the stomach and throat all the more readily because they seem to some, as it were, more refined. In this way the desire for exquisite delicacies, in which there is no meat, is commonly defended absurdly and shamefully. Any surplus after necessary food (and the surplus is very great, from the labor of their hands and the frugality of their meals) is carefully distributed to the needy, all the more carefully because it was not obtained by those who distribute it. For they never act with a view to having abundance for themselves, but always act with a view to allowing no superfluity to remain with them" (Augustine, On the Manners of the Catholic Church, chapter 31). Afterward, describing their austerity, of which he had himself seen instances both at Milan and elsewhere, he says: "Meanwhile no one is pressed to austerities which he is unable to bear; no one is obliged to do what he declines, nor is he condemned by the others, whom he confesses himself too weak to imitate. For they remember how greatly charity is commended; they remember that to the pure all things are pure (Titus 1:15). Therefore all their vigilance is spent, not in rejecting kinds of food as polluted, but in subduing desire and maintaining brotherly love. They remember, 'Meats for the belly, and the belly for meats,' and so on (1 Corinthians 6:13). Many, though strong, abstain because of the weak. In many this is not the reason for their conduct; they take pleasure in sustaining themselves on the plainest and least expensive food. Hence the very persons who restrain themselves in health do not refuse in sickness to use what their health requires. Many do not drink wine, and yet do not think themselves polluted by it, for they most kindly have it given to the sicklier and to those whose health requires it; and some who foolishly refuse it they admonish as brothers, lest by vain superstition they become weaker rather than holier. Thus they diligently practice godliness, while they know that bodily exercise is only for a short time. Charity especially is observed: their food is fitted to charity, their speech to charity, their dress to charity, their looks to charity. They come together and breathe only charity; they count it as unlawful to offend charity as to offend God; if anyone opposes it, he is cast out and shunned; if anyone offends against it, he is not permitted to remain one day" (Augustine, On the Manners of the Catholic Church, chapter 33). Since this holy man seems in these words to have displayed the monastic life of ancient times as in a picture, I have thought it right to insert them here, though they are somewhat long, because I see that I would be considerably longer if I gathered them from different writers, however concise I might try to be.

10¶

Here, however, I had no intention of discussing the whole subject. I only wished to show in passing what kind of monks the early Church had, and what the monastic profession was then, so that from the contrast sound readers might judge how great is the effrontery of those who allege antiquity in support of present-day monkery. Augustine, while tracing out a holy and lawful monasticism, would keep away all rigorous exaction of those things which the word of the Lord has left free. But in our day nothing is more rigorously exacted. For they consider it an inexpiable crime if anyone departs in the least degree from the prescribed form in the color or kind of dress, in the kind of food, or in other frivolous and cold ceremonies. Augustine strenuously contends that it is not lawful for monks to live in idleness on other men's means (Augustine, On the Work of Monks). He denies that any such example was to be found in his day in a well-ordered monastery. Our monks place the principal part of their holiness in idleness. For if you take away their idleness, where will that contemplative life be, by which they boast that they excel all others and come near to the angels? Augustine, in short, requires a monasticism which is nothing else than a training for, and an aid to, the duties of godliness commended to all Christians. What then? When he makes charity its chief and almost its only rule, do we think he praises that league by which a few men, bound to one another, are separated from the whole body of the Church? On the contrary, he wishes them to set others an example of preserving the unity of the Church. So different in both respects is the nature of present-day monasticism that it would be difficult to find anything so unlike, not to say contrary. For our monks, not satisfied with that godliness which alone Christ commands his followers to be intent on pursuing, imagine some new kind of godliness, by aspiring to which they are more perfect than all other men.

11¶

If they deny this, I should like to know why they honor their own order alone with the title of perfection and deny it to all other divine callings. Laurentius, defending his written assertion that the monks falsely imagined that by means of their profession they merited more than others, admirably concludes, "There is no safer, no better way than that taught by Christ, and in it no profession is enjoined."

French, ",Par ce moyen ils attirent farine au moulin et vendent leur sainteté tres cherement; cependant cette glose est cachee et comme ensevelie en peu de livres;" — by this means they bring grist to their mill and sell their holiness very dear; meanwhile the gloss is hidden and, as it were, buried in a few books.

12¶

But let them say that I slandered them when I declared that they were not content with the rule prescribed by God. Still, even if I were silent, they accuse themselves more than enough; for they plainly declare that they take on a greater burden than Christ has laid on his followers, since they promise that they will keep the evangelical counsels concerning the love of enemies, the suppression of vengeful feelings, and abstinence from swearing, counsels to which Christians are not commonly bound. In this, what antiquity can they claim? None of the ancients ever thought of such a thing: all with one voice proclaim that not one syllable came from Christ which it is not necessary to obey. And the very things which these worthy expounders pretend that Christ only counseled, the ancients uniformly declare, without any doubt, that he expressly commanded. But since we have shown above that this is a most pestilent error, let it suffice here to have briefly noted that monasticism as it now exists is founded on an idea which all godly men ought to detest — namely, the pretense that there is some more perfect rule of life than the common rule which God has delivered to the whole Church. Whatever is built on this foundation cannot but be abominable.

13¶

But they produce another argument for their perfection, and consider it invincible. Our Lord said to the young man who asked him about the perfection of righteousness, "If thou wilt be perfect, go and sell that thou hast, and give to the poor" (Matthew 19:21). Whether they do so I do not now dispute. Let us grant for the present that they do. They boast, then, that they have become perfect by abandoning their all. If the sum of perfection consists in this, what is the meaning of Paul's doctrine that though a man should give all his goods to feed the poor, and have not charity, he is nothing? (1 Corinthians 13:3). What kind of perfection is that which, if charity is lacking, is reduced to nothing along with the man himself? Here they must necessarily answer that it is indeed the highest work of perfection, but not the only one. But here again Paul intervenes, and does not hesitate to declare that charity, without any renunciation of that sort, is the "bond of perfectness" (Colossians 3:14). If it is certain that there is no disagreement between the scholar and the master, and the latter clearly denies that the perfection of a man consists in renouncing all his goods, and on the other hand asserts that perfection may exist without it, we must see in what sense we should understand the words of Christ, "If thou wilt be perfect, go and sell that thou hast." Now, there will not be the least obscurity in the meaning if we consider (and this ought to be attended to in all our Savior's discourses) to whom the words are addressed (Luke 10:25). A young man asks by what works he shall enter into eternal life. Christ, since he was asked about works, refers him to the law. And rightly; for, considered in itself, the law is the way of eternal life, and its inability to give eternal life is owing to our depravity. By this answer Christ declared that he was not delivering any other rule of life than that which had formerly been delivered in the law of the Lord. Thus he both bore witness to the divine law, that it was a doctrine of perfect righteousness, and at the same time met the slanderous charge of seeming, by some new rule of life, to incite the people to revolt from the law. The young man, who was not ill-disposed but was puffed up with vain confidence, answers that he had kept all the precepts of the law from his youth. It is absolutely certain that he was immeasurably far from the goal he boasted of having reached. Had his boast been true, he would have lacked nothing of absolute perfection. For it has been demonstrated above that the law contains a perfect righteousness. This is obvious even from the fact that the keeping of it is called the way to eternal life. To show him how little progress he had made in that righteousness which he too boldly answered that he had fulfilled, it was right to bring before him his besetting sin. Now, since he abounded in riches, he had his heart set on them. Therefore, because he did not feel this secret wound, Christ probes it: "Go," he says, "and sell that thou hast." Had he been as good a keeper of the law as he supposed, he would not have gone away sorrowful on hearing these words. For he who loves God with his whole heart not only regards everything that wars against his love as dross, but hates it as destruction (Philippians 3:8). Therefore, when Christ orders a rich miser to leave all that he has, it is the same as if he had ordered the ambitious man to renounce all his honors, the pleasure-seeker all his luxuries, the unchaste man all the instruments of his lust. Thus consciences which are not reached by any general admonition are to be recalled to a particular sense of their particular sin. In vain, therefore, do they twist that special case into a general interpretation, as if Christ had decided that the perfection of man consists in the abandonment of his goods, since by the expression he intended nothing more than to bring a youth who was beyond measure satisfied with himself to feel his sore, and so to understand that he was still far from that perfect obedience to the law which he falsely ascribed to himself. I admit that this passage was poorly understood by some of the Fathers; Chrysostom, in his Homily on the words of Paul, "Salute Prisca," and so on, says, "All who retire to monasteries separate themselves from the Church, seeing they plainly assert that their monasticism is the form of a second baptism."

14¶

Still, nothing was further from the intention of the Fathers than to establish that kind of perfection which was afterward fabricated by cowled monks in order to raise up a kind of double Christianity. For the sacrilegious dogma had not yet been broached which compares the profession of monasticism to baptism, indeed plainly asserts that it is the form of a second baptism. Who can doubt that the Fathers abhorred such blasphemy with their whole hearts? Then what need is there to demonstrate in words that the last quality which Augustine mentions as belonging to the ancient monks — namely, that they fitted themselves to charity in all things — is utterly foreign to this new profession? The thing itself declares that all who retire into monasteries withdraw from the Church. How so? Do they not separate themselves from the lawful fellowship of the faithful by acquiring for themselves a special ministry and a private administration of the sacraments? What is it to destroy the communion of the Church, if this is not? And to follow out the comparison with which I began, and close the point at once, what resemblance do they have in this respect to the ancient monks? Those, though they dwelt apart from others, did not have a separate Church; they partook of the sacraments with others, they attended the public assemblies, and they were then part of the people. But what have these men done in setting up a private altar for themselves but break the bond of unity? For they have excommunicated themselves from the whole body of the Church and despised the ordinary ministry by which the Lord has been pleased that peace and charity should be preserved among his followers. Therefore I hold that as many monasteries as there are in our day, so many conventicles are there of schismatics, who have disturbed ecclesiastical order and been cut off from the lawful fellowship of the faithful. And so that there might be no doubt about their separation, they have given themselves the various names of factions. They have not been ashamed to glory in that which Paul so detests that he cannot express his abhorrence too strongly. Unless, indeed, we suppose that Christ was not divided by the Corinthians when one teacher set himself above another (1 Corinthians 1:12, 13; 3:4), and that now no injury is done to Christ when, instead of Christians, we hear some called Benedictines, others Franciscans, others Dominicans, and so called that, while they affect to be distinguished from the common body of Christians, they proudly substitute these names for a religious profession.

15¶

The differences I have pointed out so far between the ancient monks and those of our age are not in conduct but in profession. Let my readers remember, then, that I have spoken of monasticism rather than of monks, and have noted not the vices which cling to a few, but vices which are inseparable from the very way of life. As regards conduct, what use is there in going into particulars and showing how great the difference is? This much is certain, See Bernard, to William, Abbot: "I wonder why there is so much intemperance among monks. O vanity of vanities! but not more vain than insane." See also Augustine, On the Work of Monks, at the end.

16¶

By this contrast between ancient and modern monasticism, I trust I have achieved my object, which was to show that our cowled monks falsely claim the example of the primitive Church in defense of their profession, since they differ no less from the monks of that period than apes do from men. Meanwhile, I do not hide that even in that ancient form which Augustine commends there was something which pleases me little. I admit that they were not superstitious in the outward exercises of a more rigorous discipline, but I say that they were not without a degree of affectation and false zeal. It was a fine thing to cast away their possessions and free themselves from all worldly cares; but God sets more value on the godly management of a household, when its head, discarding all avarice, ambition, and other lusts of the flesh, makes it his purpose to serve God in some particular calling. It is fine to philosophize in seclusion, far removed from the company of society; but it ill accords with Christian meekness for anyone, as if in hatred of the human race, to flee to the wilderness and to solitude, and at the same time to desert the duties which the Lord has especially commanded. Even if we granted that there was nothing worse in that profession, there is certainly no small evil in its having introduced a useless and dangerous example into the Church.

17¶

Now, then, let us see the nature of the vows by which the monks of our day are initiated into this famous order. First, since their intention is to set up a new and invented worship with a view to winning favor with God, I conclude from what has been said above that everything they vow is an abomination to God. Second, I hold that since they frame their own way of life at their pleasure, without any regard to the calling of God or to his approval, the attempt is rash and unlawful, because their conscience has no ground on which it can rest before God; and "whatsoever is not of faith is sin" (Romans 14:23). Moreover, I maintain that in binding themselves to many perverse and impious forms of worship, such as are displayed in modern monasticism, they consecrate themselves not to God but to the devil. For why should the prophets have been permitted to say that the Israelites sacrificed their sons to devils and not to God (Deuteronomy 32:17; Psalm 106:37), merely because they had corrupted the true worship of God with profane ceremonies, and we not be permitted to say the same of monks who, along with the cowl, wrap themselves in the net of a thousand impious superstitions? Then what is the nature of their vows? They offer God a promise of perpetual virginity, as if they had previously made a compact with him to free them from the necessity of marriage. They cannot allege that they make this vow trusting entirely to the grace of God; for since he declares this to be a special gift not given to all (Matthew 19:11), no man has a right to assume that the gift will be his. Let those who have it use it; and if at any time they feel the weakness of the flesh, let them turn to the help of him by whose power alone they can resist. If this does not avail, let them not despise the remedy which is offered to them. If the power of continence is denied, the voice of God plainly calls them to marry. By continence I mean not merely that by which the body is kept pure from fornication, but that by which the mind keeps its chastity unstained. For Paul commands caution not only against outward lewdness but also against burning of mind (1 Corinthians 7:9). It has been the practice, they say, from the remotest times for those who wished to devote themselves entirely to God to bind themselves by a vow of continence. I confess that the custom is ancient, but I do not admit that the age in which it began was so free from every defect that everything done then is to be regarded as a rule. Moreover, the unbending rigor of holding that once the vow is made there is no room for repentance crept in gradually. This is clear from Cyprian: "If virgins have dedicated themselves to Christian faith, let them live modestly and chastely, without pretense. Thus, strong and steadfast, let them wait for the reward of virginity. But if they will not or cannot persevere, it is better to marry than by their faults to fall into the fire." In our day, with what invectives would they not tear apart anyone who sought to temper the vow of continence by such a fair course? Those, therefore, have wandered far from the ancient custom who not only use no moderation and grant no pardon when anyone proves unequal to keeping his vow, but shamelessly declare that it is a more heinous sin to cure the intemperance of the flesh by marriage than to defile body and soul by whoredom.

18¶

But they still insist, and try to show that this vow was used in the days of the apostles, because Paul says that widows who marry after having once undertaken a public office "cast off their first faith" (1 Timothy 5:12). I by no means deny that widows who dedicated themselves and their labors to the Church at the same time came under an obligation of perpetual celibacy, not because they regarded it as a religious duty, as afterward began to be the case, but because they could not perform their functions unless they had their time at their own disposal and were free from the marriage tie. But if, after giving their pledge, they began to look toward a new marriage, what else was this but to shake off the calling of God? It is not strange, therefore, when Paul says that through such desires they grow wanton against Christ. In further explanation he afterward adds that, by not keeping their promises to the Church, they violate and nullify their first faith given in baptism, one of the things contained in this first faith being that everyone should answer to his calling. Unless you prefer to interpret it as meaning that, having lost their modesty, they afterward cast off all concern for decency, giving themselves over to every kind of lewdness and forwardness, leading licentious and dissolute lives, than which nothing can less become Christian women. I am much pleased with this exposition. Our answer, then, is that those widows who were admitted to a public ministry came under an obligation of perpetual celibacy, and hence we easily understand how, when they married, they threw off all modesty and became more insolent than became Christian women, so that in this way they not only sinned by violating the faith given to the Church, but revolted from the common rule of godly women. But first, I deny that they had any other reason for professing celibacy than simply that marriage was altogether inconsistent with the function they undertook. Hence they bound themselves to celibacy only so far as the nature of their function required. Second, I do not admit that they were bound to celibacy in such a sense that it was not better for them to marry than to suffer under the incitements of the flesh and fall into uncleanness. Third, I hold that what Paul commanded was, in the ordinary case, free from danger, because he orders the selection to be made from those who, content with one marriage, had already given proof of continence. Our only reason for disapproving of the vow of celibacy is that it is wrongly regarded as an act of worship, and is rashly undertaken by persons who do not have the power to keep it.

19¶

But what ground can there be for applying this passage to nuns? For deaconesses were appointed, not to soothe God with chanting or unintelligible murmuring and spend the rest of their time in idleness, but to carry out a public ministry of the Church toward the poor, and to labor with all zeal, constancy, and diligence in works of charity. They did not vow celibacy so that they might afterward display abstinence from marriage as a kind of worship rendered to God, but only so that they might be freer from encumbrance in carrying out their office. In short, they did not vow on reaching adolescence or in the bloom of life, and so afterward learn by too-late experience over what a precipice they had plunged themselves; but after they were thought to have passed beyond all danger, they took a vow no less safe than holy. But not to press the two former points, I say that it was unlawful to allow women to take a vow of continence before their sixtieth year, since the apostle admits such only, and commands the younger to marry and bear children. Therefore it is impossible on any ground to excuse the lowering of the age, first by twelve years, then by twenty, and finally by thirty. Still less possible is it to tolerate the case of miserable girls who, before they have reached an age at which they can know themselves or have any experience of their own character, are not only lured by fraud but compelled by force and threats to entangle themselves in these accursed snares. I will not enter at length into a refutation of the other two vows. This only I say: that besides involving, as matters stand in our day, not a few superstitions, they seem to be deliberately framed in such a way as to make those who take them mock God and men. But lest we should seem, with too spiteful a feeling, to attack every particular point, we will be content with the general refutation which has been given above.

20¶

The nature of the vows which are lawful and acceptable to God I think I have sufficiently explained. Yet because some ill-informed and timid consciences, even when a vow displeases them and is condemned, still hesitate as to its obligation and are grievously tormented, shuddering at the thought of breaking a pledge given to God and, on the other hand, fearing to sin more by keeping it, we must come to their aid here and enable them to escape from this difficulty. And to remove every scruple at once, I say that all vows which are not lawful and not rightly made, since they are of no account with God, should be regarded by us as null. (See Calvin, to the Council of Trent.) For if in human contracts only those promises are binding in which the one with whom we contract wishes to have us bound, it is absurd to say that we are bound to perform things which God does not require of us at all, especially since our works can be right only when they please God and have the testimony of our consciences that they do please him. For it always remains fixed that "whatsoever is not of faith is sin" (Romans 14:23). By this Paul means that any work undertaken in doubt is faulty, because at the root of all good works lies faith, which assures us that they are acceptable to God. Therefore, if Christian men may not attempt anything without this assurance, why, if they have undertaken something rashly through ignorance, may they not afterward be set free and desist from their error? Since vows rashly undertaken are of this kind, they not only do not bind, but must necessarily be rescinded. What, then, when they are not only of no value in the sight of God but are even an abomination, as has already been demonstrated? There is no need to discuss further a point which does not require it. To quiet godly consciences and free them from all doubt, this one argument seems to me sufficient — namely, that all works whatever which do not flow from a pure fountain and are not directed to a proper end are rejected by God, and so rejected that he forbids us to continue them no less than to begin them. Hence it follows that vows dictated by error and superstition are of no weight with God and ought to be abandoned by us.

21¶

He who understands this solution is furnished with the means of repelling the slanders of the wicked against those who withdraw from monasticism to some honest way of making a living. They are grievously charged with having perjured themselves and broken their faith, because they have broken the bond, commonly supposed to be indissoluble, by which they had bound themselves to God and the Church. But I say, first, that there is no bond when God abrogates what man confirms; and second, that even granting they were bound while they remained entangled in ignorance and error, now that they have been enlightened by the knowledge of the truth, I hold that they are at the same time free by the grace of Christ. For if such is the power of the cross of Christ that it frees us from the curse of the divine law by which we were held bound, how much more must it rescue us from foreign chains, which are nothing but the cunning nets of Satan? There can be no doubt, therefore, that all on whom Christ shines with the light of his Gospel he frees from all the snares in which they had entangled themselves through superstition. At the same time, they have another defense if they were unfit for celibacy. For if an impossible vow is certain destruction to the soul, which God wills to be saved and not destroyed, it follows that it ought by no means to be held to. Now, how impossible the vow of continence is for those who have not received it as a special gift we have shown, and experience declares it even if I were silent; while the great obscenity with which almost all monasteries teem is no secret. If some seem more decent and modest than others, they are not, however, chaste. The sin of unchastity presses on them and lurks within. Thus God, by fearful examples, punishes the audacity of men when, forgetful of their weakness, they reach against nature for what has been denied them, and, despising the remedies which the Lord has placed in their hands, are confident of their ability to overcome the disease of incontinence by stubborn obstinacy. For what other name can we give it when a man, warned of his need of marriage and of the remedy with which the Lord has thereby furnished him, not only despises it but binds himself by an oath to despise it?

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin's warning about vows cuts against the perennial human itch to invent some service to God that he never asked for and to bind the conscience with chains of our own forging. A vow is legitimate only when offered to the God who delights in obedience, measured to the strength and calling he has actually given, and aimed at gratitude or genuine help to our weakness—never at a manufactured holiness that despises the ordinary obedience owed by every believer. In 2026 the snare wears new clothing: the spiritual performance that outruns Scripture, the rash commitment made in fervor and kept in misery, the quiet pride that ranks one's chosen austerities above faithful work, marriage, and the daily duties God commands. There is no higher rule than the one delivered to the whole church. Steward your household, keep your callings, and let no self-imposed burden crowd out the simple obedience that pleases God—the surest piety is not the one we devise, but the one he commands.
