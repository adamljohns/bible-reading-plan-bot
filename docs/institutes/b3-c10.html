How to Use the Present Life, and the Comforts of It

The divisions of this chapter are, I. The necessity and usefulness of this doctrine. Extremes to be avoided, if we would rightly use the present life and its comforts, sec. 1, 2. II. One of these extremes—viz. the intemperance of the flesh—to be carefully avoided. Four methods of doing so described in order, sec. 3-6. 1. Necessity of this doctrine. Use of the goods of the present life. Extremes to be avoided. 1. Excessive austerity. 2. Carnal intemperance and lasciviousness. 2. God, by creating so many mercies, consulted not only for our necessities, but also for our comfort and delight. Confirmation from a passage in the Psalms, and from experience. 3. Excessive austerity, therefore, to be avoided. So also must the wantonness of the flesh. 1. The creatures invite us to know, love, and honour the Creator. 2. This not done by the wicked, who only abuse these temporal mercies. 4. All earthly blessings to be despised in comparison of the heavenly life. Aspiration after this life destroyed by an excessive love of created objects. First, Intemperance. 5. Second, Impatience and immoderate desire. Remedy of these evils. The creatures assigned to our use. Man still accountable for the use he makes of them. 6. God requires us in all our actions to look to his calling. Use of this doctrine. It is full of comfort.

1¶

By such first principles Scripture at the same time instructs us well in the proper use of earthly blessings, a subject which is by no means to be neglected in laying out a plan of life. For if we are to live, we must use the necessary supports of life; nor can we even avoid those things which seem to serve delight more than necessity. We must therefore keep to a mean, so that we may use them with a pure conscience, whether for necessity or for pleasure. This the Lord prescribes by his word when he tells us that for his people the present life is a kind of pilgrimage by which they hasten to the heavenly kingdom. If we are only to pass through the earth, there can be no doubt that we are to use its blessings only so far as they help our progress rather than hinder it. Accordingly, Paul, not without cause, warns us to use this world without abusing it, and to buy possessions as though we were selling them (1 Corinthians 7:30, 31). But since this is slippery ground, and there is great danger of falling on either side, let us plant our feet where we can stand safely. There have been some good and holy men who, seeing that intemperance and luxury are always carried to excess unless strictly curbed, and wishing to correct so ruinous an evil, imagined that there was no other method than to allow man to use bodily goods only so far as they were necessities: a godly counsel indeed, but needlessly severe; for it does the very dangerous thing of binding consciences in tighter fetters than those with which they are bound by the word of God. 2¶

Let this be our principle: we do not go wrong in the use of the gifts of Providence when we refer them to the end for which their Author made and destined them, since he created them for our good and not for our destruction. No man will keep to the true path better than he who keeps this end carefully in view. Now, then, if we consider for what end he created food, we shall find that he provided not only for our necessity but also for our enjoyment and delight. So in clothing, the end, besides necessity, was comeliness and honor; and in herbs, fruits, and trees, besides their various uses, beauty of appearance and sweetness of smell. If it were not so, the Prophet would not list among the mercies of God "wine that maketh glad the heart of man, and oil to make his face to shine" (Psalm 104:15). The Scriptures would not everywhere mention, in praise of his kindness, that he had given such things to men. The natural qualities of things themselves show to what end, and how far, they may lawfully be enjoyed. Has the Lord adorned the flowers with all the beauty that spontaneously meets the eye, and the sweet fragrance that delights the sense of smell, and shall it be unlawful for us to enjoy that beauty and this fragrance? What? Has he not so distinguished the colors as to make some more pleasing than others? Has he not given qualities to gold and silver, ivory and marble, by which he has made them precious above other metals or stones? In short, has he not given many things a value apart from any necessary use?

3¶

Away, then, with that inhuman philosophy which, in allowing no use of the creatures except for necessity, not only maliciously robs us of the lawful fruit of the divine kindness, but cannot be put into practice without stripping man of all his senses and reducing him to a block. But, on the other hand, let us guard with no less care against the lusts of the flesh, which, if they are not kept in order, break through all bounds, and which are, as I have said, championed by those who, under the pretext of liberty, allow themselves every kind of license. The first restraint is imposed when we hold that the purpose of creating all things was to teach us to know their Author and to feel grateful for his kindness. Where is the gratitude if you so gorge or stupefy yourself with feasting and wine as to be unfit for the offices of godliness or the duties of your calling? Where is the acknowledgment of God if the flesh, boiling over in lust through excessive indulgence, infects the mind with its impurity, so that it loses the discernment of honor and uprightness? Where is thankfulness to God for clothing if, because of costly garments, we both admire ourselves and look down on others? If, from a love of show and splendor, we pave the way for immodesty? Where is our acknowledgment of God if the glare of these things captivates our minds? For many are so given over to luxury in all their senses that their mind lies buried; many are so delighted with marble, gold, and pictures that they become hearts of marble — are changed, as it were, into metal and made like painted figures. The kitchen, with its savory smells, so absorbs them that they have no spiritual savor. The same thing may be seen in other matters. It is plain, therefore, that there is great need here to curb licentious abuse and to conform to the rule of Paul, "make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof" (Romans 13:14). Where too much liberty is given to them, they break out without measure or restraint.

4¶

There is no surer or quicker way of accomplishing this than by despising the present life and aspiring to heavenly immortality. For from this two rules arise. First, "it remaineth, that both they that have wives be as though they had none"; "and they that use this world, as not abusing it" (1 Corinthians 7:29, 31). Second, we must learn to be no less calm and patient in enduring want than moderate in enjoying abundance. He who makes it his rule to use this world as if he did not use it not only cuts off all gluttony in food and drink, and all effeminacy, ambition, pride, excessive display, and austerity in his table, his house, and his clothes, but also removes every care and affection that might draw him away or hinder him from aspiring to the heavenly life and tending to the good of his soul. The French reads, "Parer notre ame de ses vrais ornemens" — deck our soul with its true ornaments.

5¶

Another rule is that those in narrow and slender circumstances should learn to bear their wants patiently, so that they may not become immoderately eager for things whose moderate use shows no small progress in the school of Christ. For besides the many other vices that go along with a longing for earthly goods, he who is impatient in poverty almost always displays the opposite disease in abundance. By this I mean that he who is ashamed of a shabby garment will be vain of a splendid one; he who, not content with a plain supper, is annoyed at the lack of a more luxurious one, will abuse his luxury without restraint if he obtains it; he who finds it hard and is dissatisfied to submit to a private and humble condition will be unable to keep from pride if he attains to honor. Let it be the aim of all who have any sincere desire for godliness to learn, after the example of the Apostle, "both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need" (Philippians 4:12). Scripture, moreover, has a third rule for regulating the use of earthly blessings. We have already touched on it when considering the offices of love. For it declares that they have all been given us by the kindness of God, and appointed for our use on the condition that we regard them as trusts, for which we must one day give account. We must therefore manage them as if we constantly heard the words ringing in our ears, "Give an account of your stewardship." At the same time, let us remember by whom the account is to be taken — namely, by him who, while he so highly commends abstinence, sobriety, frugality, and moderation, abhors luxury, pride, ostentation, and vanity; who approves of no management but that which is joined with love; who with his own lips has already condemned all those pleasures which draw the heart away from chastity and purity or darken the understanding.

6¶

The last thing to be observed is that the Lord commands every one of us, in all the actions of life, to have regard to his own calling. He knows the seething restlessness of the human mind, the fickleness with which it is carried this way and that, its eagerness to grasp opposites at the same time, its ambition. Therefore, lest all things should be thrown into confusion by our folly and rashness, he has assigned distinct duties to each in the different ways of life. And so that no one may presume to overstep his proper limits, he has distinguished the different ways of life by the name of callings. Every man's way of life, therefore, is a kind of post assigned to him by the Lord, so that he may not be driven about at random all his days. So necessary is this distinction that all our actions are judged by it in his sight, and often in a very different way from that in which human reason or philosophy would judge them. There is no more illustrious deed, even among the philosophers, than to free one's country from tyranny; and yet the private individual who stabs the tyrant is openly condemned by the voice of the heavenly Judge. But I do not wish to dwell on particular examples; it is enough to know that in everything the call of the Lord is the foundation and beginning of right action. He who does not act with reference to it will never keep the right path in the discharge of his duty. He may perhaps sometimes be able to give the appearance of something praiseworthy, but whatever it may be in the sight of man, it will be rejected before the throne of God; and besides, there will be no harmony among the different parts of his life. Hence only he who directs his life to this end will have it properly ordered; because, free from the impulse of rashness, he will not attempt more than his calling warrants, knowing that it is unlawful to leap over the bounds set for him. He who is obscure will not refuse to lead a private life, so that he may not desert the post at which God has placed him. Again, in all our cares, toils, annoyances, and other burdens, it will be no small relief to know that all these are under the oversight of God. The magistrate will perform his office more willingly, and the father of a family will keep to his proper sphere. Everyone in his particular way of life will bear its inconveniences, cares, discomforts, and anxieties without complaining, persuaded that God has laid the burden on him. This, too, will give admirable comfort: that in following your proper calling, no work will be so lowly and mean as not to have a splendor and value in the eyes of God.

Having taught us to look toward heaven, Calvin now steers between two ditches in the use of earthly things — the false austerity that forbids what God gave for delight, and the carnal license that gorges on his gifts until the soul lies buried. God made wine to gladden and flowers to please; to despise them is ingratitude, yet to abuse them is to forget the Giver and answer for a wasted stewardship. The 2026 home needs this sober middle: neither the grim suspicion of every good thing nor the appetite the age inflames at every screen, but a thankful, measured enjoyment that never withdraws the heart from the heavenly life. Learn, with Paul, to be full and to be hungry — content in plenty, patient in want — remembering that we hold all things in trust and must one day give account. And honor the doctrine of callings: whatever post the Lord has assigned, however obscure, fill it without repining, for no work is so mean that it lacks splendor in the eye of God.
