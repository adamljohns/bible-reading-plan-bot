The Arguments Usually Alleged in Support of Free Will Refuted

Objections reduced to three principal heads:—I. Four absurdities advanced by the opponents of the orthodox doctrine concerning the slavery of the will, stated and refuted, sec. 1–5. II. The passages of Scripture which they pervert in favour of their error, reduced to five heads, and explained, sec. 6–15. III. Five other passages quoted in defence of free will expounded, sec. 16–19.

1¶

Enough would seem to have been said on the subject of man's will, were there not some who try to drive him to his ruin by a false opinion of liberty, and who at the same time attack our view in order to support their own. First, they gather together certain absurd inferences by which they try to bring odium on our doctrine, as if it were repugnant to common sense; and then they oppose it with certain passages of Scripture (below, section 6). We shall dispose of both devices in their order. If sin, they say, is necessary, it ceases to be sin; if it is voluntary, it can be avoided. These, too, were the weapons with which Pelagius attacked Augustine. But we do not wish to crush them by the weight of his name until we have satisfactorily disposed of the objections themselves. I deny, therefore, that sin ought to be the less imputed because it is necessary; and, on the other hand, I deny the inference that sin can be avoided because it is voluntary. If anyone wishes to dispute with God and to evade his judgment by pretending that he could not have done otherwise, the answer already given is sufficient: it is owing not to creation but to the corruption of nature that man has become the slave of sin and can will nothing but evil. For where does that impotence come from, which the wicked so readily use as an excuse, except from the fact that Adam voluntarily subjected himself to the tyranny of the devil? Hence the corruption by which we are held bound as with chains had its origin in the first man's revolt from his Maker. If all men are justly held guilty of this revolt, let them not think themselves excused by a necessity in which they see the clearest cause of their condemnation. But I have fully explained this above; and in the case of the devil himself I have given an example of one who sins no less voluntarily than he sins necessarily. I have also shown, in the case of the elect angels, that though their will cannot turn aside from good, it does not therefore cease to be will. Bernard shrewdly explains this when he says (Serm. 81, in Cantica) that we are the more miserable in this, that the necessity is voluntary; and yet this necessity so binds us who are subject to it that we are the slaves of sin, as we have already observed. The second step in the reasoning is faulty, because it leaps from voluntary to free; whereas we have proved above that a thing may be done voluntarily even though it is not subject to free choice.

2¶

They add that unless virtue and vice proceed from free choice, it is absurd either to punish man or to reward him. Although this argument is taken from Aristotle, I admit that it is also used by Chrysostom and Jerome. Jerome, however, does not hide the fact that it was familiar to the Pelagians. He even quotes their words: "If grace acts in us, grace, and not we who do the work, will be crowned" (Hieron. in Ep. ad Ctesiphont. et Dialog. 1). With regard to punishment, I answer that it is properly inflicted on those by whom the guilt is incurred. What does it matter whether you sin with a free or an enslaved judgment, so long as you sin voluntarily, especially when man is proved to be a sinner precisely because he is under the bondage of sin? With regard to the rewards of righteousness, is there any great absurdity in acknowledging that they depend on the kindness of God rather than on our own merits? How often we meet in Augustine this expression: "God crowns not our merits but his own gifts; and the name of reward is given not to what is due to our merits, but to the recompense of grace previously bestowed." Some seem to think it a clever remark that there is no place at all for the mind if good works do not spring from free will as their proper source; but in thinking this so very unreasonable they are greatly mistaken. Augustine does not hesitate to describe consistently as necessary the very thing which they count it impious to acknowledge. Thus he asks, "What is human merit? He who came to bestow not a due recompense but free grace, though himself free from sin and the giver of freedom, found all men sinners" (Augustin. in Psal. 31). Again: "If you are to receive what is due to you, you must be punished. What then is done? God has not rendered to you the punishment that is due, but bestows on you unmerited grace. If you wish to be a stranger to grace, boast of your merits" (in Psal. 70). Again: "You are nothing in yourself; sin is yours, merit is God's. Punishment is your due; and when the reward comes, God will crown his own gifts, not your merits" (Ep. 52). To the same effect he says elsewhere (De Verb. Apostol. Serm. 15) that grace is not of merit, but merit of grace. And shortly afterward he concludes that God by his gifts anticipates all our merit, so that he may thereby display his own merit and give what is absolutely free, because he sees nothing in us that can be a ground of salvation. But why lengthen the list of quotations, when similar statements recur again and again in his works? The defenders of this error would see a still better refutation of it if they would attend to the source from which the apostle derives the glory of the saints: "Moreover, whom he did predestinate, them he also called; and whom he called, them he also justified; and whom he justified, them he also glorified" (Romans 8:30). On what ground, then, with the apostle as judge (2 Timothy 4:8), are believers crowned? Because by the mercy of God, not by their own exertions, they are predestinated, called, and justified. Away, then, with the empty fear that unless free will stands there will no longer be any merit! It is most foolish to take alarm and shrink back from what Scripture teaches. "If you received it, why do you glory as if you had not received it?" (1 Corinthians 4:7). You see how everything is denied to free will for the very purpose of leaving no room for merit. And yet, since the kindness and liberality of God are manifold and inexhaustible, the grace which he bestows on us he rewards, inasmuch as he makes it our own, as if the virtuous acts were our own.

3¶

But it is added, in terms which seem to be borrowed from Chrysostom (Homil. 22, in Genes.), that if our will does not possess the power of choosing good or evil, all who share the same nature must be alike good or alike bad. A view akin to this appears in the work De Vocatione Gentium (lib. 4 c. 4), usually attributed to Ambrose, where it is argued that no one would ever fall away from faith if the grace of God did not leave us in a changeable state. It is strange that such men should have blundered so badly. How did it not occur to Chrysostom that it is divine election which distinguishes among men? We have not the least hesitation in admitting what Paul strenuously maintains, that all without exception are depraved and given over to wickedness; but at the same time we add that, through the mercy of God, not all remain in wickedness. Therefore, while we all naturally labor under the same disease, only those recover health to whom the Lord is pleased to stretch out his healing hand. The others, whom he passes over in just judgment, waste away and rot until they are consumed. And this is the only reason why some persevere to the end, while others, after beginning their course, fall away. Perseverance is the gift of God, which he does not lavish indiscriminately on all, but imparts to whom he pleases. If it is asked how the difference arises, why some steadily persevere and others fail in steadfastness, we can give no other reason than this: the Lord by his mighty power strengthens and sustains the former, so that they do not perish, while he does not supply the same help to the latter, but leaves them to be monuments of instability.

4¶

Still they insist that exhortations are vain, warnings superfluous, and rebukes absurd, if the sinner does not have the power to obey. When similar objections were urged against Augustine, he was obliged to write his book De Correptione et Gratia, in which he has fully disposed of them. The substance of his answer to his opponents is this: "O man, learn from the precept what you ought to do; learn from rebuke that it is your own fault that you do not have the power; and learn in prayer where you may receive the power." Very similar is the argument of his book De Spiritu et Litera, in which he shows that God does not measure the precepts of his law by human strength, but, after commanding what is right, freely gives his elect the power to fulfill it. The subject, indeed, does not require a long discussion. For we do not stand alone in our doctrine; we have Christ and all his apostles with us. Let our opponents consider, then, how they are to come off victorious in a contest which they wage against such adversaries. Christ declares, "Without me ye can do nothing" (John 15:5). Does he therefore any the less censure and chastise those who, without him, did wickedly? Does he any the less exhort every man to be intent on good works? How severely Paul rebukes the Corinthians for their lack of love (1 Corinthians 3:3); and yet at the same time he prays that love may be given them by the Lord. In the Epistle to the Romans he declares that "it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that showeth mercy" (Romans 9:16). Still he does not cease to warn, exhort, and rebuke them. Why then do they not remonstrate with God for making sport of men, by demanding of them things which he alone can give, and chastising them for faults committed through lack of his grace? Why do they not advise Paul to spare those who do not have it in their power to will or to run unless the mercy of God, which has forsaken them, goes before? As if the doctrine were not founded on the strongest reason, a reason which no serious inquirer can fail to see. How far doctrine, exhortation, and rebuke are in themselves able to change the mind, Paul indicates when he says, "Neither is he that planteth any thing, neither he that watereth; but God that giveth the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:7). In the same way we see that Moses delivers the precepts of the Law under a heavy sanction, and that the prophets strongly urge and threaten transgressors, though at the same time they confess that men are wise only when an understanding heart is given them; that it is the proper work of God to circumcise the heart and to change it from stone into flesh; to write his law on their inward parts; in short, to renew souls so as to give efficacy to doctrine.

5¶

What purpose, then, do exhortations serve? It is this: since the wicked despise them with obstinate hearts, they will be a testimony against them when they stand before the judgment seat of God; indeed, even now they strike and lash their consciences. For however insolently they may deride them, they cannot disapprove of them. But what, you will ask, can a miserable mortal do, when the softness of heart that is necessary to obedience is denied him? I ask in reply, why resort to evasion, since hardness of heart cannot be imputed to anyone but the sinner himself? The ungodly, though they would gladly escape the divine admonitions, are forced, whether they will or not, to feel their power. But their chief use is to be seen in the case of believers, in whom the Lord, while he always acts by his Spirit, also does not neglect the instrument of his word, but uses it, and not without effect. Let this, then, stand as a settled truth: the whole strength of the godly consists in the grace of God, according to the words of the prophet, "I will give them one heart, and I will put a new spirit within you; and I will take the stony heart out of their flesh, and will give them an heart of flesh, that they may walk in my statutes" (Ezekiel 11:19, 20). But it will be asked, why are they now admonished of their duty, and not rather left to the guidance of the Spirit? Why are they urged with exhortations when they cannot hasten any faster than the Spirit impels them? And why are they chastised if at any time they go astray, seeing that this is caused by the necessary weakness of the flesh? "O man, who art thou that repliest against God?" If, in order to prepare us for the grace which enables us to obey exhortation, God sees fit to use exhortation, what is there in such an arrangement for you to carp at and scoff at? If exhortations and reprimands had no other profit for the godly than to convince them of sin, they could not be thought altogether useless. But now, when by the Spirit of God working within they have the effect of kindling their desire for good, of rousing them from lethargy, of destroying the pleasure and honeyed sweetness of sin and making it hateful and loathsome, who will presume to cavil at them as superfluous?

Should anyone want a clearer reply, let him take the following. God works in his elect in two ways: inwardly, by his Spirit; outwardly, by his Word. By his Spirit, enlightening their minds and training their hearts to the practice of righteousness, he makes them new creatures; by his Word, he stirs them to long for and seek this renewal. In both he exerts the might of his hand in proportion to the measure in which he dispenses them. The Word, when addressed to the reprobate, though not effectual for their amendment, has another use. It presses on their consciences now, and will render them more inexcusable on the day of judgment. Thus our Savior, while declaring that none can come to him except those whom the Father draws, and that the elect come after they have heard and learned from the Father (John 6:44, 45), does not lay aside the office of teacher, but carefully invites those who must be taught inwardly by the Spirit before they can make any progress. As for the reprobate, Paul declares that the doctrine is not in vain with them, because while it is to them a savor of death unto death, it is still a sweet savor unto God (2 Corinthians 2:16).

6¶

The enemies of this doctrine take great pains to collect passages of Scripture, as if, unable to accomplish anything by their weight, they meant to overwhelm us by their number. But as in battle, when it comes to close quarters, an unwarlike multitude, however great the pomp and show they make, gives way after a few blows and takes to flight, The French is, "Mais c'est comme si un capitaine assembloit force gens qui ne fussent nullement duits ˆ la guerre pour espouvanter son ennemi. Avant que les mettre en œuvre, il feroient grande monstre; mais s'il faloit venir en bataille et joindre eontre son ennemi on les feroit fuir du premier coup." But it is as if a captain were to assemble a large body of people, in no way trained for war, to astonish the enemy. Before coming into action they would make a great show; but if they had to go into battle and come to close quarters with the enemy, the first stroke would put them to flight.

All the precepts which they heap together in this way may be divided into three classes. Some enjoin a first conversion to God, others speak simply of the keeping of the law, and others urge perseverance in the grace which has been received. We shall first treat of precepts in general, and then go on to consider each class separately. That the abilities of man are equal to the precepts of the divine law has long been a common idea, and it has some show of plausibility. It is founded, however, on the grossest ignorance of the law. Those who think it a kind of sacrilege to say that the keeping of the law is impossible insist, as their strongest argument, that if this is so, the Law has been given in vain (below, chapter 7, section 5). For they speak just as if Paul had never said anything about the Law. But what, I ask, is meant by saying that the Law "was added because of transgressions"; "by the law is the knowledge of sin"; "I had not known sin but by the law"; "the law entered that the offence might abound"? (Galatians 3:19; Romans 3:20; 7:7; 5:20). Is it meant that the Law was to be limited to our strength, so that it might not be given in vain? Is it not rather meant that it was placed far above us, in order to convince us of our utter weakness? Paul indeed declares that love is the end and fulfilling of the Law (1 Timothy 1:5). But when he prays that the minds of the Thessalonians may be filled with it, he plainly enough acknowledges that the Law sounds in our ears without profit unless God implants it thoroughly in our hearts (1 Thessalonians 3:12).

7¶

I admit, indeed, that if Scripture taught nothing else on the subject than that the Law is a rule of life by which we ought to regulate our pursuits, I would at once agree with their opinion. But since it carefully and clearly explains that the use of the Law is manifold, the proper course is to learn from that explanation what the power of the Law is in man. With regard to the present question, while it explains what our duty is, it teaches that the power of obeying comes from the goodness of God, and it accordingly urges us to pray that this power may be given us. If there were merely a command and no promise, it would be necessary to test whether our strength were sufficient to fulfill the command; but since promises are attached which proclaim that not only our help but our whole power comes from divine grace, they at the same time abundantly testify that we are not only unequal to the keeping of the Law, but mere fools in regard to it. Therefore let us hear no more of a proportion between our ability and the divine precepts, as if the Lord had adjusted the standard of justice which he was to give in the Law to our feeble capacities. We should rather gather from the promises how ill provided we are, since we need grace so greatly in everything. But, they say, who will believe that the Lord designed his Law for blocks and stones? No one wishes to make anyone believe this. The ungodly are neither blocks nor stones when, taught by the Law that their lusts are offensive to God, they are proved guilty by their own confession; nor are the godly blocks or stones when, admonished of their powerlessness, they take refuge in grace. To this effect are the pithy sayings of Augustine: "God commands what we cannot do, that we may know what we ought to ask of him. There is great usefulness in precepts, if all that is given to free will is to do greater honor to divine grace. Faith obtains what the Law requires; indeed, the Law requires in order that faith may obtain what is thus required; indeed, more than this, God demands of us faith itself, and does not find what he demands until by giving he makes it possible to find it." Again he says, "Let God give what he commands, and command what he wills." August. Enchir. ad Laurent. de Gratia et Liber. Arbit. cap. 16. Homil 29, in Joann. Ep. 24.

8¶

This will be seen more clearly by attending again to the three classes of precepts to which we referred above. Both in the Law and in the Prophets, God repeatedly calls on us to turn to him. Joel 2:12; Jeremiah 31:18; Deuteronomy 10:16; 30:6; Ezekiel 36:26; Jeremiah 31:18. Vid. Calvin. adv. Pighium.

9¶

Some, who would like to be thought more clever, try to evade all these passages by the quibble that nothing prevents us from contributing our part while God at the same time supplies what we lack. They also bring forward passages from the Prophets in which the work of our conversion seems to be shared between God and ourselves: "Turn ye unto me, saith the Lord of hosts, and I will turn unto you, saith the Lord of hosts" (Zechariah 1:3). The kind of help which God gives us has been shown above (sections 7, 8), and need not be repeated now. I ask only that one thing be conceded to me: it is vain to think that we have a power of fulfilling the Law merely because we are commanded to obey it. Since the grace of the Lawgiver is both necessary for our fulfilling the divine precepts and has been promised to us, this much at least is clear, that more is demanded of us than we are able to pay. Nor can any cavil evade the declaration in Jeremiah that the covenant which God made with his ancient people was broken because it was only of the letter, and that to make it effectual it was necessary for the Spirit to intervene and train the heart to obedience (Jeremiah 31:32). The opinion we are now opposing gains no support from the words, "Turn unto me, and I will turn unto you." The turning spoken of there is not that by which God renews the heart to repentance, but that in which, by bestowing prosperity, he displays his kindness and favor, just as he sometimes expresses his displeasure by sending adversity. When the people complained, under the many calamities that had come upon them, that they were forsaken by God, he answers that his kindness would not fail them if they would return to a right course, and to himself, the standard of righteousness. The passage, therefore, is wrenched from its proper meaning when it is made to support the idea that the work of conversion is divided between God and man (above, chapter 2, section 27). We have only glanced briefly at this subject, since the proper place for it will come when we treat of the Law (chapter 7, sections 2 and 3).

10¶

The second class of objections is akin to the first. They bring forward the promises in which the Lord makes a compact with our will. Such are the following: "Seek good, and not evil, that ye may live" (Amos 5:14). "If ye be willing and obedient, ye shall eat the good of the land: but if ye refuse and rebel, ye shall be devoured with the sword; for the mouth of the Lord has spoken it" (Isaiah 1:19, 20). "If thou wilt put away thine abominations out of my sight, then thou shalt not remove" (Jeremiah 4:1). "It shall come to pass, if thou shalt hearken diligently unto the voice of the Lord thy God, to observe and do all the commandments which I command thee this day, that the Lord thy God will set thee on high above all nations of the earth" (Deuteronomy 28:1). There are other similar passages (Leviticus 26:3, etc.). They think that the blessings contained in these promises are offered to our will absurdly and in mockery if it is not in our power to secure or reject them. It is, indeed, an easy thing to indulge in rhetorical complaint on this subject, and to say that we are cruelly mocked The French is, "Et de fait cette raison a grande apparence humainement. Car on peut deduire gue ce seroit une cruauté de Dieu," etc. And, in fact, humanly speaking, there is great plausibility in this argument. For it may be maintained that it would be cruelty in God, etc.

The French adds, "Veu qu'en cela il fait le profit de ses serviteurs et rend les iniques plus damnables;" seeing that by this he promotes the good of his servants, and renders the wicked more deserving of condemnation.

11¶

The third class of objections is not unlike the other two. For they bring forward passages in which God reproaches his people for their ingratitude, implying that it was not his fault that they did not obtain every kind of favor from his kindness. The following are examples of such passages: "The Amalekites and the Canaanites are before you, and ye shall fall by the sword: because ye are turned away from the Lord, therefore the Lord will not be with you" (Numbers 14:43). "Because ye have done all these works, saith the Lord, and I spake unto you, rising up early and speaking, but ye heard not; and I called you, but ye answered not; therefore will I do unto this house, which is called by my name, wherein ye trust, and unto the place which I gave to you and to your fathers, as I have done to Shiloh" (Jeremiah 7:13, 14). "They obeyed not thy voice, neither walked in thy law; they have done nothing of all that thou commandedst them to do: therefore thou hast caused all this evil to come upon them" (Jeremiah 32:23). How, they ask, can such reproaches be directed against those who have it in their power to reply at once: Prosperity was dear to us, and we feared adversity; that we did not obey the Lord and listen to his voice in order to obtain the one and avoid the other is owing to the fact that we were not free to do so, because of our subjection to the dominion of sin; it is in vain, therefore, that we are reproached with evils which it was not in our power to escape? But to say nothing of the pretext of necessity, which is only a feeble and flimsy defense of their conduct, can they, I ask, deny their guilt? If they are held convicted of any fault, the Lord is not unjust in reproaching them for having deprived themselves, by their own perverseness, of the benefits of his kindness. Let them say, then, whether they can deny that their own will is the depraved cause of their rebellion. If they find within themselves a fountain of wickedness, why do they stand declaiming about outside causes, in order to make it appear that they are not the authors of their own destruction? If it is true that sinners are both deprived of the divine favor and visited with punishment for no one's faults but their own, there is good reason why they should hear these rebukes from the mouth of God. If they obstinately persist in their vices, let them learn in their calamities to accuse and detest their own wickedness, instead of charging God with cruelty and injustice. If they have not shown themselves teachable, let them, disgusted with the sins which they see to be the cause of their misery and ruin, return to the right path, and with serious contrition confess the very thing of which the Lord reminds them by his rebuke. Of what use those reproaches of the prophets quoted above are to believers appears from the solemn prayer of Daniel, as given in his ninth chapter. Of their use with regard to the ungodly, we see an example in the Jews, to whom Jeremiah was ordered to explain the cause of their miseries, though the outcome could not be otherwise than the Lord had foretold. "Therefore thou shalt speak these words unto them; but they will not hearken unto thee: thou shalt also call unto them; but they will not answer thee" (Jeremiah 7:27). Of what use, then, was it to speak to the deaf? It was so that, even against their will, they might understand that what they heard was true, and that it was impious blasphemy to shift the blame for their wickedness onto God when it lay in themselves.

These few explanations will make it very easy for the reader to free himself from the immense heap of passages (containing both precepts and reproofs) which the enemies of divine grace are accustomed to pile up, in order to erect their statue of free will upon it. The Psalmist reproaches the Jews as "a stubborn and rebellious generation; a generation that set not their heart aright" (Psalm 78:8); and in another passage he exhorts the men of his time, "Harden not your heart" (Psalm 95:8). This implies that the whole blame for the rebellion lies in human depravity. But it is foolish to infer from this that the heart, whose preparation is from the Lord, can be bent equally in either direction. The Psalmist says, "I have inclined my heart to perform thy statutes alway" (Psalm 119:112), meaning that with a willing and cheerful readiness of mind he had devoted himself to God. He does not boast, however, that he was the author of that disposition, for in the same psalm he acknowledges it to be the gift of God. We must, therefore, attend to the admonition of Paul when he addresses believers in this way: "Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure" (Philippians 2:12, 13). He assigns them a part in acting so that they may not indulge in carnal sloth; but by enjoining fear and trembling he humbles them, so as to keep them mindful that the very thing they are ordered to do is the proper work of God. He thereby plainly indicates that believers act (if I may so speak) passively, inasmuch as the power is given them from heaven and cannot in any way be claimed for themselves. Accordingly, when Peter exhorts us to "add to faith virtue" (2 Peter 1:5), he does not grant us a second place, as if we could do anything on our own. He only rouses the sluggishness of our flesh, by which faith itself is often stifled. The words of Paul are to the same effect. He says, "Quench not the Spirit" (1 Thessalonians 5:19), because a spirit of sloth, if not guarded against, is continually creeping in upon believers. But should anyone infer from this that it is entirely in their own power to cherish the light offered them, his ignorance will easily be refuted by the fact that the very diligence which Paul enjoins comes only from God (2 Corinthians 7:1). We are often commanded to cleanse ourselves of all impurity, though the Spirit claims this as his own peculiar office. In short, that what properly belongs to God is transferred to us only by way of concession is plain from the words of John: "He that is begotten of God keepeth himself" (1 John 5:18). The advocates of free will seize on the expression as if it implied that we are kept partly by the power of God and partly by our own, whereas the very keeping of which the Apostle speaks is itself from heaven. Hence Christ prays his Father to keep us from evil (John 17:15), and we know that believers, in their warfare against Satan, owe their victory to the armor of God. Accordingly Peter, after saying, "Ye have purified your souls in obeying the truth," immediately adds by way of correction, "through the Spirit" (1 Peter 1:22). In short, the nothingness of human strength in the spiritual contest is briefly shown by John when he says that "Whosoever is born of God does not commit sin; for his seed remaineth in him" (1 John 3:9). Elsewhere he gives the reason: "This is the victory that overcometh the world, even our faith" (1 John 5:4).

12¶

But a passage is brought forward from the Law of Moses which seems very much opposed to the view now given. After proclaiming the Law, he calls the people to witness in these terms: "This commandment which I command thee this day, it is not hidden from thee, neither is it far off. It is not in heaven, that thou shouldest say, Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us, that we may hear it, and do it? But the word is very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayest do it" (Deuteronomy 30:11, 12, 14). Certainly, if this is to be understood of mere precepts, I admit that it is of no small importance to the matter at hand. For though it would be easy to evade the difficulty by saying that the thing treated here is not the keeping of the law but the ease and readiness with which one may come to know it, some scruple would perhaps still remain. The Apostle Paul, however, no mean interpreter, removes all doubt when he affirms that Moses here spoke of the doctrine of the Gospel (Romans 10:8). If anyone is so stubborn as to contend that Paul forcibly wrenched the words in applying them to the Gospel, though his boldness is chargeable with impiety, we are still able, apart from the authority of the Apostle, to repel the objection. For if Moses spoke merely of precepts, he was only puffing up the people with vain confidence. Had they attempted the keeping of the law in their own strength, as a matter in which they would find no difficulty, what else could have resulted than to hurl them headlong? Where, then, was that easy means of keeping the law, when the only access to it lay over a fatal precipice? The French is "Où est-ce que sera cette facilité, veu que notre natute succombe en cet endroit, et n'y a celui qui ne trebusche voulant marcher?" Where is this ease, seeing that our nature here gives way, and there is not a man who does not stumble when he tries to walk?

13¶

They are accustomed also to bring forward certain passages in which God is said at times to test men by withdrawing the help of his grace, and to wait until they turn to him, as in Hosea: "I will go and return to my place, till they acknowledge their offence, and seek my face" (Hosea 5:15). It would be absurd, they say, for the Lord to wait until Israel should seek his face, if their minds were not flexible, so as to turn in either direction of their own accord. As if anything were more common in the prophetic writings than for God to take on the appearance of rejecting and casting off his people until they reform their lives! But what can our opponents extract from such threats? If they mean to maintain that a people abandoned by God are able of themselves to think of turning to him, they will be doing so in the very face of Scripture. On the other hand, if they admit that divine grace is necessary for conversion, why do they argue with us? But while they admit that grace is necessary to that extent, they insist on reserving some ability for man. How do they prove it? Certainly not from this or any similar passage; for it is one thing to withdraw from man and watch what he will do when thus abandoned and left to himself, and quite another to assist his powers (whatever they may be) in proportion to their weakness. What, then, it will be asked, is meant by such expressions? I answer, just the same as if God were to say: Since nothing is gained by admonishing, exhorting, and rebuking this stubborn people, I will withdraw for a little while and silently leave them to be afflicted; I shall see whether, after long calamity, any remembrance of me will return and lead them to seek my face. But by the Lord's departure to a distance is meant the withdrawal of prophecy. By his waiting to see what men will do is meant that, while he is silent and in a manner hides himself, he tests them for a season with various afflictions. He does both in order to humble us the more; for we shall sooner be broken than corrected by the blows of adversity unless his Spirit trains us to be teachable. Moreover, when the Lord, offended and, as it were, wearied with our obstinate perverseness, leaves us for a while (by withdrawing his word, in which he is accustomed to display his presence in some degree), and tests what we will do in his absence, it is wrongly inferred from this that there is some power of free will whose extent is to be examined and tested; whereas the only end he has in view is to bring us to acknowledge our utter nothingness.

14¶

Another objection is founded on a way of speaking that is constantly observed both in Scripture and in ordinary speech. God's works are said to be ours, and we are said to do what is holy and acceptable to God, just as we are said to commit sin. But if sins are justly imputed to us as proceeding from ourselves, then for the same reason, they say, some share must certainly be attributed to us in works of righteousness. It would not be consistent with reason to say that we do things which we are incapable of doing by our own motion, God moving us as if we were stones. These expressions, therefore, they say, indicate that while in the matter of grace we give the first place to God, a secondary place must be assigned to our own agency. If the only thing insisted on here were that good works are called ours, I in my turn would reply that the bread which we ask God to give us is also called ours. What, then, can be inferred from the title of possession, except simply that by the kindness and free gift of God that becomes ours which in other respects is by no means due to us? Therefore let them either ridicule the same absurdity in the Lord's Prayer, or let them stop regarding it as absurd that good works should be called ours, though our only ownership of them comes from the liberality of God. But there is something stronger in the fact that we are often said in Scripture to worship God, to do justice, to obey the law, and to follow after good works. Since these are proper functions of the mind and will, how can they consistently be referred to the Spirit and at the same time attributed to us, unless there is some concurrence on our part with the divine agency? This difficulty will be easily disposed of if we attend to the way in which the Holy Spirit acts in the righteous. The comparison with which they spitefully attack us is beside the point; for who is so absurd as to imagine that movement in man differs in nothing from the push given to a stone? Nor can anything of the kind be inferred from our doctrine. To the natural powers of man we ascribe approving and rejecting, willing and not willing, striving and resisting; namely, approving vanity, rejecting solid good, willing evil and not willing good, striving for wickedness and resisting righteousness. What then does the Lord do? If he sees fit to use depravity of this kind as an instrument of his anger, he gives it whatever aim and direction he pleases, so that by a guilty hand he may accomplish his own good work. When a wicked man thus serves the power of God while he is bent only on following his own lust, can we compare him to a stone, which, driven by an outside force, is carried along without motion or sense or will of its own? We see how wide the difference is. But how does the case stand with the godly, about whom the question is chiefly raised? When God sets up his kingdom in them, he by his Spirit curbs their will, so that it may not follow its natural bent and be carried here and there by wandering lusts; he bends, shapes, trains, and guides it according to the rule of his justice, so as to incline it to righteousness and holiness; and he establishes and strengthens it by the power of his Spirit, so that it may not stumble or fall. For this reason Augustine expresses himself in this way (De Corrept. et Gratia, cap. 2): "It will be said that we are therefore acted upon, and do not act. No, you both act and are acted upon, and you act well when you are acted upon by one who is good. The Spirit of God who moves you is your helper in acting, and bears the name of helper because you, too, do something." In the first part of this sentence he reminds us that the agency of man is not destroyed by the motion of the Holy Spirit, because nature supplies the will, which is guided so as to aspire to good. As to the second part of the sentence, in which he says that the very idea of help implies that we also do something, we must not understand it as if he were attributing to us some independent power of action; rather, so as not to encourage a spirit of sloth, he reconciles the agency of God with our own agency by saying that to will is from nature, to will well is from grace. Accordingly he had said a little before, "If God did not help us, we would not only be unable to conquer, but unable even to fight."

15¶

Hence it appears that the grace of God (as this name is used when regeneration is spoken of) is the rule of the Spirit in directing and governing the human will. He cannot govern it without correcting, reforming, and renewing it (which is why we say that the beginning of regeneration consists in the abolition of what is ours); in the same way, he cannot govern it without moving, impelling, urging, and restraining it. Accordingly, all the actions which are done afterward are truly said to be wholly his. Meanwhile, we do not deny the truth of Augustine's doctrine that the will is not destroyed but rather repaired by grace. The two things are perfectly consistent: namely, that the human will may be said to be renewed when, its corruption and perverseness having been corrected, it is conformed to the true standard of righteousness; and that, at the same time, the will may be said to be made new, since it is so vitiated and corrupted that its nature must be entirely changed. There is nothing, then, to prevent us from saying that our will does what the Spirit does in us, although the will contributes nothing of itself apart from grace. We must, therefore, remember what we quoted from Augustine, that some men labor in vain to find in the human will some good quality properly belonging to it. Any mixture which men try to make by joining the effort of their own will with divine grace is a corruption, just as when unwholesome and muddy water is used to dilute wine. But though everything good in the will is derived entirely from the influence of the Spirit, yet because we naturally have an innate power of willing, we are not improperly said to do the things for which God claims all the praise for himself: first, because everything which his kindness produces in us is our own (only we must understand that it is not of ourselves); and second, because it is our mind, our will, our effort that are guided by him to what is good.

16¶

The other passages which they gather together from various places will not give much trouble to anyone of reasonable understanding who pays due attention to the explanations already given. They bring forward the passage in Genesis, "Unto thee shall be his desire, and thou shalt rule over him" (Genesis 4:7). This they interpret of sin, as if the Lord were promising Cain that the dominion of sin would not prevail over his mind if he would labor to subdue it. We, however, maintain that it agrees much better with the context to understand the words as referring to Abel, since God's purpose there is to point out the injustice of the envy which Cain had conceived against his brother. And he does this in two ways: by showing, first, that it was vain to think that he could surpass his brother in the favor of God by means of wickedness, since God esteems nothing but righteousness; and second, how ungrateful he was for the kindness he had already received, in being unable to bear with a brother who had been placed under his authority. But lest it be thought that we adopt this interpretation because the other is contrary to our view, let us grant that God does speak here of sin. If so, his words contain either a command or a promise. If a command, we have already shown that this is no proof of man's ability; if a promise, where is the fulfillment of the promise, when Cain gave way to the sin over which he ought to have prevailed? They will allege that a tacit condition is implied in the promise, as if it were said that he would gain the victory if he fought. This subterfuge is altogether useless. For if the dominion spoken of refers to sin, no one can doubt that the form of expression is imperative, declaring not what we are able to do but what it is our duty to do, even if it is beyond our ability. Although both the nature of the case and the rule of grammar require that it be regarded as a comparison between Cain and Abel, we think the only preference given to the younger brother was that the elder made himself inferior by his own wickedness.

17¶

They appeal, moreover, to the testimony of the Apostle Paul, because he says, "It is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that showeth mercy" (Romans 9:15). From this they infer that there is something in will and effort which, though weak in themselves, are still not without some measure of success when mercifully aided by God. But if they would soberly attend to the subject Paul is treating there, they would not so rashly pervert his meaning. I am aware that they can quote Origen and Jerome Orig. Lig. 7 in Epist. ad Rom. Hieron. Dial. i in Pelagium. For the passage in Augustine, see the extract in Book 3, chapter 24, section 1.

18¶

They appeal also to Ecclesiasticus, who is well known to be a writer of doubtful authority. But though we might justly decline his testimony, let us see what he says in support of free will. His words are: "He himself made man from the beginning, and left him in the hand of his counsel; If thou wilt, to keep the commandments, and perform acceptable faithfulness. He has set fire and water before thee: stretch forth thy hand unto whether thou wilt. Before man is life and death; and whether him liketh shall be given him" (Ecclesiasticus 15:14–17). Grant that man received at his creation a power of attaining life or death; what then, if we in turn can reply that he has lost it? Certainly I have no intention of contradicting Solomon, who asserts that "God has made man upright" and that "they have sought out many inventions" (Ecclesiastes 7:29). But since man, by degenerating, has made shipwreck of himself and all his blessings, it certainly does not follow that everything attributed to his nature as originally constituted applies to it now that it is vitiated and degenerate. Therefore this is my answer, not only to my opponents but to the author of Ecclesiasticus himself, whoever he may have been: If you mean to tell man that there is in himself a power of attaining salvation, your authority with us is not so great as to prejudice in the least degree the undoubted word of God. But if, wishing only to curb the malice of the flesh, which by shifting the blame for its own wickedness onto God is accustomed to snatch at a vain defense, you say that uprightness was given to man in order to make it plain that he was the cause of his own destruction, I willingly agree. Only agree with me in this, that it is by his own fault that he is stripped of the ornaments with which the Lord at first clothed him; and then let us unite in acknowledging that what he now needs is a physician, and not a defender.

19¶

Nothing is more frequently on their lips than the parable of the traveler who fell among thieves and was left half dead (Luke 10:32). I am aware that it is a common idea with almost all writers that the calamity of the human race is represented under the figure of the traveler. Hence our opponents argue that man was not so mutilated by the robbery of sin and the devil as not to retain some remnants of his former endowments, because it is said that he was left half dead. For where is the half that lives, unless some portion of right will and reason remains? First, if I were to deny that there is any room for their allegory, what could they say? There can be no doubt that the Fathers invented it contrary to the genuine sense of the parable. Allegories ought to be carried no further than Scripture expressly sanctions; so far are they from forming a sufficient basis on which to found doctrines. And if I were so inclined, I could easily find the means of tearing this fiction up by the roots. The Word of God leaves no half life to man, but teaches that, with respect to life and happiness, he has utterly perished. Paul, when he speaks of our redemption, does not say that the half dead are cured (Ephesians 2:5, 6; 5:14), but that those who were dead are raised up. He does not call on the half dead to receive the light of Christ, but on those who are asleep and buried. In the same way our Lord himself says, "The hour is coming, and now is, when the dead shall hear the voice of the Son of God" (John 5:25). How can they presume to set up a flimsy allegory against so many clear statements? But suppose this allegory is good evidence: what can they wring out of it? Man is half dead; therefore there is some soundness in him. True! He has a mind capable of understanding, though incapable of attaining heavenly and spiritual wisdom; he has some discernment of what is honorable; he has some sense of the Divinity, though he cannot reach the true knowledge of God. But what do these amount to? They certainly do not refute the doctrine of Augustine, a doctrine confirmed by the common vote even of the Schoolmen, that after the fall the free gifts on which salvation depends were withdrawn, and the natural gifts corrupted and defiled (above, chapter 2, section 2). Let it stand, therefore, as an indubitable truth which no siege engines can shake, that the mind of man is so entirely alienated from the righteousness of God that he cannot conceive, desire, or plan anything but what is wicked, distorted, foul, impure, and iniquitous; that his heart is so thoroughly poisoned by sin that it can breathe out nothing but corruption and rottenness; and that if some men at times make a show of goodness, their mind is always interwoven with hypocrisy and deceit, and their soul inwardly bound with the fetters of wickedness.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Patiently and without flinching, Calvin dismantles every argument raised to rescue free will — the supposed absurdities, the proof-texts wrested from their meaning, the appeals to exhortation and reward as if commands proved our power to obey. They do no such thing; the law shows us our duty and our inability together, and grace must supply what nature cannot. For a generation in 2026 weaned on the conviction that to demand is to enable, this is bracing correction: God's commands measure our obligation, not our capacity, and our failure drives us not to self-effort but to the Spirit. This is no excuse for sloth — the believer strives precisely because God works in him to will and to do. Teach your household to hear every command of Scripture as both a summons and a confession of need, so that obedience flows from grace received and never from a will imagined to be free. We work hard, knowing the working is itself his gift.
