It now remains to treat, in this last Book, of the Church and the Communion of Saints, or of the external means or helps by which God invites us to fellowship with Christ, and keeps us in it. 1¶

In the last Book it was shown that by the faith of the gospel Christ becomes ours, and we are made partakers of the salvation and eternal blessedness he has procured. But since our ignorance and sloth (I may add, the vanity of our minds) need outward helps by which faith may be begotten in us, and may grow and make progress until it is brought to completion, God, accommodating himself to our weakness, has added such helps, and has secured the effectual preaching of the gospel by depositing this treasure with the Church. He has appointed pastors and teachers, through whose lips he might build up his people (Ephesians 4:11); he has invested them with authority; and, in short, he has omitted nothing that might promote holy agreement in the faith and right order. In particular, he has instituted sacraments, which we find by experience to be most useful helps in nourishing and confirming our faith. For since we are shut up in the prison of the body and have not yet attained to the rank of angels, God, accommodating himself to our capacity, has in his admirable providence provided a way by which, though we are far removed from him, we might still draw near to him. Therefore due order requires that we treat first of the Church, of its government, orders, and power; next, of the sacraments; and lastly, of civil government — at the same time guarding godly readers against the corruptions of the Papacy, by which Satan has adulterated everything God had appointed for our salvation. I will begin with the Church, into whose bosom God is pleased to gather his children, not only so that by her help and ministry they may be nourished while they are infants and children, but also so that they may be guided by her motherly care until they grow up to manhood and finally reach the perfection of faith. What God has thus joined together, let not man put asunder (Mark 10:9): to those for whom he is a Father, the Church must also be a mother. This was true not only under the Law but also now, after the coming of Christ, since Paul declares that we are the children of a new, indeed a heavenly, Jerusalem (Galatians 4:26).

2¶

When in the Creed we profess to believe the Church, reference is made not only to the visible Church of which we are now treating, but also to all the elect of God, including even those who have departed this life. And for this reason the word used is "believe," because often no difference can be seen between the children of God and the profane, between his own flock and the untamed herd. The particle "in" is often inserted, but without any probable ground. I admit, indeed, that it is the more usual form, and that it is not without support from antiquity, since the Nicene Creed, as quoted in the Ecclesiastical History, adds the preposition. At the same time, we can see from early writers that the expression received without controversy in ancient times was to believe "the Church," and not "in the Church." This is not only the expression used by Augustine and by that ancient writer, whoever he may have been, whose treatise De Symboli Expositione is extant under the name of Cyprian; they also expressly remark that adding the preposition would make the expression improper, and they give good grounds for thinking so. We declare that we believe in God, both because our mind rests on him as true and because our confidence is fully satisfied in him. This cannot be said of the Church, just as it cannot be said of the forgiveness of sins or the resurrection of the body. Therefore, although I am unwilling to dispute about words, I would rather keep to the proper form, as better suited to express the thing meant, than adopt terms by which the meaning is needlessly obscured. The purpose of the expression is to teach us that, though the devil leaves no stone unturned to destroy the grace of Christ, and the enemies of God rush with insane violence in the same direction, that grace cannot be extinguished — the blood of Christ cannot be made barren and prevented from bearing fruit. Hence we must have regard both to the secret election and to the inward calling of God, because he alone "knoweth them that are his" (2 Timothy 2:19); and, as Paul puts it, he holds them as it were enclosed under his seal, although at the same time they wear his insignia and are thus distinguished from the reprobate. But since they are a small and despised number, hidden in an immense crowd, like a few grains of wheat buried in a heap of chaff, the knowledge of his Church must be left to God alone, and his secret election is its foundation. 103 D103 Here Calvin provides a basis for the concept, "the invisible Church." The term "invisible" refers to the fact that a knowledge of those who are truly members of Christ's body is known to God alone and cannot with certainty be ascertained by men (except for themselves, through their effectual calling). (see also section 7)

3¶

Moreover, this article of the Creed relates in some measure to the external Church, so that each of us must maintain brotherly concord with all the children of God, give due authority to the Church, and, in short, conduct ourselves as sheep of the flock. Hence the added expression, the "communion of saints." For this clause, though usually omitted by ancient writers, must not be overlooked, since it admirably expresses the character of the Church; it is as if it had been said that the saints are united in the fellowship of Christ on this condition, that all the blessings God bestows on them are shared with one another. This, however, is not inconsistent with a diversity of graces, for we know that the gifts of the Spirit are variously distributed; nor is it inconsistent with civil order, by which each person is allowed to possess his own goods privately, since distinct rights of property among men are necessary for preserving peace among them. Still, a community is asserted, such as Luke describes when he says, "The multitude of them that believed were of one heart and of one soul" (Acts 4:32); and such as Paul describes when he reminds the Ephesians, "There is one body, and one Spirit, even as ye are called in one hope of your calling" (Ephesians 4:4). For if they are truly persuaded that God is the common Father of them all, and Christ their common head, they cannot help being united in brotherly love and sharing their blessings with one another. It is also of the highest importance for us to know what benefit comes to us from this. For when we believe the Church, it is so that we may be firmly persuaded that we are its members. In this way our salvation rests on a foundation so firm and sure that, though the whole fabric of the world were to give way, it could not be destroyed. First, it stands with the election of God, and cannot change or fail any more than his eternal providence can. Next, it is in a sense joined to the stability of Christ, who will no more allow his faithful followers to be torn away from him than he would allow his own members to be torn to pieces. We may add that, so long as we remain in the bosom of the Church, we are sure that the truth will remain with us. Lastly, we feel that we have a share in such promises as these: "In Mount Zion and in Jerusalem shall be deliverance" (Joel 2:32; Obadiah 17); "God is in the midst of her, she shall not be moved" (Psalm 46:5). So effective is communion with the Church in keeping us in the fellowship of God. In the very word communion there is great consolation; for while we are assured that everything God bestows on his members belongs to us, all the blessings conferred on them confirm our hope. But in order to embrace the unity of the Church in this way, it is not necessary, as I have observed, to see it with our eyes or touch it with our hands. On the contrary, the very fact that it is placed among the things of faith reminds us that our thoughts are to dwell on it as much when it escapes our perception as when it openly appears. Nor is our faith the worse for laying hold of what is unknown, since we are not commanded here to distinguish between the elect and the reprobate (this belongs not to us but to God alone), but to be firmly assured in our minds that all who, by the mercy of God the Father, through the working of the Holy Spirit, have become partakers of Christ, are set apart as God's proper and peculiar possession, and that since we are of that number, we are also partakers of this great grace.

4¶

But since it is now our purpose to discuss the visible Church, 104 D104 Here Calvin employs the term, "the visible Church." (He also calls it "the external Church" in the first sentence of section 3). The expression, "visible Church" refers to the Church as it is seen by the eyes of fallible men. From this standpoint, since only the Lord "knows them that are His," it is possible to have (and sometimes discover) deceived persons and hypocrites in the church—those who are not truly members of Christ's body. This mixed state of affairs forms part of the warrant for Church discipline. (see also section 7)

5¶

But let us proceed to a full exposition of this view. Paul says that our Savior "ascended far above all heavens, that he might fill all things. And he gave some, apostles; and some, prophets; and some, evangelists; and some, pastors and teachers; for the perfecting of the saints, for the work of the ministry, for the edifying of the body of Christ: till we all come in the unity of the faith, and of the knowledge of the Son of God, unto a perfect man, unto the measure of the stature of the fulness of Christ" (Ephesians 4:10-13). We see that God, who could perfect his people in a moment, chooses not to bring them to maturity in any other way than by the education of the Church. We see the means by which this is done expressly stated: the preaching of heavenly doctrine is committed to pastors. We see that all without exception are brought into the same order, so that with a meek and teachable spirit they may allow themselves to be governed by teachers appointed for this purpose. Isaiah had long before given this as the mark of the kingdom of Christ: "My Spirit that is upon thee, and my words which I have put in thy mouth, shall not depart out of thy mouth, nor out of the mouth of thy seed, nor out of the mouth of thy seed's seed, saith the Lord, from henceforth and for ever" (Isaiah 59:21). It follows that all who reject the spiritual food of the soul, divinely offered to them by the hands of the Church, deserve to perish of hunger and famine. God inspires faith in us, but he does it by means of his gospel, as Paul reminds us: "Faith cometh by hearing" (Romans 10:17). God reserves to himself the power of sustaining it, but it is by the preaching of the gospel, as Paul also declares, that he brings it forth and unfolds it. With this in view, it pleased him in ancient times that sacred assemblies should be held in the sanctuary, so that agreement in the faith might be nourished by doctrine coming from the lips of the priest. Those magnificent titles, as when the temple is called God's rest, his sanctuary, his dwelling place, and when he is said to dwell between the cherubim (Psalm 132:13, 14; 80:1), are used for no other purpose than to win respect, love, reverence, and dignity for the ministry of heavenly doctrine, which the appearance of an insignificant human being might otherwise greatly diminish. Therefore, to teach us that the treasure offered to us in earthen vessels is of priceless value (2 Corinthians 4:7), God himself appears and, as the author of this ordinance, requires that his presence be recognized in his own institution. Accordingly, after forbidding his people to give heed to familiar spirits, wizards, and other superstitions (Leviticus 19:30, 31), he adds that he will give what ought to be sufficient for all — namely, that he will never leave them without prophets. For as he did not commit his ancient people to angels, but raised up teachers on the earth to perform a truly angelic office, so he is pleased to instruct us today by human means. And as in ancient times he did not confine himself to the Law alone, but added priests as interpreters, from whose lips the people might ask his true meaning, so today he would not only have us be attentive to reading, but has appointed masters to give us their help. In this there is a twofold advantage. For on the one hand, he tests our obedience by an admirable proof when we listen to his ministers just as we would to himself; and on the other hand, he provides for our weakness by being pleased to address us in the manner of men by means of interpreters, so that he may draw us to himself instead of driving us away by his thunder. How well this familiar way of teaching suits us all, the godly know from the dread with which the divine majesty rightly fills them.

Those who think that the authority of the doctrine is impaired by the insignificance of the men who are called to teach it betray their ingratitude; for among the many noble endowments with which God has adorned the human race, one of the most remarkable is that he condescends to consecrate the mouths and tongues of men to his service, making his own voice heard in them. Therefore let us not, for our part, refuse to embrace obediently the doctrine of salvation delivered by his command and by his mouth; for although the power of God is not bound to outward means, he has nevertheless bound us to his ordinary method of teaching, and when fanatics refuse to keep to this method, they entangle themselves in many deadly snares. Pride, or fastidiousness, or rivalry leads many to persuade themselves that they can profit enough by reading and meditating in private, and so to despise public assemblies and consider preaching superfluous. But since, as far as lies in them, they loosen or break the sacred bond of unity, none of them escapes the just punishment of this impious divorce; they become bewitched by pestilent errors and the foulest delusions. Therefore, so that the pure simplicity of the faith may flourish among us, let us not refuse to use this exercise of piety, which God, by instituting it, has shown to be necessary, and which he so highly commends. No one, even among the most insolent of men, would dare to say that we are to shut our ears against God; yet in every age prophets and godly teachers have had a hard struggle to maintain against the ungodly, whose perversity cannot submit to the yoke of being taught by the lips and ministry of men. This is just the same as if they wished to destroy the image of God as it is set before us in doctrine. For no other reason were believers in ancient times commanded to seek the face of God in the sanctuary (Psalm 105:4) (a command so often repeated in the Law) than because the doctrine of the Law and the exhortations of the prophets were to them a living image of God. So Paul declares that in his preaching the glory of God shone in the face of Jesus Christ (2 Corinthians 4:6). All the more detestable are the apostates who delight in creating schisms in churches, as if they wished to drive the sheep from the fold and throw them into the jaws of wolves. Let us hold, in agreement with the passage we quoted from Paul, that the Church can be built up only by outward preaching, and that there is no other bond by which the saints can be kept together than by uniting with one consent to observe the order which God has appointed in his Church for learning and making progress. It was especially for this purpose, as I have observed, that believers under the Law were commanded to gather at the sanctuary; for when Moses speaks of the dwelling place of God, he at the same time calls it the place of the name of God, the place where he will record his name (Exodus 20:24), plainly teaching that no use could be made of it without the doctrine of godliness. And there can be no doubt that, for the same reason, David complains with great bitterness of soul that by the tyrannical cruelty of his enemies he was kept from entering the tabernacle (Psalm 84). To many the complaint seems childish, as if no great loss were suffered, and not much pleasure lost, by exclusion from the temple, so long as other amusements were available. David, however, laments this one deprivation as something that fills him with anxiety and sadness, torments him, and almost destroys him. He does so because there is nothing believers value more highly than this aid, by which God gradually raises his people to heaven. For it must be observed that he always showed himself to the holy patriarchs in the mirror of his doctrine in such a way as to make their knowledge spiritual. Hence the temple is not only called his face but also, in order to remove all superstition, is called his footstool (Psalm 132:7; 99:5). Here the unity of the faith is happily realized, when all, from the highest to the lowest, aspire to the head. All the temples which the Gentiles built to God with a different intention were a mere profanation of his worship — a profanation into which the Jews also fell, though not so grossly. Stephen reproaches them with this in the words of Isaiah when he says, "Howbeit the Most High dwelleth not in temples made with hands; as saith the Prophet, Heaven is my throne," and so on (Acts 7:48). For God alone consecrates temples to their lawful use by his word. And when we rashly attempt anything without his command, setting out at once from a bad principle, we bring in invented fictions by which evil spreads without measure. Xerxes acted thoughtlessly when, on the advice of the magi, he burned or pulled down all the temples of Greece, because he thought it absurd that God, to whom all things ought to be free and open, should be enclosed by walls and roofs — as if it were not in God's power in some manner to come down to us, so that he may be near us, and yet neither change his place nor touch us by earthly means, but rather, by certain vehicles as it were, raise us up to his own heavenly glory, which in its immensity fills all things and in its height is above the heavens.

6¶

Moreover, since there is at this time a great dispute about the efficacy of the ministry — some extravagantly overrating its dignity, and others wrongly maintaining that what belongs properly to the Spirit of God is transferred to mortal man when we suppose that ministers and teachers penetrate to the mind and heart, so as to correct the blindness of the one and the hardness of the other — it is necessary to set this controversy on its proper footing. The arguments on both sides will be settled without difficulty if we pay careful attention to the passages in which God, the author of preaching, joins his Spirit to it and then promises a fruitful result; or, on the other hand, to the passages in which God, separating himself from outward means, claims for himself alone both the beginning and the whole course of faith. The office of the second Elijah, as Malachi declares, was to "turn the heart of the fathers to the children, and the heart of the children to their fathers" (Malachi 4:6). Christ declares that he sent the apostles to bring forth fruit from his labors (John 15:16). What this fruit is, Peter briefly defines when he says that we are begotten again of incorruptible seed (1 Peter 1:23). Hence Paul glories that by means of the gospel he had begotten the Corinthians, who were the seal of his apostleship (1 Corinthians 4:15); and, moreover, that his was not a ministry of the letter, which only sounded in the ear, but that the effectual working of the Spirit was given to him, so that his doctrine might not be in vain (1 Corinthians 9:2; 2 Corinthians 3:6). In this sense he declares elsewhere that his gospel came not in word only, but in power (1 Thessalonians 1:5). He also affirms that the Galatians received the Spirit by the hearing of faith (Galatians 3:2). In short, in several passages he not only makes himself a fellow worker with God, but assigns to himself the task of bestowing salvation (1 Corinthians 3:9). He certainly never said all these things with a view to claiming for himself one iota apart from God, as he briefly explains elsewhere: "For this cause also thank we God without ceasing, because, when ye received the word of God which ye heard of us, ye received it not as the word of men, but (as it is in truth) the word of God, which effectually worketh also in you that believe" (1 Thessalonians 2:13). Again, in another place: "He that wrought effectually in Peter to the apostleship of the circumcision, the same was mighty in me toward the Gentiles" (Galatians 2:8). And that he allows no more than this to ministers is clear from other passages: "So then neither is he that planteth any thing, neither he that watereth; but God that giveth the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:7). Again: "I laboured more abundantly than they all: yet not I, but the grace of God which was with me" (1 Corinthians 15:10). And it is indeed necessary to keep these statements in view, since God, in claiming for himself the illumination of the mind and the renewal of the heart, reminds us that it is sacrilege for man to claim any part of either for himself. Still, everyone who listens teachably to the ministers whom God appoints will know by the fruitful result that God has good reason to be pleased with this way of teaching, and good reason to have placed believers under this modest yoke.

7¶

The judgment we ought to form concerning the visible Church, which comes under our observation, must, I think, be sufficiently clear from what has been said. I have observed that the Scriptures speak of the Church in two ways. Sometimes, when they speak of the Church, they mean the Church as it really is before God — the Church into which none are admitted but those who by the gift of adoption are sons of God and by the sanctification of the Spirit are true members of Christ. In this sense it includes not only the saints who dwell on the earth, but all the elect who have existed from the beginning of the world. Often, too, the name of Church designates the whole multitude of people scattered throughout the world who profess to worship one God and Christ; who are initiated into the faith by baptism; who by partaking of the Lord's Supper profess unity in true doctrine and love; who agree in holding the word of the Lord; and who observe the ministry which Christ has appointed for preaching it. In this Church there is a very large mixture of hypocrites, who have nothing of Christ but the name and outward appearance: of ambitious, greedy, envious, slanderous men, and some also of more impure lives, who are tolerated for a time, either because their guilt cannot be legally proved or because due strictness of discipline is not always maintained. Hence, as it is necessary to believe the invisible Church, 105 D105 In this section (7), Calvin specifically distinguishes between the invisible and the visible Church (note first line for term "visible"). The invisible Church is the Church as it really is before God; the visible Church is the Church as it appears to man.

8¶

Accordingly, since it was important for us to recognize it, the Lord has distinguished it by certain marks, and as it were by tokens. It is, indeed, the special prerogative of God to know those who are his, as Paul declares in the passage already quoted (2 Timothy 2:19). And no doubt this has been so arranged as a check on human rashness, since daily experience reminds us how far his secret judgments surpass our understanding. For even those who seemed most abandoned, and who had been utterly despaired of, are recalled to life by his goodness, while those who seemed most stable often fall. Hence, as Augustine says, "In regard to the secret predestination of God, there are very many sheep without, and very many wolves within" (Augustine, Homilies on John, 45). For he knows and has marked those who know neither him nor themselves. Again, of those who openly bear his badge, his eyes alone see which of them are sincerely holy and will persevere to the end, which alone is the completion of salvation. On the other hand, foreseeing that it was in some degree useful for us to know whom we are to regard as his sons, he has in this matter accommodated himself to our capacity. But since full certainty was not necessary here, he has substituted in its place the judgment of charity, by which we acknowledge as members of the Church all who, by confession of faith, regularity of conduct, and participation in the sacraments, join with us in acknowledging the same God and Christ. 106 D106 Here Calvin enumerates the criteria by which we are to judge who are to be acknowledged as members of the visible Church. The presence of these criteria does not, of course, give us full certainty as to who are members of Christ's body, the invisible church (such certainty rests with God alone); but the absence of them leaves us with no bases upon which to judge who are to be regarded as members of the visible Church.

9¶

Hence the form of the Church appears and stands out plainly before our eyes. Wherever we see the word of God purely preached and heard, and the sacraments administered according to the institution of Christ, there we cannot doubt that the Church of God exists in some form, since his promise cannot fail: "Where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them" (Matthew 18:20). But so that we may have a clear summary of this subject, we must proceed by the following steps. The Church universal is the multitude gathered out of all nations, who, though scattered and far distant from one another, agree in the one truth of divine doctrine and are bound together by the tie of a common religion. In this way it includes individual churches, which exist in different towns and villages according to the needs of human society, so that each of them rightly holds the name and authority of the Church; and it also includes individual persons who by a religious profession are counted as belonging to such churches, although they are in fact strangers to the Church, but have not been cut off by a public decision. There is, however, a slight difference in the way we judge individuals and churches. For it may happen in practice that those whom we consider not altogether worthy of the fellowship of believers we nevertheless ought to treat as brothers and regard as believers, because of the common consent of the Church in tolerating and bearing with them in the body of Christ. Such persons we do not approve by our vote as members of the Church, but we leave them the place they hold among the people of God until they are lawfully deprived of it. With regard to the general body, we must judge differently: if they have the ministry of the word and honor the administration of the sacraments, they are undoubtedly entitled to be counted with the Church, because it is certain that these things are not without fruitful result. In this way we both maintain the Church universal in its unity, which malicious minds have always been eager to tear apart, and we do not deny due authority to lawful assemblies distributed as circumstances require. 107 D107 Here Calvin distinguishes between the Church universal (the visible church throughout the world), and local churches and individuals (the visible Church manifested in particular places).

10¶

We have said that the tokens by which the Church is discerned are the preaching of the word and the observance of the sacraments, for these cannot exist anywhere without bearing fruit and prospering by the blessing of God. I do not say that wherever the word is preached fruit appears at once; but that in every place where it is received and has a settled dwelling, it always displays its power. However that may be, when the preaching of the gospel is reverently heard and the sacraments are not neglected, there for the time the face of the Church appears without deception or ambiguity; and no one may with impunity spurn her authority, or reject her admonitions, or resist her counsels, or make sport of her censures, much less revolt from her and violate her unity (see chapter 2, sections 1, 10, and chapter 8, section 12). For the Lord places such value on the communion of his Church that he regards as deserters of religion all who stubbornly separate themselves from any Christian society in which the true ministry of his word and sacraments is maintained. So highly does he commend her authority that when it is violated he considers his own authority to be impaired. For there is no small weight in the title given to her, "the house of God," "the pillar and ground of the truth" (1 Timothy 3:15). By these words Paul indicates that, to keep the truth from perishing in the world, the Church is its faithful guardian, because God has been pleased to preserve the pure preaching of his word through her ministry, and to show himself to us as a parent while he feeds us with spiritual nourishment and provides whatever serves our salvation. Moreover, no small praise is given to the Church when she is said to have been chosen and set apart by Christ as his bride, "not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing" (Ephesians 5:27), and as "his body, the fulness of him that filleth all in all" (Ephesians 1:23). It follows that revolt from the Church is denial of God and Christ. Therefore there is all the more need to beware of so wicked a dissent; for since by it we aim, as far as lies in us, at the destruction of God's truth, we deserve to be crushed by the full thunder of his anger. No crime can be imagined more atrocious than sacrilegiously and treacherously violating the sacred marriage which the only begotten Son of God has condescended to contract with us.

11¶

Therefore let these marks be carefully impressed on our minds, and let us value them as in the sight of the Lord. There is nothing Satan is more intent on than destroying and effacing one or both of them — at one time to delete and abolish these marks, and so destroy the true and genuine distinction of the Church; at another, to bring them into contempt, and so drive us headlong into open revolt from the Church. It was owing to his schemes that for several ages the pure preaching of the word disappeared; and now, with the same dishonest aim, he labors to overthrow the ministry, which, however, Christ has so ordered in his Church that if it is removed the whole building must fall. How dangerous, then — no, how fatal — is the temptation when we even entertain the thought of separating ourselves from that assembly in which are seen the signs and badges which the Lord has judged sufficient to mark out his Church! We see how much caution must be used in both respects. So that we may not be deceived by the name of Church, every congregation that claims the name must be brought to this test as to a Lydian stone. If it holds the order instituted by the Lord in word and sacraments, there will be no deception; we may safely pay it the honor due to a church. On the other hand, if it presents itself without word and sacraments, we must in this case be no less careful to avoid the imposture than we were to shun pride and presumption in the other.

12¶

When we say that the pure ministry of the word and the pure celebration of the sacraments are a fit pledge and guarantee, so that we may safely recognize a church in every society in which both exist, we mean that we are never to cast it off so long as these remain, even though it may otherwise abound in many faults. Indeed, even in the administration of word and sacraments defects may creep in which ought not to alienate us from its communion. For not all the heads of true doctrine are on the same footing. Some are so necessary to be known that all must hold them as fixed and beyond doubt, as the proper essentials of religion: for instance, that God is one, that Christ is God and the Son of God, that our salvation depends on the mercy of God, and the like. Others, again, which are matters of controversy among the churches, do not destroy the unity of the faith. For why should it be treated as a ground of division between churches if one, without any spirit of contention or perversity in dogmatizing, holds that the soul on leaving the body flies to heaven, and another, without venturing to speak positively about its abode, holds it as certain that it lives with the Lord? French, "Pour donner exemple, s'il advenoit qu'une Eglise tint que les ames etant separées des corps fussent transferés au ciel incontinent: une autre, sans oser determiner du lieu pens't semplement qu'elles vivent en Dieu: et que telle diversité fut sans contention et sans opiniatreté pourquoy se diviseroient elles d'ensemble?" — To give an example, if one church should happen to hold that the soul, when separated from the body, is at once carried to heaven, and another, without venturing to determine the place, should simply think that it lives in God, and if such a difference were held without contention and obstinacy, why should they be divided from each other?

French, "La doctrine principale de nostre salut;" — the fundamental doctrine of our salvation.

French, "Et aussi que demeurant en icelle nous ne troublions point la police ni la discipline;" — and also that, remaining in it, we do not disturb its order and discipline.

13¶

Our indulgence ought to extend much further in tolerating imperfection of conduct. Here the danger of falling is great, and Satan uses all his schemes to ensnare us. For there have always been persons who, filled with a false conviction of their own perfect holiness, as if they had already become a kind of airy spirits, French, "Comme s'ils eussent ete quelques anges de Paradis;" — as if they had been some angels of Paradise.

14¶

They exclaim that it is impossible to tolerate the vice that everywhere stalks abroad like a plague. What if the apostle's judgment applies here also? Among the Corinthians it was not a few who went astray, but almost the whole body had become tainted; there was not just one kind of sin, but a multitude, and those not trivial errors, but some of them detestable crimes. There was corruption not only in morals but also in doctrine. What course did the holy apostle take — in other words, the instrument of the heavenly Spirit, by whose testimony the Church stands and falls? Does he seek separation from them? Does he cast them out of the kingdom of Christ? Does he strike them with the thunder of a final anathema? He not only does none of these things, but he acknowledges and proclaims them as a Church of Christ and a society of saints. If the Church remains among the Corinthians, where envyings, divisions, and contentions rage; where quarrels, lawsuits, and greed prevail; where a crime which even the Gentiles would abhor is openly approved; where the name of Paul, whom they ought to have honored as a father, is insolently attacked; where some hold the resurrection of the dead in derision, though with it the whole gospel must fall; where the gifts of God are made to serve ambition, not love; where many things are done neither decently nor in order: 1 Corinthians 1:11; 3:3; 5:l; 6:7; 9:l; 15:12.

French, "Toutesfois Sainct Paul recognoissoit entre eux quelque Eglise;" — yet Saint Paul recognized some church among them.

15¶

They also object that Paul sharply rebukes the Corinthians for allowing a heinous offender in their communion, and then lays down a general rule by which he declares it unlawful even to eat bread with a man of impure life (1 Corinthians 5:11, 12). Here they exclaim: If it is not lawful to eat ordinary bread, how can it be lawful to eat the Lord's bread? I admit that it is a great disgrace if dogs and swine are admitted among the children of God; much more if the sacred body of Christ is prostituted to them. And indeed, when churches are well ordered, they will not bear the wicked in their bosom, nor will they admit the worthy and the unworthy without distinction to that sacred feast. But because pastors are not always diligently watchful, are sometimes more indulgent than they ought to be, or are prevented from acting as strictly as they would wish, the result is that even the openly wicked are not always excluded from the fellowship of the saints. This I admit to be a fault, and I have no wish to excuse it, since Paul sharply rebukes it in the Corinthians. But although the Church fails in her duty, it does not therefore follow that every private individual is to decide the question of separation for himself. I do not deny that it is the duty of a godly man to withdraw from all private dealings with the wicked, and not to entangle himself with them by any voluntary tie; but it is one thing to shun the company of the wicked, and another to renounce the communion of the Church out of hatred for them. Those who think it sacrilege to partake of the Lord's bread with the wicked are in this more rigid than Paul. See Calvin, Lib. de Cœna Domini; item, Instructio adv. Anabapt.

16¶

Still, even the good are sometimes affected by this thoughtless zeal for righteousness, though we shall find that this excessive severity springs more from pride and a false notion of holiness than from genuine holiness and true zeal for it. Accordingly, those who are the most forward, and as it were the leaders, in producing revolt from the Church have for the most part no other motive than to display their own superiority by despising all other men. Well and wisely, therefore, does Augustine say: "Seeing that godly reason and the manner of ecclesiastical discipline ought especially to regard the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace, which the Apostle commands us to keep by bearing with one another (for if we do not keep it, the application of medicine is not only superfluous but harmful, and so proves to be no medicine at all), those bad sons who, not from hatred of other men's iniquities but from zeal for their own contentions, try altogether to draw away, or at least to divide, weak brothers ensnared by the glare of their name — while swollen with pride, stuffed with insolence, insidiously slanderous, and turbulently seditious — use the cloak of a rigorous severity, so that they may not seem to lack the light of truth, and they pervert to sacrilegious schism and to purposes of cutting off those things which are commanded in the Holy Scriptures (due regard being had to sincere love and the unity of peace) for correcting a brother's faults by the application of a moderate cure" (Augustine, Against Parmenianus, chapter 1). To the godly and peaceable his advice is to correct mercifully what they can, and to bear patiently with what they cannot correct, lamenting and mourning in love until God either reforms or corrects, or at the harvest roots up the tares and scatters the chaff (ibid., chapter 2). Let all the godly take care to provide themselves with these weapons, lest, while they consider themselves strenuous and ardent defenders of righteousness, they revolt from the kingdom of heaven, which is the only kingdom of righteousness. For since God has been pleased that the communion of his Church be maintained in this external society, anyone who, from hatred of the ungodly, violates the bond of this society enters on a downward course in which he runs great danger of cutting himself off from the communion of saints. Let them reflect that in a large body there are many who may escape their notice and yet are truly righteous and innocent in the eyes of the Lord. Let them reflect that of those who seem diseased, there are many who are far from taking pleasure or flattering themselves in their faults, and who, again and again aroused by a serious fear of the Lord, aspire to greater integrity. Let them reflect that they have no right to pass judgment on a man for one act, since the holiest sometimes fall most grievously. Let them reflect that in the ministry of the word and the participation of the sacraments, the power to gather the Church is too great to be deprived of all its efficacy by the fault of some ungodly men. Lastly, let them reflect that in assessing the Church, divine judgment carries more weight than human.

17¶

Since they also argue that there is good reason for the Church being called holy, it is necessary to consider what the holiness is in which it excels, lest by refusing to acknowledge any church except one that is completely perfect, we leave no church at all. It is true, indeed, as Paul says, that Christ "loved the church, and gave himself for it, that he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the word, that he might present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing; but that it should be holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:25-27). Nevertheless, it is also true that the Lord is daily smoothing its wrinkles and wiping away its spots. Hence it follows that its holiness is not yet perfect. Such, then, is the holiness of the Church: it makes daily progress but is not yet perfect; it advances every day but has not yet reached the goal, as will be explained more fully elsewhere. Therefore, when the prophets foretell, "Then shall Jerusalem be holy, and there shall no strangers pass through her any more," and "It shall be called, The way of holiness; the unclean shall not pass over it" (Joel 3:17; Isaiah 35:8), let us not understand this as if no blemish remained in the members of the Church, but only that they aspire with their whole heart after holiness and perfect purity; and hence that the purity which they have not yet fully attained is, by the kindness of God, attributed to them. And though the signs of such holiness among men are all too rare, we must understand that at no time since the world began has the Lord been without his Church, nor ever will be until the final consummation of all things. 108 D108 Calvin here asserts that, in all ages, there has been a Church, and that this Church will continue until the final consummation of all things. This emphasis, of course, does not agree with the view that God dealt with the nation of Israel in the Old Testament, but now deals with the Church; nor does it comport with the view that Old Testament believers cannot be said to have been in the Church, since the Church began at Pentecost. Calvin views believers of all ages and dispensations as members of Christ's Church.

18¶

On this point Christ himself, his apostles, and almost all the prophets have given us examples. Fearful are the descriptions in which Isaiah, Jeremiah, Joel, Habakkuk, and others lament the diseases of the Church of Jerusalem. In the people, the rulers, and the priests, corruption prevailed to such a degree that Isaiah does not hesitate to liken Jerusalem to Sodom and Gomorrah (Isaiah 1:10). Religion was partly despised and partly adulterated, while as for morals, we everywhere meet with accounts of theft, robbery, treachery, murder, and similar crimes. The prophets, however, did not on that account either form new churches for themselves or set up new altars on which they might offer separate sacrifices. Whatever their countrymen might be, they reflected that the Lord had deposited his word with them and instituted the ceremonies by which he was then worshiped, and so they lifted up pure hands to him, even amid the company of the ungodly. Certainly, had they thought that they contracted any pollution by doing so, they would have died a hundred deaths sooner than allow themselves to be dragged there. Nothing, therefore, kept them from separating themselves except a desire to preserve unity. But if the holy prophets felt no obligation to withdraw from the Church on account of the very many and heinous crimes, not of one or two individuals but of almost the whole people, we claim too much for ourselves if we presume to withdraw at once from the communion of the Church because the lives of all do not agree with our judgment, or even with the Christian profession.

19¶

Then what kind of age was that of Christ and the apostles? Yet neither the desperate ungodliness of the Pharisees nor the dissolute license of morals that everywhere prevailed could keep them from using the same sacred rites as the people and meeting in one common temple for the public exercises of religion. And why so, except that they knew that those who joined in these sacred rites with a pure conscience were in no way polluted by the company of the wicked? If anyone is little moved by prophets and apostles, let him at least defer to the authority of Christ. Well, therefore, does Cyprian say: "Although tares or unclean vessels are seen in the Church, that is no reason why we ourselves should withdraw from the Church; we must only labor that we may be able to be wheat; we must give our effort, and strive as far as we can, to be vessels of gold or silver. But to break the earthen vessels belongs to the Lord alone, to whom a rod of iron has been given. Let no one claim for himself what belongs to the Son alone, and think himself able to winnow the floor and cleanse away the chaff, and separate all the tares by human judgment. What depraved zeal thus takes upon itself is proud obstinacy and sacrilegious presumption" (Cyprian, book 3, epistle 5). Let both points, therefore, be regarded as fixed: first, that there is no excuse for anyone who voluntarily abandons the external communion of a church in which the word of God is preached and the sacraments are administered; secondly, that notwithstanding the faults of a few or of many, nothing prevents us from duly professing our faith there in the ordinances instituted by God, because a godly conscience is not injured by the unworthiness of another, whether he is a pastor or a private person; and sacred rites are no less pure and wholesome to a man who is holy and upright because they are at the same time handled by the impure.

20¶

Their severity and pride go to even greater lengths. Refusing to acknowledge any church that is not pure of the smallest blemish, they take offense at sound teachers for urging believers to make progress, and so teaching them to groan all their lives under the burden of sin and to flee for pardon. For they preten Latin, "Jactant." — French, "Ces grands correcteurs leur reprochent;" — those great reformers reproach them.

21¶

Nor is it only once for all that the Lord, by the forgiveness of sins, elects and admits us into the Church; by the same means he also preserves and defends us in it. For what would it profit us to receive a pardon of which we were afterward to have no use? That the mercy of the Lord would be vain and illusory if it were granted only once, all the godly can testify; for there is no one who is not conscious throughout his whole life of many weaknesses that need divine mercy. And truly it is not without cause that the Lord promises this gift especially to his own household, nor in vain that he commands the same message of reconciliation to be delivered to them daily. Therefore, since throughout our whole lives we carry about with us the remains of sin, we could not remain in the Church for one single moment unless we were sustained by the unbroken grace of God in forgiving our sins. On the other hand, the Lord has called his people to eternal salvation, and so they ought to consider that pardon for their sins is always ready. Hence let us firmly hold that if we are admitted and grafted into the body of the Church, the forgiveness of sins has been bestowed, and is daily bestowed, on us by divine generosity, through the intervention of Christ's merits and the sanctification of the Spirit.

22¶

To impart this blessing to us, the keys have been given to the Church (Matthew 16:19; 18:18). For when Christ gave the command to the apostles and conferred the power of forgiving sins, he did not merely intend that they should loose the sins of those who would be converted from ungodliness to the faith of Christ; French, "Ce n'a pas eté seulement afin qu'ils deliassent ceux qui si convertiroient alla foy Christienne, et qu'ils fissent cela pour une fois." — It was not only that they might loose those who would be converted to the Christian faith, and that they should do so once for all

23¶

But since those frantic spirits of whom I have spoken try to rob the Church of this, the only anchor of salvation, consciences must be more firmly strengthened against this pestilent opinion. The Novatians in ancient times disturbed the churches with this dogma; and in our day some of the Anabaptists, not unlike the Novatians, have fallen into the same delirious dreams. For they claim that in baptism the people of God are regenerated to a pure and angelic life, which is not polluted by any fleshly defilement. But if a man sins after baptism, they leave him nothing but the inexorable judgment of God. In short, to the sinner who has fallen after receiving grace they give no hope of pardon, because they admit no other forgiveness of sins except that by which we are first regenerated. But although no falsehood is more clearly refuted by Scripture, yet since these men find ways to deceive (as Novatus also of old had a great many followers), let us briefly show how they rave, to the destruction both of themselves and of others. In the first place, since by the command of our Lord the saints daily repeat this prayer, "Forgive us our debts" (Matthew 6:12), they confess that they are debtors. Nor do they ask in vain, for the Lord has commanded them to ask only what he will give. Indeed, while he has declared that the whole prayer will be heard by his Father, he has sealed this absolution with a special promise. What more do we want? The Lord requires of his saints confession of sins throughout their whole lives, and that without ceasing, and he promises pardon. How presumptuous, then, to exempt them from sin, or, when they have stumbled, to shut them out from grace altogether! Again, whom does he command us to pardon seventy times seven? Is it not our brothers (Matthew 18:22)? And why has he so commanded, except that we may imitate his clemency? He therefore pardons not once or twice only, but as often as, aware of our faults, we are alarmed and call upon him with sighs.

24¶

And to begin almost with the very first beginning of the Church: the patriarchs had been circumcised, admitted to a share in the covenant, and no doubt instructed by their father's care in righteousness and integrity, when they conspired to commit fratricide. The crime was one which the most abandoned robbers would have abhorred. Genesis 37:18, 28; 34:25; 35:22; 38:16; 2 Samuel 11:4, 15; xii 13.

25¶

But I am unwilling to begin a list that could never be finished. The prophetic books are full of similar promises, offering mercy to a people covered with countless transgressions. What crime is more heinous than rebellion? It is called a divorce between God and the Church, and yet by his goodness it is overcome. They say, "If a man put away his wife, and she go from him, and become another man's, shall he return unto her again? shall not that land be greatly polluted? But thou hast played the harlot with many lovers; yet return again unto me, saith the Lord." "Return, thou backsliding Israel, saith the Lord; and I will not cause mine anger to fall upon you; for I am merciful, saith the Lord, and I will not keep anger for ever" (Jeremiah 3:1, 12). And surely he could have no different feeling who declares, "I have no pleasure in the death of him that dieth"; "Wherefore turn yourselves, and live ye" (Ezekiel 18:23, 32). Accordingly, when Solomon dedicated the temple, one of the uses for which it was intended was that prayers offered for the pardon of sins might be heard there: "If they sin against thee (for there is no man that sinneth not), and thou be angry with them, and deliver them to the enemy, so that they carry them away captive unto the land of the enemy, far or near; yet if they shall bethink themselves in the land whither they were carried captives, and repent, and make supplication unto thee in the land of them that carried them captives, saying, We have sinned, and have done perversely, we have committed wickedness; and so return unto thee with all their heart, and with all their soul, in the land of their enemies which led them away captive, and pray unto thee towards their land, which thou gavest unto their fathers, the city which thou hast chosen, and the house which I have built for thy name: then hear thou their prayer and their supplication in heaven thy dwelling-place, and maintain their cause, and forgive thy people that have sinned against thee, and all their transgressions wherein they have transgressed against thee" (1 Kings 8:46-50). Nor was it in vain that God in the Law ordained a daily sacrifice for sins. Had he not foreseen that his people would constantly suffer from the disease of sin, he would never have appointed these remedies.

26¶

Did the coming of Christ, in which the fullness of grace was displayed, deprive believers of this privilege of praying for the pardon of their sins? If they offended against the Lord, were they to obtain no mercy? What would this be but to say that Christ came not for the salvation but for the destruction of his people, if the divine indulgence in pardoning sin, which was constantly provided for the saints under the Old Testament, is now declared to have been taken away? But if we believe the Scriptures when they plainly proclaim that in Christ alone the grace and loving-kindness of the Lord have fully appeared, the riches of his mercy have been poured out, and reconciliation between God and man has been accomplished (Titus 2:11; 3:4; 2 Timothy 1:9, 10), let us not doubt that the clemency of our heavenly Father, instead of being cut off or curtailed, is present in much greater abundance. Nor are proofs of this lacking. Peter, who had heard our Savior declare that whoever did not confess his name before men would be denied before the angels of God, denied him three times in one night, and not without cursing; yet he is not denied pardon (Mark 8:38). Those among the Thessalonians who lived disorderly, though they are rebuked, are still invited to repentance (2 Thessalonians 3:6). Not even Simon Magus is thrown into despair. He is rather told to hope, since Peter invites him to turn to prayer (Acts 8:22).

27¶

What shall we say to the fact that at times whole churches have been implicated in the grossest sins, and yet Paul, instead of giving them over to destruction, mercifully rescued them? The defection of the Galatians was no trivial fault; the Corinthians were still less excusable, since the iniquities prevailing among them were more numerous and no less heinous; yet neither is excluded from the mercy of the Lord. Indeed, the very persons who had sinned beyond others in uncleanness and fornication are expressly invited to repentance. The covenant of the Lord remains, and will always remain, inviolable — that covenant which he solemnly ratified with Christ, the true Solomon, and with his members, in these words: "If his children forsake my law, and walk not in my judgments; if they break my statutes, and keep not my commandments; then will I visit their transgression with the rod, and their iniquity with stripes. Nevertheless, my loving-kindness will I not utterly take from him" (Psalm 89:30-33). In short, by the very arrangement of the Creed we are reminded that the forgiveness of sins always resides in the Church of Christ, for after the Church is, as it were, established, the forgiveness of sins is added.

28¶

Some persons with somewhat more discernment, seeing that the dogma of Novatus is so clearly refuted in Scripture, do not make every fault unpardonable, but only that voluntary transgression of the Law into which a man falls knowingly and willingly. Those who speak in this way allow pardon only for sins committed through ignorance. But since the Lord in the Law commanded some sacrifices to be offered in expiation of the voluntary sins of believers, and others to redeem sins of ignorance (Leviticus 4), how perverse it is to grant no expiation for a voluntary sin! I hold nothing to be plainer than that the one sacrifice of Christ avails to remit the voluntary sins of believers, the Lord having attested this by fleshly sacrifices as emblems. Again, how is David, who was so well instructed in the Law, to be excused by ignorance? Did David, who was daily punishing these things in others, not know how heinous a crime murder and adultery were? Did the patriarchs consider fratricide a lawful act? Had the Corinthians made so little progress as to imagine that God was pleased with lewdness, impurity, whoredom, hatred, and strife? Was Peter, after being so carefully warned, ignorant of how heinous it was to forswear his Master? Therefore let us not by our malice shut the door against the divine mercy when it is so kindly displayed.

29¶

I am not unaware that by the sins which are daily forgiven to believers, ancient writers understood the lighter errors that creep in through the weakness of the flesh, while they thought that the formal repentance then required for more heinous crimes was no more to be repeated than baptism. This opinion is not to be taken as if they wished to plunge into despair those who had fallen after their first repentance, or to make light of those errors as if they were of no account before God. For they knew that the saints often stumble through unbelief, that needless oaths sometimes escape them, that they sometimes boil with anger, indeed break out into open abuse, and labor besides under other evils which are in no slight degree offensive to the Lord; but they called them by that name to distinguish them from public crimes, which came under the notice of the Church and caused great scandal. French, "Ils usoient de cette maniere de parler afin de mettre difference autre les fautes privees, et les crimes publiques qui emportoient grands scandales en l'Eglise." — They used this manner of speaking in order to make a distinction between private faults and the public crimes which brought great scandal into the Church.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin calls the true Church the mother of all the godly, outside whose nurture there is no ordinary hope of growing into salvation — for God feeds his children through the preaching of the Word and the right administration of the sacraments, and we cut ourselves off from him when we despise the means he has appointed. In 2026, when the digital age tempts believers to a private, disembodied faith — a sermon streamed alone, a worship of convenience that answers to no one — this word is bracing. You were not made to follow Christ as an isolated unit; you were given a mother. The marks are not buildings or brands but the Word truly preached and the sacraments rightly administered, and where these are found, however imperfect the congregation, there the godly are bound to gather and remain. Bring your household under that nurture and stay. Patience with a flawed church is not compromise but obedience, for the Lord saves his people together, and the steadfast are those who do not abandon the assembly when it disappoints them.
