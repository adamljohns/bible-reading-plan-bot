Book 2 · Chapter 3 · USMC modern English

Every Thing Proceeding From the Corrupt Nature of Man Damnable

The principal matters in this chapter are—I. A recapitulation of the former chapter, proving, from passages of Scriptures that the intellect and will of man are so corrupted, that no integrity, no knowledge or fear of God, can now be found in him, sect. 1 and 2. II. Objections to this doctrine, from the virtues which shone in some of the heathen, refuted, sect. 3 and 4. III. What kind of will remains in man, the slave of sin, sect. 5. The remedy and cure, sect. 6. IV. The opinion of Neo-Pelagian sophists concerning the preparation and efficacy of the will, and also concerning perseverance and co-operating grace, refuted, both by reason and Scripture, sect. 7–12. V. Some passages from Augustine confirming the truth of this doctrine, sect. 13 and 14.

Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. The intellect and will of the whole man corrupt. The term flesh applies not only to the sensual, but also to the higher part of the soul. This demonstrated from Scripture.
  2. 2. The heart also involved in corruption, and hence in no part of man can integrity, or knowledge or the fear of God, be found.
  3. 3. Objection, that some of the heathen were possessed of admirable endowments, and, therefore, that the nature of man is not entirely corrupt. Answer, Corruption is not entirely removed, but only inwardly restrained. Explanation of this answer.
  4. 4. Objection still urged, that the virtuous and vicious among the heathen must be put upon the same level, or the virtuous prove that human nature, properly cultivated, is not devoid of virtue. Answer, That these are not ordinary properties of human nature, but special gifts of God. These gifts defiled by ambition, and hence the actions proceeding from them, however esteemed by man, have no merit with God.
  5. 5. Though man has still the faculty of willing there is no soundness in it. He falls under the bondage of sin necessarily, and yet voluntarily. Necessity must be distinguished from compulsion. The ancient Theologians acquainted with this necessity. Some passages condemning the vacillation of Lombard.
  6. 6. Conversion to God constitutes the remedy or soundness of the human will. This not only begun, but continued and completed; the beginning, continuance, and completion, being ascribed entirely to God. This proved by Ezekiel’s description of the stony heart, and from other passages of Scripture.
  7. 7. Various Objections.—1. The will is converted by God, but, when once prepared, does its part in the work of conversion. Answer from Augustine. 2. Grace can do nothing without will, nor the will without grace. Answer. Grace itself produces will. God prevents the unwilling, making him willing, and follows up this preventing grace that he may not will in vain. Another answer gathered from various passages of Augustine.
  8. 8. Answer to the second Objection continued. No will inclining to good except in the elect. The cause of election out of man. Hence right will, as well as election, are from the good pleasure of God. The beginning of willing and doing well is of faith; faith again is the gift of God; and hence mere grace is the cause of our beginning to will well. This proved by Scripture.
  9. 9. Answer to second Objection continued. That good will is merely of grace proved by the prayers of saints. Three axioms 1. God does not prepare man’s heart, so that he can afterwards do some good of himself, but every desire of rectitude, every inclination to study, and every effort to pursue it, is from Him. 2. This desire, study, and effort, do not stop short, but continue to effect. 3. This progress is constant. The believer perseveres to the end. A third Objection, and three answers to it.
  10. 10. A fourth Objection. Answer. Fifth Objection. Answer. Answer confirmed by many passages of Scripture, and supported by a passage from Augustine.
  11. 11. Perseverance not of ourselves, but of God. Objection. Two errors in the objection. Refutation of both.
  12. 12. An objection founded on the distinction of co-operating grace. Answer. Answer confirmed by the testimony of Augustine and Bernard.
  13. 13. Last part of the chapter, in which it is proved by many passages of Augustine, that he held the doctrine here taught.
  14. 14. An objection, representing Augustine at variance with himself and other Theologians, removed. A summary of Augustine’s doctrine on free will.
1¶

The nature of man, in both parts of his soul — namely, intellect and will — cannot be better ascertained than by paying attention to the terms that Scripture applies to him. If he is fully portrayed (and it can easily be proved that he is) by the words of our Savior, "that which is born of the flesh is flesh" (John 3:6), he must be a very miserable creature. For, as an apostle declares, "to be carnally minded is death" (Romans 8:8); "it is enmity against God, and is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be." Is it true that the flesh is so perverse that it is perpetually striving with all its might against God? That it cannot agree with the righteousness of the divine law? That, in short, it can produce nothing but the materials of death? Grant that there is nothing in human nature but flesh, and then draw something good out of it if you can. But it will be said that the word flesh applies only to the sensual part of the soul, and not to the higher part. This, however, is completely refuted by the words both of Christ and of his apostle. Our Lord's statement is that a man must be born again because he is flesh. He does not require him to be born again with reference to the body. But a mind is not born again merely by having some portion of it reformed. It must be totally renewed. This is confirmed by the contrast drawn in both passages. In the contrast between the Spirit and the flesh, nothing of an intermediate nature is left. In this way everything in man that is not spiritual falls under the name of carnal. But we have nothing of the Spirit except through regeneration. Everything, therefore, that we have from nature is flesh. Any possible doubt on the subject is removed by the words of Paul (Ephesians 4:23), where, after describing the old man, who, he says, "is corrupt according to the deceitful lusts," he bids us "be renewed in the spirit" of our mind. You see that he places unlawful and depraved desires not merely in the sensual part, but in the mind itself, and therefore requires that the mind be renewed. Indeed, a little before this he had drawn a picture of human nature which shows that there is no part of it that is not perverted and corrupted. For when he says that the "Gentiles walk in the vanity of their mind, having the understanding darkened, being alienated from the life of God through the ignorance that is in them, because of the blindness of their heart" (Ephesians 4:17, 18), there can be no doubt that his words apply to all whom the Lord has not yet formed anew both to wisdom and to righteousness. This is made still clearer by the comparison that immediately follows, in which he reminds believers that they "have not so learned Christ" — words implying that the grace of Christ is the only remedy for that blindness and its evil consequences. So too Isaiah had prophesied of the kingdom of Christ, when the Lord promised the Church that though darkness should "cover the earth, and gross darkness the people," yet he would "arise" upon it, and "his glory" would be seen upon it (Isaiah 40:2). When it is declared in this way that divine light is to arise on the Church alone, everything outside the Church is left in blindness and darkness. I will not list everything that occurs throughout Scripture, and particularly in the Psalms and the Prophets, concerning the vanity of man. There is much in what David says: "Surely men of low degree are vanity, and men of high degree are a lie: to be laid in the balance, they are altogether lighter than vanity" (Psalm 62:10). The human mind receives a humbling blow when all the thoughts that come from it are derided as foolish, frivolous, perverse, and insane.

2¶

The condemnation of the heart is in no way more lenient, when it is described as "deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked" (Jeremiah 17:9). But since I am aiming at brevity, I will be content with a single passage, one, however, in which, as in a bright mirror, we may see a complete image of our nature. The Apostle, when he wants to humble man's pride, uses these words: "There is none righteous, no, not one: there is none that understandeth, there is none that seeketh after God. They are all gone out of the way, they are together become unprofitable; there is none that does good, no, not one. Their throat is an open sepulchre; with their tongues they have used deceit; the poison of asps is under their lips: whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness: their feet are swift to shed blood: destruction and misery are in their ways: and the way of peace have they not known: there is no fear of God before their eyes" (Romans 3:10–18). In this way he thunders not against certain individuals, but against the whole posterity of Adam — not against the depraved morals of any single age, but against the perpetual corruption of nature. His purpose in the passage is not merely to rebuke men so that they may repent, but to teach that all are overwhelmed by an inescapable calamity, and can be delivered from it only by the mercy of God. Since this could not be proved without first proving the overthrow and destruction of nature, he brought forward those passages to show that its ruin is complete.

Let it be settled, then, that men are as they are here described, not by vicious habit, but by depravity of nature. Otherwise the Apostle's reasoning — that there is no salvation for man except in the mercy of God, because in himself he is hopeless and undone — could not stand. I will not labor here to prove that the passages apply, in order to remove the doubts of any who might think them quoted out of place. I will take them as if Paul had used them for the first time, and not taken them from the Prophets. First, then, he strips man of righteousness, that is, of integrity and purity; and second, he strips him of sound understanding. He argues that the lack of understanding is proved by apostasy from God. To seek Him is the beginning of wisdom, and therefore this lack must exist in all who have revolted from Him. He adds that all have gone astray and become, as it were, mere corruption; that there is none that does good. He then lists the crimes by which those who have once given free rein to their wickedness pollute every member of their bodies. Lastly, he declares that they have no fear of God, by whose rule all our steps should be directed. If these are the hereditary properties of the human race, it is vain to look for anything good in our nature. I admit, indeed, that all these iniquities do not break out in every individual. Still, it cannot be denied that the hydra lurks in every breast. For just as a body that contains and nourishes the cause and matter of disease cannot be called healthy, even though no pain is actually felt, so a soul teeming with such seeds of vice cannot be called sound. This comparison, however, does not hold at every point. In a body, however diseased, the functions of life are still performed; but the soul, once plunged into that deadly abyss, not only labors under vice, but is altogether empty of good.

3¶

Here again we meet a question very much like the one that was solved earlier. In every age there have been some who, under the guidance of nature, were devoted to virtue all their lives. It makes no difference that many blots may be found in their conduct; by the very pursuit of virtue they showed that there was some purity in their nature. The value that virtues of this kind have in the sight of God will be considered more fully when we treat the merit of works. In the meantime, however, it will be proper to consider it in this place as well, so far as is necessary for explaining the subject at hand. Such examples, then, seem to warn us against supposing that the nature of man is utterly vicious, since under its guidance some have not only excelled in illustrious deeds, but conducted themselves most honorably through the whole course of their lives. But we ought to consider that, in spite of the corruption of our nature, there is some room for divine grace — such grace as, without purifying nature, may place it under inward restraint. For if the Lord let every mind loose to run wild in its lusts, there is doubtless not a man who would not show that his nature is capable of all the crimes with which Paul charges it (Romans 3, compared with Psalm 14:3, etc.). What? Can you exempt yourself from the number of those whose feet are swift to shed blood; whose hands are foul with robbery and murder; whose throats are like open sepulchres; whose tongues are deceitful; whose lips are venomous; whose actions are useless, unjust, rotten, and deadly; whose soul is without God; whose inward parts are full of wickedness; whose eyes are on the watch for deception; whose minds are ready for insult; whose every part, in short, is framed for endless deeds of wickedness? If every soul is capable of such abominations (and the Apostle boldly declares that it is), it is surely easy to see what the result would be if the Lord were to allow human passion to follow its own bent. No ravenous beast would rush so furiously, no stream, however swift and violent, would burst its banks so headlong. In the elect, God cures these diseases in the way that will shortly be explained; in others, he only places them under such restraint as may keep them from breaking out to a degree incompatible with the preservation of the established order of things. Hence, however much men may disguise their impurity, some are held back only by shame, and others by fear of the laws, from breaking out into many kinds of wickedness. Some aspire to an honest life because they consider it most conducive to their own interest, while others are raised above the common lot so that by the dignity of their station they may keep those beneath them to their duty. In this way God, by his providence, curbs the perversity of nature and keeps it from breaking out into action, yet without making it inwardly pure.

4¶

The objection, however, is not yet solved. For we must either put Catiline on the same footing as Camillus, or hold Camillus to be proof that nature, when carefully cultivated, is not wholly empty of goodness. I admit that the attractive qualities Camillus possessed were gifts of God, and appear to deserve praise when they are viewed in themselves. But in what way will they be proofs of a virtuous nature? Must we not go back to the mind, and begin to reason from it in this way? If a natural man possesses such integrity of character, nature is not without the capacity to pursue virtue. But what if his mind was depraved and perverted, and followed anything rather than uprightness? That is undoubtedly what it was, if you grant that he was only a natural man. How then will you praise the power of human nature for good, if even where there is the highest appearance of integrity a corrupt bias is always detected? Therefore, just as you would not commend for virtue a man whose vices deceive you by a show of virtue, so you will not ascribe to the human will a power of choosing uprightness while it is rooted in depravity (see Augustine, Against Julian, book 4). Still, the surest and easiest answer to the objection is that these are not common endowments of nature, but special gifts of God, which he distributes in various forms, and in a definite measure, to men who are otherwise profane. For this reason we do not hesitate, in ordinary speech, to say that one man has a good nature and another a vicious one; yet we do not cease to hold that both are placed under the universal condition of human depravity. All we mean is that God has conferred on the one a special grace which he has not seen fit to confer on the other. When he was pleased to set Saul over the kingdom, he made him, as it were, a new man. This is what Plato means when, alluding to a passage in the Iliad, he says that the children of kings are marked out at birth by certain special qualities — God, in kindness to the human race, often giving a heroic spirit to those whom he destines to rule. In this way the great leaders celebrated in history were formed. The same judgment must be given in the case of private individuals. But since those endowed with the greatest talents were always driven by the greatest ambition (a stain that defiles all virtues and makes them lose all favor in the sight of God), we cannot place any value on anything that seems praiseworthy in ungodly men. We may add that the principal part of uprightness is missing where there is no zeal for the glory of God, and there is no such zeal in those whom he has not regenerated by his Spirit. Nor is it said without good reason in Isaiah that on Christ would rest "the spirit of knowledge, and of the fear of the Lord" (Isaiah 11:2); for by this we are taught that all who are strangers to Christ are without that fear of God which is the beginning of wisdom (Psalm 111:10). The virtues that deceive us by an empty show may have their praise in civil society and the common dealings of life, but before the judgment seat of God they will be of no value in establishing a claim to righteousness.

5¶

When the will is chained as the slave of sin, it cannot make a single movement toward goodness, much less steadily pursue it. Every such movement is the first step in that conversion to God which Scripture ascribes entirely to divine grace. So Jeremiah prays, "Turn thou me, and I shall be turned" (Jeremiah 31:18). Hence also, in the same chapter, describing the spiritual redemption of believers, the Prophet says, "The Lord has redeemed Jacob, and ransomed him from the hand of him that was stronger than he" (Jeremiah 31:11), indicating how tight the fetters are with which the sinner is bound, so long as he is abandoned by the Lord and acts under the yoke of the devil. Nevertheless, there remains a will which both inclines toward sin and rushes on to it with the strongest affection; for man, when placed under this bondage, is deprived not of will, but of soundness of will. Bernard says, not inappropriately, that all of us have a will; but to will well is progress, and to will ill is defect. Thus simply to will belongs to man, to will ill belongs to corrupt nature, and to will well belongs to grace. Moreover, when I say that the will, deprived of liberty, is led or dragged by necessity to evil, it is strange that anyone should consider the expression harsh, since there is no absurdity in it, and it does not conflict with godly usage. It does, however, offend those who do not know how to distinguish between necessity and compulsion. If anyone were to ask them, "Is not God necessarily good? Is not the devil necessarily wicked?" what answer would they give? The goodness of God is so bound up with his Godhead that it is no more necessary for him to be God than to be good; whereas the devil, by his fall, was so estranged from goodness that he can do nothing but evil. If anyone should utter the profane jeer (see Calvin, Against Pighius) that little praise is due to God for a goodness to which he is forced, is not the answer obvious to everyone — that it is owing not to violent impulse, but to his boundless goodness, that he cannot do evil? Therefore, if God's free will in doing good is not hindered because he necessarily must do good; if the devil, who can do nothing but evil, nevertheless sins voluntarily; can it be said that man sins any less voluntarily because he is under a necessity of sinning? Augustine consistently proclaims this necessity, and even when pressed by the spiteful quibble of Celestius, he did not hesitate to assert it in these terms: "Man through liberty became a sinner, but corruption, following as the penalty, has turned liberty into necessity" (Augustine, On the Perfection of Righteousness). Whenever the subject comes up, he does not hesitate to speak in this way of the necessary bondage of sin (Augustine, On Nature and Grace, and elsewhere). Let this, then, be taken as the sum of the distinction. Man, since he was corrupted by the fall, sins not by force or unwillingly, but voluntarily, by a most eager bent of the mind; not by violent compulsion or external force, but by the movement of his own passion; and yet such is the depravity of his nature that he cannot move and act except in the direction of evil. If this is true, what is plainly expressed is that he is under a necessity of sinning. Bernard, agreeing with Augustine, writes: "Among animals, man alone is free; and yet, sin intervening, he suffers a kind of violence, but a violence proceeding from his will, not from nature, so that it does not even deprive him of his innate liberty" (Bernard, Sermons on the Song of Songs, 81). For what is voluntary is also free. A little later he adds, "Thus, in some strange and wicked way, the will itself, being made worse by sin, creates a necessity; but in such a way that the necessity, inasmuch as it is voluntary, cannot excuse the will, and the will, inasmuch as it is enticed, cannot exclude the necessity." For this necessity is in a certain manner voluntary. He afterward says that "we are under a yoke, but no other yoke than that of voluntary servitude; therefore, in respect of servitude, we are miserable, and in respect of will, inexcusable; because the will, when it was free, made itself the slave of sin." At length he concludes, "Thus the soul, in some strange and evil way, is held under this kind of voluntary, yet sadly free, necessity, both bond and free: bond in respect of necessity, free in respect of will; and, what is still more strange and still more miserable, it is guilty because free, and enslaved because guilty, and therefore enslaved because free." My readers see from this that the doctrine I am setting forth is not new, but is the doctrine that Augustine taught long ago with the consent of all the godly, and which was afterward shut up in the cloisters of monks for almost a thousand years. Lombard, by not knowing how to distinguish between necessity and compulsion, gave occasion to a destructive error. The French adds, "Qui a esté une peste mortelle ˆ l'Eglise, d'estimer que l'homme pouvoit eviter le peché pource qu'il peche franchement;" which has been a deadly plague to the Church — namely, the notion that man could avoid sin because he sins freely.

6¶

On the other hand, it may be proper to consider what remedy divine grace provides for the correction and cure of natural corruption. Since the Lord, in bringing us help, supplies what we lack, the nature of that help will at once make plain its opposite — namely, our poverty. When the Apostle says to the Philippians, "Being confident of this very thing, that he which has begun a good work in you, will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:6), there can be no doubt that by the good work thus begun he means the very beginning of conversion in the will. God, therefore, begins the good work in us by awakening in our hearts a desire, a love, and a zeal for righteousness, or (to speak more correctly) by turning, training, and guiding our hearts toward righteousness; and he completes this good work by confirming us to perseverance. But lest anyone should quibble that the good work thus begun by the Lord consists in helping the will, which is weak in itself, the Spirit declares elsewhere what the will is able to do when left to itself. His words are, "A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh. And I will put my Spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them" (Ezekiel 36:26, 27). How can it be said that the weakness of the human will is helped so as to enable it to aspire effectively to the choice of good, when the fact is that it must be wholly transformed and renewed? If there is any softness in a stone; if you can make it tender and bendable into any shape, then it may be said that the human heart can be shaped for uprightness, provided that what is imperfect in it is supplemented by divine grace. But if the Spirit, by this comparison, meant to show that no good can ever be drawn from our heart until it is made altogether new, let us not try to share with Him what He claims for himself alone. If it is like turning a stone into flesh when God turns us to the pursuit of uprightness, then everything proper to our own will is abolished, and what takes its place is wholly of God. I say the will is abolished, but not insofar as it is will, for in conversion everything essential to our original nature remains. I also say that it is created anew, not because the will then begins to exist, but because it is turned from evil to good. This, I maintain, is wholly the work of God, because, as the Apostle testifies, we are not "sufficient of ourselves to think any thing as of ourselves" (2 Corinthians 3:5). Accordingly, he says elsewhere not merely that God assists the weak will or corrects the depraved will, but that he works in us to will (Philippians 2:13). From this it is easily inferred, as I have said, that everything good in the will is entirely the result of grace. In the same sense the Apostle says elsewhere, "It is the same God which worketh all in all" (1 Corinthians 12:6). For he is not treating there of God's universal government, but declaring that all the good qualities believers possess are due to God. In using the term "all," he certainly makes God the author of spiritual life from its beginning to its end. He had taught this earlier in different terms, when he said that there is "one Lord Jesus Christ, by whom are all things, and we by him" (1 Corinthians 8:6), thus plainly exalting the new creation, by which everything of our common nature is destroyed. There is here an implied contrast between Adam and Christ, which he explains more clearly elsewhere when he says, "We are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God has before ordained that we should walk in them" (Ephesians 2:10). In this way he means to show that our salvation is free, because the beginning of goodness comes from the second creation, which is obtained in Christ. If there were any ability in ourselves, even the smallest, there would also be some merit. But to show our utter destitution, he argues that we merit nothing, because we are created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God has prepared; again indicating by these words that all the fruits of good works come originally and immediately from God. Hence the Psalmist, after saying that the Lord "has made us," immediately adds, to deprive us of all share in the work, "not we ourselves." That he is speaking of regeneration, which is the beginning of the spiritual life, is obvious from the context, where the next words are, "we are his people, and the sheep of his pasture" (Psalm 100:3). Not content with simply giving God the praise for our salvation, he expressly excludes us from any share in it, just as if he had said that not one particle remains to man as a ground for boasting. The whole is of God.

7¶

But perhaps there will be some who, while they admit that the will is by its own nature averse to righteousness and is converted solely by the power of God, will still hold that once it has been prepared it plays a part in the action. They base this on the words of Augustine, that grace goes before every good work, the will accompanying, not leading; a handmaid, and not a guide (Augustine, To Boniface, Letter 106). These words, which the holy writer used not improperly, Lombard absurdly twists to support this view (Lombard, book 2, Distinction 25). But I maintain that, both in the words of the Psalmist I have quoted and in other passages of Scripture, two things are clearly taught — namely, that the Lord both corrects, or rather destroys, our depraved will, and also puts in its place a good will from himself. Insofar as the will is preceded by grace, I have no objection to your calling it a handmaid; but insofar as, when it is formed again, it is the work of the Lord, it is wrong to say that it accompanies prevenient grace as a voluntary attendant. Chrysostom is therefore inaccurate when he says that grace cannot do anything without the will, nor the will anything without grace (Sermon on the Finding of the Holy Cross), as if grace did not, in the terms of the passage just quoted from Paul, produce the very will itself. Augustine's intention in calling the human will the handmaid of grace was not to assign it a kind of second place beside grace in the performance of good works. His aim was simply to refute the pestilent dogma of Pelagius, who made human merit the first cause of salvation. As was sufficient for his purpose at the time, he contends that grace comes before all merit, while in the meantime he says nothing of the other question, concerning the continuing effect of grace, which, however, he handles admirably in other places. For when he says, as he often does, that the Lord goes before the unwilling to make him willing, and follows the willing so that he may not will in vain, he makes Him the sole author of good works. Indeed, his views on this subject are too clear to need any lengthy illustration. "Men," he says, "labor to find in our will something that is our own and not God's; how they can find it, I do not know" (Augustine, On the Forgiveness of Sins, book 2, chapter 18). In his first book against Pelagius and Celestius, explaining the saying of Christ, "Every man therefore that has heard, and has learned of the Father, cometh unto me" (John 6:45), he says, "The will is helped not only to know what is to be done, but also to do what it knows." And so, when God teaches not by the letter of the Law but by the grace of the Spirit, he teaches in such a way that everyone who has learned not only sees by knowing, but also desires by willing, and performs by acting.

8¶

Since we are now dealing with the chief point on which the controversy turns, let us give the reader the sum of the matter in a few passages of Scripture, and those the most unambiguous; and then, lest anyone should accuse us of distorting Scripture, let us show that the truth which we maintain is drawn from Scripture is not without the support of the testimony of this holy man (I mean Augustine). I consider it unnecessary to bring forward every separate passage of Scripture in confirmation of my doctrine. A selection of the choicest passages will prepare the way for understanding all those that lie scattered here and there throughout the sacred volume. On the other hand, I thought it not out of place to show my agreement with a man whose authority rightly carries so much weight in the Christian world. It is certainly easy to prove that the beginning of good is with God alone, and that none but the elect have a will inclined to good. But the cause of election must be sought outside of man; and hence it follows that a right will comes not from man himself, but from the same good pleasure by which we were chosen before the creation of the world. Another argument closely related to this may be added. Since the beginning of right willing and acting is of faith, we must see where faith itself comes from. But since Scripture proclaims throughout that faith is the free gift of God, it follows that when men, who are by nature prone to evil with their whole soul, begin to have a good will, it is owing to grace alone. Therefore, when the Lord, in the conversion of his people, sets down these two things as necessary — namely, to take away the heart of stone and to give a heart of flesh — he openly declares that, for our conversion to righteousness, what is ours must be taken away, and that what is put in its place is from himself. Nor does he declare this in one passage only. For he says in Jeremiah, "I will give them one heart, and one way, that they may fear me for ever"; and a little later he says, "I will put my fear in their hearts, that they shall not depart from me" (Jeremiah 32:39, 40). Again, in Ezekiel: "I will give them one heart, and I will put a new spirit within you; and I will take the stony heart out of their flesh, and will give them an heart of flesh" (Ezekiel 11:19). He could not more clearly claim for himself, and deny to us, everything good and right in our will than by declaring that in our conversion there is the creation of a new spirit and a new heart. It always follows both that nothing good can come from our will until it is formed again, and that after it is formed again, insofar as it is good, it is of God and not of us.

9¶

The prayers of the saints likewise agree with this view. So Solomon prays that the Lord may "incline our hearts unto him, to walk in his ways, and keep his commandments" (1 Kings 8:58), indicating that our heart is perverse and naturally gives itself to rebellion against the divine law until it is turned. Again, it is said in the Psalms, "Incline my heart unto thy testimonies" (Psalm 119:36). For we should always note the contrast between the rebellious movement of the heart and the correction by which it is brought under to obedience. David, feeling for a time that he was deprived of directing grace, prays, "Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me" (Psalm 51:10). Is this not an acknowledgment that every part of the heart is full of impurity, and that the soul has received a twist that has turned it from straight to crooked? And then, in describing the cleansing that he earnestly asks for as something to be created by God, does he not ascribe the work entirely to Him? If it is objected that the prayer itself is a sign of a godly and holy affection, it is easy to reply that although David had already repented to some degree, he was here contrasting the sad fall he had experienced with his former state. Therefore, speaking in the person of a man estranged from God, he rightly prays for the blessings that God bestows on his elect in regeneration. Accordingly, like one who is dead, he desires to be created anew, so as to become an instrument of the Holy Spirit instead of a slave of Satan. Strange and monstrous are the cravings of our pride. There is nothing the Lord commands more strictly than the religious observance of his Sabbath, in other words, resting from our works; yet in nothing do we show greater reluctance than in renouncing our own works and giving the works of God their proper place. If arrogance did not stand in the way, we could not overlook the clear testimony Christ has borne to the power of his grace. "I," he said, "am the true vine, and my Father is the husbandman." "As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, except it abide in the vine; no more can ye, except ye abide in me" (John 15:1, 4). If we can no more bear fruit of ourselves than a vine can bud when it is uprooted and deprived of moisture, there is no longer any room to ask what capacity our nature has for good. There is no ambiguity in the conclusion: "For without me ye can do nothing." He does not say that we are too weak to be sufficient for ourselves; but by reducing us to nothing he excludes the idea that we possess any ability, even the least. If, when engrafted into Christ, we bear fruit like the vine, which draws its power of growth from the moisture of the ground, the dew of heaven, and the nourishing warmth of the sun, I see nothing in a good work that we can call our own without encroaching on what is due to God. It is useless to resort to the frivolous quibble that the sap and the power of producing are already contained in the vine, and that therefore, instead of drawing everything from the earth or the original root, it contributes something of its own. Our Savior's words simply mean that when we are separated from him we are nothing but dry, useless wood, because when so separated we have no power to do good, as he says elsewhere, "Every plant which my heavenly Father has not planted, shall be rooted up" (Matthew 15:13). Accordingly, in the passage already quoted from the Apostle Paul, he attributes the whole operation to God: "It is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure" (Philippians 2:13). The first part of a good work is the will; the second is vigorous effort in doing it. French: "La premiere partie des bonnes œuvres est la volonté; l'autre est de s'efforcer a l'executer et le pouvoir faire." — The first part of good works is the will; the second is the attempt to carry it out, and the power to do so.

10¶

This movement of the will is not of the kind that was taught and believed for many ages — namely, a movement that afterward leaves us the choice to obey or resist it — but one that acts on us effectually. We must therefore reject the often-repeated saying of Chrysostom, "Whom he draws, he draws willingly," which suggests that the Lord only stretches out his hand and waits to see whether we will be pleased to accept his help. We grant that man, as he was originally made, could incline to either side; but since he has taught us by his example how miserable a thing free will is if God does not work in us to will and to do, what use to us would grace be if it were imparted in so scanty a measure? Indeed, by our own ingratitude we obscure and weaken divine grace. The Apostle's doctrine is not that the grace of a good will is offered to us if we will accept it, but that God himself is pleased so to work in us as to guide, turn, and govern our heart by his Spirit, and to reign in it as his own possession. Ezekiel promises that a new spirit will be given to the elect, not merely so that they may be able to walk in his precepts, but so that they may actually walk in them (Ezekiel 11:19; 36:27). And the only meaning that can be given to our Savior's words, "Every man, therefore, that has heard and learned of the Father, cometh unto me" (John 6:45), is that the grace of God is effectual in itself. Augustine maintains this in his book De Prædestinatione Sancta. This grace is not bestowed on all indiscriminately, according to the common maxim (of Occam, if I am not mistaken) that it is not denied to anyone who does what is in him. Men are indeed to be taught that the favor of God is offered, without exception, to all who ask for it; but since only those begin to ask whom heaven inspires by grace, even this tiny portion of praise must not be withheld from him. It is the privilege of the elect to be regenerated by the Spirit of God and then placed under his guidance and government. For this reason Augustine rightly ridicules some who claim for themselves a certain power of willing, and also censures others who imagine that what is a special evidence of free election is given to all (Augustine, On the Words of the Apostle, Sermon 21). He says, "Nature is common to all, but not grace"; and he calls it a showy cleverness "which shines by mere vanity, when what God bestows on whom he will is attributed generally to all." Elsewhere he says, "How did you come? By believing. Beware lest, by claiming for yourself the merit of finding the right way, you perish from the right way. I came, you say, by free choice; I came by my own will. Why do you boast? Would you know that even this was given to you? Hear Christ crying out, 'No man cometh unto me, except the Father which has sent me draw him.'" And from the words of John 6:44 he infers it to be an undeniable fact that the hearts of believers are so effectually governed from above that they follow with unswerving affection. "Whosoever is born of God does not commit sin; for his seed remaineth in him" (1 John 3:9). That intermediate movement which the sophists imagine, a movement that everyone is free to obey or to reject, is plainly excluded by the doctrine of effectual perseverance. The French reads: "Nous voyons que ce mouvement sans vertu, lequel imaginent les sophistes, est exclus; J'entend ce qu'ils disent, qu Dieu offre seulement sa grace, a telle condition que chacun la refuse ou accepte selon que bon lui semble. Telle reverie di-je, qui n'est ne chair ne poisson, est exclue, quand il est dit que Dieu nous fait tellement perseverer que nous sommes hors de danger de decliver." — We see that this powerless movement which the sophists imagine is excluded; I mean their dogma that God only offers his grace on such terms that each person may refuse or accept it as seems good to him. Such a fantasy, I say, which is neither fish nor flesh, is excluded when it is said that God makes us persevere in such a way that we are in no danger of falling away.

11¶

As for perseverance, it would undoubtedly have been regarded as the free gift of God, had not the very harmful error prevailed that it is bestowed in proportion to human merit, according to the reception each individual gives to the first grace. Since this gave rise to the idea that it was entirely in our own power to receive or reject the offered grace of God, as soon as that idea is exploded the error built on it must fall. The error, indeed, is twofold. For besides teaching that our gratitude for the first grace and our lawful use of it are rewarded by later supplies of grace, its defenders add that after this grace does not work alone, but only cooperates with ourselves. As to the first point, we must hold that the Lord, while he daily enriches his servants and loads them with new gifts of his grace, finds in them, because he approves of and takes pleasure in the work he has begun, something that he may follow up with larger measures of grace. To this effect are the sayings, "To him that has shall be given," and "Well done, good and faithful servant: thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things" (Matthew 25:21, 23, 29; Luke 19:17, 26). But here two cautions are necessary. It must not be said that the lawful use of the first grace is rewarded by later measures of grace, as if man made the grace of God effectual by his own diligence; nor must it be thought that there is any reward of such a kind as to make it cease to be the free grace of God. I admit, then, that believers may expect as a blessing from God that the better use they make of earlier grace, the larger the supplies of future grace they will receive; but I say that even this use is from the Lord, and that this reward is bestowed freely, out of pure good will. The worn distinction between operating and cooperating grace is used no less perversely than unhappily. Augustine did indeed use it, but he softened it with a suitable definition — namely, that God, by cooperating, completes what he begins by operating; that both graces are the same, but receive different names from the different ways in which they produce their effects. From this it follows that he does not divide the work between God and man, as if a movement proper to each produced a mutual concurrence. All he does is mark a multiplication of grace. To this effect, accordingly, he says elsewhere that in man a good will goes before many gifts from God, but that among these gifts is this good will itself (Augustine, Enchiridion to Laurentius, chapter 32). From this it follows that nothing is left that the will may claim as its own. Paul has stated this expressly. For after saying, "It is God which worketh in you both to will and to do," he immediately adds, "of his good pleasure" (Philippians 2:13), indicating by this expression that the blessing is free. As to the common saying that, after we have given admission to the first grace, our efforts cooperate with later grace, this is my answer: if it means that after we have once been subdued by the power of the Lord to the obedience of righteousness, we go forward voluntarily and are inclined to follow the movement of grace, I have no objection. For it is most certain that where the grace of God reigns, there is also this readiness to obey. And where does this readiness come from, except that the Spirit of God, being everywhere consistent with himself, after first begetting a principle of obedience, nourishes and strengthens it for perseverance? If, on the other hand, it means that man is able of himself to be a fellow worker with the grace of God, I hold it to be a most pestilent delusion.

12¶

In support of this view, some make an ignorant and false application of the Apostle's words: "I labored more abundantly than they all: yet not I, but the grace of God which was with me" (1 Corinthians 15:10). The meaning they give them is that since Paul might have seemed to speak somewhat presumptuously in preferring himself to all the other apostles, he qualifies the expression by referring the praise to the grace of God, yet at the same time calls himself a cooperator with grace. It is strange that this should have been a stumbling block to so many writers who are otherwise respectable. The Apostle does not say that the grace of God labored with him so as to make him a partner in the labor. Rather, he transfers the whole merit of the labor to grace alone, by qualifying his first expression in this way: "It was not I," he says, "that labored, but the grace of God that was present with me." Those who have adopted the mistaken interpretation have been misled by an ambiguity in the expression, or rather by an absurd translation in which the force of the Greek article is overlooked. For if we take the words literally, the Apostle does not say that grace was a fellow worker with him, but that the grace which was with him was the sole worker. And Augustine teaches this plainly, though briefly, when he says, "Good will in man goes before many gifts from God, but not all gifts, since the will that goes before is itself among their number." He adds the reason: "for it is written, 'the God of my mercy shall prevent me' (Psalm 59:10), and 'Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me' (Psalm 23:6); it goes before him who is unwilling, and makes him willing; it follows him who is willing, so that he may not will in vain." Bernard agrees with this, presenting the Church as praying in this way: "Draw me, who am in some measure unwilling, and make me willing; draw me, who am lagging sluggishly, and make me run" (Sermon 2 on the Song of Songs).

13¶

Let us now hear Augustine in his own words, lest the Pelagians of our age — I mean the sophists of the Sorbonne — accuse us, as is their habit, of being opposed to all antiquity. In this, indeed, they imitate their father Pelagius, who long ago brought a similar charge against Augustine. In the second chapter of his treatise De Correptione et Gratis, addressed to Valentinus, Augustine explains at length what I will state briefly, but in his own words: that to Adam was given the grace of persevering in goodness if he had the will; that to us it is given to will, and by the will to overcome concupiscence; that Adam, therefore, had the power if he had the will, but did not will to have the power, whereas to us both the will and the power are given; that the original freedom of man was to be able not to sin, but that we have a much greater freedom — namely, not to be able to sin. And lest it be supposed, as Lombard mistakenly does (book 2, Distinction 25), that he is speaking of the perfection of the future state, he removes all doubt shortly afterward when he says, "For the will of the saints is so inflamed by the Holy Spirit that they are able because they are willing, and willing because God works in them so to will." For if, in such weakness (in which, however, to suppress pride, "strength" must be made "perfect"), their own will were left to them in such a sense that, by the help of God, they are able if they will, while God does not at the same time work in them to make them will, then amid so many temptations and infirmities the will itself would give way, and consequently they would not be able to persevere. Therefore, to meet the weakness of the human will and keep it from failing, however weak it might be, divine grace was made to act on it inseparably and without interruption. Augustine (same work, chapter 14), next going fully into the question of how our hearts follow the movement when God acts on them, necessarily says, indeed, that the Lord draws men by their own wills — wills, however, which he himself has produced. We now have Augustine's testimony to the truth we are especially concerned to maintain — namely, that the grace offered by the Lord is not merely one which every individual has full liberty to receive or reject, but a grace which produces in the heart both choice and will; so that all the good works that follow are its fruit and effect, the only will that yields obedience being the will that grace itself has made. In another place Augustine uses these words: "Every good work in us is performed only by grace" (Augustine, Letter 105).

14¶

When he says elsewhere that the will is not taken away by grace, but is changed from bad into good, and after it is good is assisted, he means only that man is not drawn as if by an external impulse (French, "Come une pierre" — like a stone).

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Every fruit of our corrupt nature, Calvin teaches, is damnable in itself — intellect and will alike so spoiled that no soundness, no true fear of God, survives the fall. Yet he is no cynic about the heathen virtues; he grants the admirable endowments that shone in pagan lives, while denying they could commend such men to God, since the root was unrenewed. For 2026 this rebukes both the believer's self-righteousness and the world's confidence in mere decency. Outward virtue, civic kindness, the respectable life — God may restrain us by these for the common good, but they do not save, and conversion is no polishing of the old will but its rebirth by grace. The remedy is wholly God's: he converts, he prepares, he inclines the heart to good in his elect. So we labor in the home to form good habits, yet we never mistake good behavior for a new heart — and we throw ourselves and our children entirely on the God who alone makes the will sound.
