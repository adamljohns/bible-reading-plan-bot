State in Which Man Was Created — The Faculties of the Soul — The Image of God — Free Will — Original Righteousness

This chapter is thus divided:—I. The necessary rules to be observed in considering the state of man before the fall being laid down, the point first considered is the creation of the body, and the lesson taught by its being formed out of the earth, and made alive, sec. 1. II. The immortality of the human soul is proved by various solid arguments, sec. 2. III. The image of God (the strongest proof of the soul’s immortality) is considered, and various absurd fancies are refuted, sec. 3. IV. Several errors which obscure the light of truth being dissipated, follows a philosophical and theological consideration of the faculties of the soul before the fall.

1¶

We must now speak of the creation of man, not only because of all the works of God it is the noblest and the most admirable display of his justice, wisdom, and goodness, but because, as we observed at the outset, we cannot clearly and properly know God unless the knowledge of ourselves is added to it. This knowledge is twofold: it relates, first, to the condition in which we were originally created, and second, to our condition as it began to be immediately after Adam's fall. For it would do us little good to know how we were created if we remained ignorant of the corruption and degradation of our nature that followed the fall. For the present, however, we confine ourselves to considering our nature in its original integrity. And certainly, before we come down to the miserable condition into which man has fallen, it is important to consider what he was at first. For caution is needed, lest we attend only to man's natural ills and thereby seem to ascribe them to the Author of nature. Ungodliness thinks it a sufficient defense if it can claim that everything corrupt in nature proceeded in some sense from God, and when it is accused, it does not hesitate to plead against God and throw the blame for its guilt on him. Those who wish to be thought to speak more reverently of the Deity seize on nature as an excuse for their depravity, not considering that they too, though more obscurely, bring a charge against God, on whom the dishonor would fall if anything corrupt were proved to exist in nature. Seeing, therefore, that the flesh is always on the lookout for subterfuges by which it imagines it can shift the blame for its own wickedness from itself to some other quarter, we must diligently guard against this depraved procedure. We must therefore treat the calamity of the human race in such a way as to cut off every evasion and vindicate the justice of God against all who would attack it. We shall see later, in its own place (Book 2, chapter 1, section 3), how far mankind now is from the purity originally conferred on Adam. And first, it should be observed that when he was formed out of the dust of the ground, a curb was laid on his pride — for nothing is more absurd than that those should glory in their excellence who not only dwell in tabernacles of clay, but are themselves in part dust and ashes. But since God not only deigned to give life to a vessel of clay, but also made it the dwelling of an immortal spirit, Adam might well glory in the great generosity of his Maker. On man's first origin, see Calvin against Pighius; and on the immortality of the soul, see Calvin's Psychopannychia and Instructio adv. Libertinos, chapters 9, 11, 12. It is striking to see how widely the opinion of Pliny differs from Christian doctrine: "Omnibus a suprema die eadem quæ ante primam; hic magis a morte sensus ullus aut corpori aut animæ quam ante natales. Eadem enim vanitas in futurum etiam se propagat et in mortis quoque tempora ipsa sibi vitam mentitur." — Pliny, Natural History, book 7, chapter 56.

2¶

Further, there can be no question that man consists of a body and a soul, meaning by soul an immortal yet created essence, which is his nobler part. Sometimes it is called a spirit. Though the two terms differ in meaning when they are used together, still, when spirit is used by itself it is equivalent to soul, as when Solomon, speaking of death, says that the spirit returns to God who gave it (Ecclesiastes 12:7). And when Christ commends his spirit to the Father, and Stephen his to Christ, they simply mean that when the soul is freed from the prison-house of the body, God becomes its perpetual keeper. Those who imagine that the soul is called a spirit because it is a breath or energy divinely infused into bodies, but without essence, err too grossly, as is shown both by the nature of the thing and by the whole tenor of Scripture. It is true, indeed, that men who cling too much to the earth are dull of understanding; indeed, being estranged from the Father of Lights, they are so sunk in darkness as to imagine that they will not survive the grave. Yet the light is not so completely put out in the darkness that all sense of immortality is lost. Conscience, which distinguishes between good and evil and answers to the judgment of God, is an unmistakable sign of an immortal spirit. How could a motion without essence reach the judgment seat of God, and, under a sense of guilt, strike itself with terror? The body cannot be affected by any fear of spiritual punishment. This belongs only to the soul, which must therefore be endowed with essence. Then the mere knowledge of a God sufficiently proves that souls which rise higher than the world must be immortal, since no fleeting energy could reach the very fountain of life. In short, while the many noble faculties with which the human mind is endowed proclaim that something divine is engraved on it, they are so many evidences of an immortal essence. For such sense as the lower animals possess does not go beyond the body, or at least not beyond the objects actually set before it. But the swiftness with which the human mind glances from heaven to earth, searches the secrets of nature, and, after it has taken in all ages, sets each in its proper order by intellect and memory, and reads the future in the past, clearly shows that there lies hidden in man something separate from the body. We have an intellect by which we are able to conceive of the invisible God and of angels — something of which the body is altogether incapable. We have ideas of uprightness, justice, and honesty — ideas which the bodily senses cannot reach. The seat of these ideas must therefore be a spirit. Indeed, sleep itself, which stupefies a man and seems even to deprive him of life, is no obscure evidence of immortality, since it not only suggests thoughts of things that never existed, but foreshadows future events. I touch only briefly on topics which even secular writers describe with more splendid eloquence. For godly readers a simple reference is enough. If the soul were not some kind of essence separate from the body, Scripture would not teach Job 4:19; 2 Cor. 5:4; 2 Pet. 1:13, 14; 2 Cor. 5:10; 7:1; 1 Pet. 2:25; 1:9; 2:11; Heb. 13:17; 2 Cor. 1:23; Matt. 10:28; Luke 12:5; Heb. 12:9; Luke 16:22; 2 Cor. 5:6, 8; Acts 23:8.

3¶

A strong proof of this point may be drawn from the statement that man was created in the image of God. For though the divine glory shines in man's outward appearance, there can be no doubt that the proper seat of the image is in the soul. I do not deny, indeed, that the outward shape, insofar as it distinguishes and separates us from the lower animals, brings us nearer to God; nor will I strongly oppose any who may choose to include under the image of God that

"While the mute creation downward bend

Their sight, and to their earthly mother tend,

Man looks aloft, and with erected eyes,

Beholds his own hereditary skies." Ovid, Metamorphoses, book 1. — Dryden's translation.

Only let it be understood that the image of God which is seen or made conspicuous by these outward marks is spiritual. For Osiander (whose writings show a perverse ingenuity in futile inventions), extending the image of God indiscriminately to the body as well as to the soul, confuses heaven with earth. He says that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit placed their image in man because, even if Adam had remained in his integrity, Christ would still have become man. So, according to him, the body destined for Christ was the model and pattern of the bodily form which was then fashioned. But where does he find that Christ is an image of the Spirit? I grant, indeed, that in the person of the Mediator the glory of the whole Godhead is displayed; but how can the eternal Word, who in order precedes the Spirit, be called his image? In short, the distinction between the Son and the Spirit is destroyed when the former is represented as the image of the latter. Moreover, I should like to know in what respect Christ, in the flesh with which he was clothed, resembles the Holy Spirit, and by what marks or features the likeness is expressed. And since the expression, "Let us make man in our own image," is spoken in the person of the Son also, it follows that he is the image of himself — a thing utterly absurd. Add that, according to the fiction of Osiander, As to Osiander's absurd fancy, see Book 2, chapter 12, section 5 and following. In Romans 8:3, Christ is said to have been sent by the Father in the likeness of sinful flesh, but nowhere is Adam said to have been formed in the likeness of Christ's future flesh, although Tertullian says so somewhere.

4¶

But our definition of the image does not seem complete until it appears more clearly what the faculties are in which man excels, and in which he is to be regarded as a mirror of the divine glory. This, however, cannot be better learned than from the remedy provided for the corruption of nature. There can be no doubt that when Adam lost his first estate he became estranged from God. Therefore, although we grant that the image of God was not utterly effaced and destroyed in him, it was nevertheless so corrupted that whatever remains is a fearful deformity. And so our deliverance begins with that renewal which we obtain from Christ, who is for this reason called the second Adam, because he restores us to true and substantial integrity. For although Paul, contrasting the life-giving Spirit which believers receive from Christ with the living soul which Adam was created (1 Corinthians 15:45), commends the richer measure of grace bestowed in regeneration, he does not for all that contradict the statement that the goal of regeneration is to form us anew in the image of God. Accordingly, he shows elsewhere that the new man is renewed after the image of him who created him (Colossians 3:19). To this corresponds another passage: "Put on the new man, who after God is created" (Ephesians 4:24). We must now see what particulars Paul includes under this renewal. In the first place he mentions knowledge, and in the second, true righteousness and holiness. From this we infer that at the beginning the image of God was manifested in light of intellect, uprightness of heart, and the soundness of every part. For though I admit that the forms of expression are elliptical, this principle cannot be overthrown — namely, that what is chief in the renewal of the divine image must also have held the highest place in its creation. To the same effect Paul says elsewhere that, beholding the glory of Christ with unveiled face, we are transformed into the same image. We now see how Christ is the most perfect image of God, into which we are so renewed as to bear the image of God in knowledge, purity, righteousness, and true holiness. Once this is established, Osiander's fancy about bodily form vanishes of its own accord. As to that passage of St. Paul (1 Corinthians 11:7) in which the man alone, to the express exclusion of the woman, is called the image and glory of God, it is evident from the context that it refers merely to civil order. I take it that it has already been sufficiently proved that the image includes everything which has any relation to spiritual and eternal life. The same thing is declared in different terms by St. John when he says that the light which was from the beginning, in the eternal Word of God, was the light of man (John 1:4). Since his purpose is to extol the singular grace of God in making man excel the other animals, he shows at the same time how man was formed in the image of God, in order to set him apart from the common herd as possessing not a mere animal existence, but one which joins with it the light of intelligence. Therefore, as the image of God constitutes the entire excellence of human nature, as it shone in Adam before his fall, but was afterward corrupted and almost destroyed, so that nothing remains but a ruin, confused, mutilated, and tainted with impurity, so it is now partly seen in the elect, insofar as they are regenerated by the Spirit. Its full splendor, however, will be displayed in heaven. But in order to know the particular properties in which it consists, it will be proper to treat of the faculties of the soul. For there is no solidity in Augustine's speculation, See Augustine, On the Trinity, book 10, and The City of God, book 11. See further Calvin, in Psychopannychia and in the Commentary on Genesis.

5¶

But before I go further, it is necessary to deal with the dream of the Manichees, which Servetus has tried in our day to revive. Because it is said that God breathed into man's nostrils the breath of life (Genesis 2:7), they thought that the soul was a transmission of the substance of God, as if some portion of the boundless divinity had passed into man. It will not take long to show how many gross and foul absurdities this devilish error drags in its train. For if the soul of man is a portion transmitted from the essence of God, the divine nature must be subject not only to passion and change, but also to ignorance, evil desires, weakness, and every kind of vice. Nothing is more inconstant than man; contrary impulses agitate and distract his soul. Again and again he is misled by lack of skill and overcome by the slightest temptations, while everyone feels that the soul itself is a receptacle for every kind of pollution. All these things must be attributed to the divine nature if we hold that the soul is of the essence of God, or a secret inflowing of divinity. Who does not shudder at a thing so monstrous? Paul, indeed, quoting Aratus, tells us that we are his offspring (Acts 17:28); not in substance, however, but in quality, inasmuch as he has adorned us with divine endowments. Meanwhile, to tear apart the essence of the Creator in order to assign a portion to each individual is the height of madness. It must therefore be held as certain that souls, although the divine image is engraved on them, are created just as angels are. Creation, however, is not a transfusion of essence, The French adds, "comme si on tiroit le vin d'un vaisseau en une bouteille;" — as if one were to draw wine out of a cask into a bottle.

The French reads, "qu'il le coupe de sa substance comme une branche d'arbre;" — that he cuts it from his substance like a branch from a tree.

6¶

It would be useless to look to the philosophers for a definition of the soul, since not one of them, with the exception of Plato, clearly maintained its immortality. Others of the school of Socrates, indeed, lean the same way, but still without clearly teaching a doctrine of which they were not fully persuaded. Plato, however, went still further and regarded the soul as an image of God. Others so tie its powers and faculties to the present life that they leave nothing outside the body. Moreover, having already shown from Scripture that the substance of the soul is incorporeal, we must now add that, though it is not properly enclosed by space, it nevertheless occupies the body as a kind of dwelling, not only giving life to all its parts and making the organs fit and useful for their actions, but also holding the first place in governing conduct. It does this not merely with regard to the duties of earthly life, but also with regard to the service of God. Though this is not clearly seen in our corrupt state, yet the imprint of its remains is seen in our very vices. For where do men get such a thirst for glory, if not from a sense of shame? And where does this sense of shame come from, if not from a regard for what is honorable? The first principle and source of this is an awareness that they were born to cultivate righteousness — an awareness akin to religion. But as man was undoubtedly created to meditate on the heavenly life, so it is certain that the knowledge of it was engraved on the soul. And indeed, man would lack the principal use of his understanding if he were unable to discern his happiness, the perfection of which consists in being united to God. Hence the principal action of the soul is to aspire toward that end; and accordingly, the more a man strives to draw near to God, the more he proves himself to be endowed with reason.

Though there is some plausibility in the opinion of those who maintain that man has more than one soul, namely, a sentient soul and a rational one, yet since there is no soundness in their arguments, we must reject it, unless we wish to torment ourselves with frivolous and useless things. They tell us (see chapter 5, section 4) that there is a great conflict between the organic movements and the rational part of the soul. As if reason too were not at odds with herself, and her counsels did not sometimes clash with one another like hostile armies! But since this disorder results from the corruption of nature, it is a mistake to infer that there are two souls because the faculties do not agree as harmoniously as they ought. But I leave it to the philosophers to discuss these faculties more subtly. For the edification of the godly a simple definition will be enough. I admit, indeed, that what they teach so ingeniously on the subject is true, and not only pleasant but also useful to know; nor do I forbid anyone who is so inclined to pursue the study. First, I grant that there are five senses, which Plato (in the Theaetetus) prefers to call organs, by which all objects are brought into a common sensorium, as into a kind of receptacle: The French reads, "Et que par iceux comme par canaux, tous objects qui se presentent à la veuë, au goust, ou au flair, ou a l'attouchement distillent au sens commun, comme en une cisteren qui recoit d'un coté et d'autre." — "And that by them as by channels, all objects which present themselves to the sight, taste, smell, or touch, drop into the common sensorium, as into a cistern which receives on either side."

See Aristotle, Ethics, book 1, last chapter; likewise book 6, chapter 2.

Though these things are true, or at least plausible, still, since I fear they are more likely to entangle us by their obscurity than to help us, I think it best to leave them aside. If anyone chooses to divide the powers of the mind in a different way, calling one appetitive, which, though without reason in itself, yet obeys reason if directed from another quarter, and another intellectual, as being of itself a partaker of reason, I have no great objection. Nor am I inclined to quarrel with the view that there are three principles of action — namely, sense, intellect, and appetite. But let us rather adopt a division suited to every capacity — something which certainly cannot be obtained from the philosophers. 7¶

From this method of teaching we are forced to differ somewhat. For the philosophers, being unacquainted with the corruption of nature, which is the punishment of revolt, mistakenly confuse two states of man that are very different from each other. Let us therefore hold, for the purpose of the present work, that the soul consists of two parts, the intellect and the will (Book 2, chapter 2, sections 2, 12). The office of the intellect is to distinguish between objects, according as they seem to deserve approval or disapproval; and the office of the will is to choose and follow what the intellect declares to be good, and to reject and shun what it declares to be bad (Plato, in the Phaedrus). We do not dwell on the subtlety of Aristotle, that the mind has no motion of itself, but that the moving power is choice, which he also calls the appetitive intellect. Not to lose ourselves in superfluous questions, let it be enough to know that the intellect is to us, as it were, the guide and ruler of the soul, and that the will always waits on its nod and awaits its decision in matters of desire. For this reason Aristotle rightly taught that in the appetite there is a pursuit and an avoidance corresponding in some degree to affirmation and negation in the intellect (Aristotle, Ethics, book 6, section 2). Moreover, it will be seen in another place (Book 2, chapter 2, sections 12-26) how surely the intellect governs the will. Here we only wish to observe that the soul possesses no faculty which may not properly be referred to one or the other of these two parts. And in this way we include sense under intellect. Others make the distinction this way: they say that sense inclines to pleasure in the same way as the intellect inclines to the good; that hence the appetite of sense becomes concupiscence and lust, while the affection of the intellect becomes will. In place of the term appetite, which they prefer, I use the word will, as being more common.

8¶

Therefore God has furnished the soul of man with intellect, by which he might discern good from evil and just from unjust, and might know what to follow or to shun, with reason going before him with her lamp; for this reason the philosophers, with regard to her directing power, have called her τὸ ἑγεμονικὸν. To this he has joined the will, to which choice belongs. Man excelled in these noble endowments in his original condition, when reason, intelligence, prudence, and judgment not only sufficed for the government of his earthly life, but also enabled him to rise up to God and to eternal happiness. Then choice was added to direct the appetites and to regulate all the organic motions, so that the will was perfectly submissive to the authority of reason. In this upright state man possessed freedom of will, by which, if he chose, he was able to obtain eternal life. It would be out of place here to bring in the question of the secret predestination of God, because we are not considering what might or might not have happened, but what the nature of man truly was. Adam, therefore, might have stood if he had chosen, since it was only by his own will that he fell; but it was because his will was pliable in either direction, and he had not received constancy to persevere, that he fell so easily. Still, he had a free choice of good and evil; and not only so, but in the mind and will there was the highest uprightness, and all the organic parts were rightly framed for obedience, until man corrupted its good properties and destroyed himself. Hence the great darkness of the philosophers, who have looked for a complete building in a ruin, and fit arrangement in disorder. The principle they started from was that man could not be a rational animal unless he had a free choice of good and evil. They also imagined that the distinction between virtue and vice was destroyed if man did not order his life by his own counsel. So far, so good, had there been no change in man. But since this was unknown to them, it is not surprising that they throw everything into confusion. But those who profess to be disciples of Christ, and yet still look for free will in man, even though he is lost and drowned in spiritual destruction, labor under manifold delusion; they make a mongrel mixture of inspired doctrine and philosophical opinions, and so go wrong on both. But it will be better to leave these things to their own place (see Book 2, chapter 2). At present it is only necessary to remember that man at his first creation was very different from all his posterity, who, deriving their origin from him after he was corrupted, received a hereditary taint. At first every part of the soul was formed for uprightness. There was soundness of mind and freedom of will to choose the good. If anyone objects that it was placed, as it were, in a slippery position because its power was weak, I answer that the degree conferred was sufficient to take away every excuse. For surely the Deity could not be bound to this condition — to make man such that he either could not or would not sin. Such a nature might have been more excellent; See Augustine, On Genesis, book 11, chapters 7, 8, 9, and On Rebuke and Grace, to Valentinus, chapter 11.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin lifts our gaze to man as first created—a vessel of clay made the dwelling of an immortal soul, stamped with the image of God in knowledge, righteousness, and true holiness, and granted, in his upright state, a will free to choose the good. This is no flattery of human pride but a careful guarding of God's justice: our present corruption is our own fall, not the Maker's design. The lesson cuts sharply against 2026, an age that simultaneously worships the self and despairs of the soul—exalting human autonomy while reducing persons to chemistry and appetite. Calvin will let us do neither: we are dust, yet we bear a defaced glory that only Christ, the second Adam, restores. So teach the young the dignity and the ruin together, that they neither glory in themselves nor doubt their immortal worth. The image we lost in Adam is being renewed in Christ, and will one day shine in heaven in full lustre.
