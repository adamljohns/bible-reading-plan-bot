Book 3 · Chapter 24 · USMC modern English

Election Confirmed by the Calling of God — The Reprobate Bring Upon Themselves the Righteous Destruction to Which They Are Doomed

The title of this chapter shows that it consists of two parts,—I. The case of the Elect, from sec. 1-11. II. The case of the Reprobate, from sec. 12-17. 1. The election of God is secret, but is manifested by effectual calling. The nature of this effectual calling. How election and effectual calling are founded on the free mercy of God. A cavil of certain expositors refuted by the words of Augustine. An exception disposed of. 2. Calling proved to be free, 1. By its nature and the mode in which it is dispensed. 2. By the word of God. 3. By the calling of Abraham, the father of the faithful. 4. By the testimony of John. 5 . By the example of those who have been called. 3. The pure doctrine of the calling of the elect misunderstood, 1. By those who attribute too much to the human will. 2. By those who make election dependent on faith. This error amply refuted. 4. In this and the five following sections the certainty of election vindicated from the assaults of Satan. The leading arguments are:1. Effectual calling. 2. Christ apprehended by faith. 3. The protection of Christ, the guardian of the elect. We must not attempt to penetrate to the hidden recesses of the divine wisdom, in order to learn what is decreed with regard to us at the judgment-seat. We must begin and end with the call of God. This confirmed by an apposite saying of Bernard. 5. Christ the foundation of this calling and election. He who does not lean on him alone cannot be certain of his election. He is the faithful interpreter of the eternal counsel in regard to our salvation. 6. Another security of our election is the protection of Christ our Shepherd. How it is manifested to us. Objection 1. As to the future state. 2. As to perseverance. Both objections refuted. 7. Objection, that those who seem elected sometimes fall away. Answer. A passage of Paul dissuading us from security explained. The kind of fear required in the elect. 8. Explanation of the saying, that many are called, but few chosen. A twofold call. 9. Explanation of the passage, that none is lost but the son of perdition. Refutation of an objection to the certainty of election. 10. Explanation of the passages urged against the certainty of election. Examples by which some attempt to prove that the seed of election is sown in the hearts of the elect from their very birth. Answer. 1. One or two examples do not make the rule. 2. This view opposed to Scripture. 3. Is expressly opposed by an apostle. 11. An explanation and confirmation of the third answer. 12. Second part of the chapter, which treats of the reprobate. Some of them God deprives of the opportunity of hearing his word. Others he blinds and stupefies the more by the preaching of it. 13. Of this no other account can be given than that the reprobate are vessels fitted for destruction. This confirmed by the case of the elect; of Pharaoh and of the Jewish people both before and after the manifestation of Christ. 14. Question, Why does God blind the reprobate? Two answers. These confirmed by different passages of Scripture. Objection of the reprobate. Answer. 15. Objection to this doctrine of the righteous rejection of the reprobate. The first founded on a passage in Ezekiel. The passage explained. 16. A second objection founded on a passage in Paul. The apostle’s meaning explained. A third objection and fourth objection answered. 17. A fifth objection—viz. that there seems to be a twofold will in God. Answer. Other objections and answers. Conclusion.

1¶

To set out this subject more fully, we must deal with two things: the calling of the elect, and the blinding and hardening of the ungodly. I have already touched on the first to some degree (in chapter 22, sections 10 and 11), when I refuted the error of those who think that the general terms in which the promises are framed place the whole human race on an equal footing. The special election that would otherwise remain hidden in God he finally makes plain by his calling. "For whom he foreknew, he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son." And further, "whom he predestined, them he also called; and whom he called, them he also justified," so that one day he might glorify them (Romans 8:29-30). Although the Lord, by electing his people, adopted them as his sons, we see that they do not actually take possession of this great good until they are called. But once they are called, the enjoyment of their election is in some measure imparted to them. This is why the Spirit they receive is called by Paul both the "Spirit of adoption" and the "seal" and "down payment" of the inheritance to come. By his testimony he confirms and seals upon their hearts the certainty of their future adoption. For although the preaching of the gospel flows from the fountain of election, yet since it is shared in common with the reprobate, it would not by itself be solid proof. God, however, teaches his elect effectually when he brings them to faith, as we quoted earlier from the words of our Savior: "Not that anyone has seen the Father, except he who is from God; he has seen the Father" (John 6:46). Again, "I have revealed your name to the men you gave me out of the world" (John 17:6). And he says in another place, "No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him" (John 6:44). Augustine ably explains this passage in these words: "If (as Truth says) everyone who has learned comes, then everyone who does not come has not learned. It does not therefore follow that the one who can come does come, unless he has willed and done it. But everyone who has learned from the Father not only can come, but does come."

Scripture cited
Rom 8:29, 30 · John 6:46 · John 17:6 · John 6:44
2¶

This is also clearly shown by the very nature of calling and the way it is administered, for it consists not merely in the preaching of the Word but also in the illumination of the Spirit. Who those are to whom God offers his Word is explained by the prophet: "I was sought by those who did not ask for me; I was found by those who did not seek me. I said, Here I am, here I am, to a nation that was not called by my name" (Isaiah 65:1). And so that the Jews would not suppose this mercy applied only to the Gentiles, he reminds them where it was that he found their father Abraham when he condescended to be his friend (Isaiah 41:8) — namely, in the midst of the idolatry in which he was sunk along with all his people. When God first shines the light of his Word upon those who do not deserve it, he gives clear enough proof of his free goodness. Here, then, boundless goodness is displayed, yet not in such a way as to bring everyone to salvation, since a heavier judgment awaits the reprobate for rejecting the evidence of his love. To display his own glory, God also withholds from them the effectual working of his Spirit. This inward calling, therefore, is an unfailing pledge of salvation. So John says, "By this we know that he abides in us, by the Spirit he has given us" (1 John 3:24). And so that human nature should not boast of at least responding to him when he calls and freely offers himself, he declares that there would be no ears to hear and no eyes to see if he himself did not give them. And he acts not according to each person's gratitude, but according to his election. You have a striking example of this in Luke, where Jews and Gentiles alike heard the address of Paul and Barnabas. Though they were all taught by the same Word, it says that "as many as were appointed to eternal life believed" (Acts 13:48). How can we deny that calling is free, when election alone governs it from beginning to end?

Isaiah 65:1 · Isaiah 41:8 · 1 John 3:24 · Acts 13:48
3¶

Two errors must be avoided here. Some make man a co-worker with God in such a way that man's vote ratifies election, so that, according to them, the human will is superior to the counsel of God. As if Scripture taught that we are merely given the capacity to be able to believe, and not faith itself. Others, although they do not so greatly diminish the grace of the Holy Spirit, yet — moved by I know not what reasoning — make election depend on faith, as though it were uncertain and ineffective until faith confirmed it. There is no doubt that as far as we are concerned, faith does confirm it. Moreover, we have already seen that the secret counsel of God, which lay hidden, is in this way brought to light — meaning nothing more than that what was unknown is now proved and, as it were, sealed. But it is false to say that election becomes effective only after we have embraced the gospel, and that it draws its force from this. It is true that we must look there for our assurance of it, because if we try to penetrate into the secret ordination of God, we will be swallowed up in that bottomless abyss. But once the Lord has revealed it to us, we must climb higher, so that the effect does not bury the cause. For what could be more absurd and improper than this: while Scripture teaches that we are enlightened because God has chosen us, our eyes should be so dazzled by the brightness of this light that we refuse to give any attention to election? Meanwhile, I do not deny that, in order to be assured of our salvation, we must begin with the Word, and that our confidence should go no further than the Word when we call upon God the Father. For some people, to gain more certainty about the counsel of God (which is near us, in our mouth and in our heart — Deuteronomy 30:14), absurdly long to soar above the clouds. We must therefore curb that recklessness with the sobriety of faith, and be content to have God as the witness of his hidden grace in the external Word — provided always that the channel through which the water flows, and from which we may freely drink, does not keep us from giving due honor to the fountain itself.

Deut 30:14
4¶

Therefore, just as those are in error who make the validity of election depend on the faith by which we perceive that we are elect, so we will follow the best order if, in seeking assurance of our election, we hold fast to those later signs that are sure attestations of it. Among the temptations with which Satan assaults believers, none is greater or more dangerous than when, troubling them with doubts about their election, he at the same time prods them with a corrupt desire to inquire into it in the wrong way. By inquiring in the wrong way I mean when feeble man tries to break into the hidden recesses of the divine wisdom, and goes all the way back to the remotest eternity in order to understand what final decision God has made about him. In this way he plunges headlong into an immense abyss, entangles himself in countless inextricable snares, and buries himself in the thickest darkness. For it is right that the dullness of the human mind should be punished with dreadful destruction whenever it tries to rise by its own strength to the height of divine wisdom. And this temptation is all the more deadly because it is the one to which almost all of us are most prone. For there is scarcely a mind in which this thought does not sometimes arise: Where does your salvation come from but from the election of God? But what proof do you have of your election? Once this thought has taken hold of someone, it keeps him perpetually miserable, subjects him to dreadful torment, or throws him into a state of complete numbness. I could not wish for stronger proof of the corrupt notions that people of this kind form about predestination than experience itself supplies, since the mind can be infected by no more deadly error than one that disturbs the conscience and robs it of peace and tranquility before God. Therefore, as we dread shipwreck, we must steer clear of this rock, which is fatal to everyone who strikes it. And though the discussion of predestination is regarded as a dangerous sea, yet sailing over it is calm and safe — even pleasant — provided we do not deliberately court danger. For just as a deadly abyss swallows those who, to be assured of their election, pry into the eternal counsel of God apart from the Word, so those who investigate it rightly, and in the order in which it is set forth in the Word, gather from it rich fruits of comfort.

Let our method of inquiry, then, be to begin with the calling of God and to end with it. Yet there is nothing in this to keep believers from sensing that the blessings they daily receive from God's hand originate in that secret adoption, as they themselves express it in Isaiah: "You have done wonderful things; your counsels of old are faithfulness and truth" (Isaiah 25:1). For with this as a pledge, God is pleased to assure us of as much of his counsel as may lawfully be known. But so that no one should think that this testimony is weak, let us consider how much clarity and certainty it gives us. There is a fitting passage on this subject in Bernard. After speaking of the reprobate, he says: "The purpose of God stands, and the sentence of peace upon those who fear him also stands — a sentence concealing their bad qualities and rewarding their good ones, so that in a marvelous way not only their good but even their bad qualities work together for good. Who will bring any charge against God's elect? It is completely enough for my justification to have him favorable against whom alone I have sinned. Everything he has decreed not to charge against me is as if it had never been." A little later he says: "O place of true rest — a place I consider not unworthy of the name of an inner chamber, where God is seen not as if disturbed with anger or distracted by care, but where his will is proved to be good, acceptable, and perfect. That vision does not terrify but soothes; it does not stir up restless curiosity but calms it; it does not weary but quiets the senses. Here is true rest. A God at rest brings everything to rest, and to see him at rest is to be at rest."

Isa 25:1
5¶

First, if we seek the fatherly mercy and favor of God, we must turn our eyes to Christ, in whom alone the Father is well pleased (Matthew 3:17). When we seek salvation, life, and blessed immortality, we must take ourselves to him as well, since he alone is the fountain of life, the anchor of salvation, and the heir of the kingdom of heaven. After all, what is the goal of election but that, being adopted as sons by the heavenly Father, we may by his favor obtain salvation and immortality? However much you speculate and debate, you will see that in its ultimate aim it goes no further than this. So those whom God has adopted as sons are said to have been chosen, not in themselves, but in Christ Jesus (Ephesians 1:4) — because he could love them only in him, and could honor them with the inheritance of his kingdom only as those first made partakers with him. But if we are elect in him, we cannot find the certainty of our election in ourselves — not even in God the Father if we look at him apart from the Son. Christ, then, is the mirror in which we ought to, and in which, without being deceived, we may contemplate our election. For since it is into his body that the Father has decreed to graft those whom from eternity he willed to be his own — so that he regards as sons all whom he acknowledges as his members — we have proof clear and strong enough that we are written in the Book of Life if we are in fellowship with Christ. Moreover, he admitted us to sure fellowship with himself when, by the preaching of the gospel, he declared that the Father gave him to us, to be ours with all his blessings (Romans 8:32). We are said to be clothed with him, to be one with him, so that we may live because he himself lives. The teaching is often repeated: "God so loved the world that he gave his only begotten Son, so that whoever believes in him should not perish but have everlasting life" (John 3:16). The one who believes in him is said to have passed from death to life (John 5:24). In this sense he calls himself the bread of life, of which if anyone eats, he shall never die (John 6:35). He, I say, was our witness that all who receive him in faith will be regarded by our heavenly Father as sons. If we long for anything more than to be regarded as sons of God and heirs, we would have to ascend above Christ. But if this is our final goal, how foolish it is to seek outside of him what we have already obtained in him and can find only in him. Besides, since he is the Eternal Wisdom, the Unchangeable Truth, and the Settled Counsel of the Father, there is no reason to fear that anything he tells us could differ in the slightest from that will of the Father into which we inquire. On the contrary, he faithfully discloses it to us as it was from the beginning and always will be. The practical force of this teaching should also show itself in our prayers. For though belief in our election rouses us to call upon God, yet when we frame our prayers it would be backwards to thrust this upon God or to bargain in this way: "O Lord, if I am elect, hear me." He would have us rest content with his promises and not inquire elsewhere whether or not he is willing to hear us. We will thus be freed from many snares if we know how to make right use of what is rightly written. But let us not carelessly twist it to purposes other than the one to which it ought to be confined.

Scripture cited
Mt 3:17 · Eph 1:4 · Rom 8:32 · John 3:16 · John 5:24 · John 6:35
6¶

Another confirmation that strengthens our confidence is this: our election is bound up with our calling. For those whom Christ enlightens with the knowledge of his name, and admits into the bosom of his Church, he is said to take under his guardianship and protection. All whom he thus receives are said to be committed and entrusted to him by the Father, so that they may be kept for life eternal. What more could we want? Christ proclaims aloud that all whom the Father is pleased to save he has placed in his keeping (John 6:37-39; 17:6, 12). Therefore, if we want to know whether God cares for our salvation, let us ask whether he has committed us to Christ, whom he has appointed as the only Savior of all his people. Then, if we doubt whether we have been received into Christ's protection, he removes the doubt when he freely offers himself as our Shepherd and declares that we are among his sheep if we hear his voice (John 10:3, 16). Let us, then, embrace Christ, who is kindly offered to us and comes forward to meet us. He will count us among his flock and keep us within his fold. But anxiety arises about our future state. Someone will say that we must feel concerned about what may happen to us, and that when we think of the time to come, our weakness warns us to be anxious.

John 6:37-39 · John 10:3 · John 10:16
7¶

But it happens daily that those who seemed to belong to Christ turn away from him and fall away. Indeed, in the very passage where he declares that none of those the Father has given him have perished, he makes an exception of the son of perdition. This is certainly true; but it is equally true that such people never clung to Christ with that heartfelt confidence by which, as I say, the certainty of our election is established. "They went out from us," John says, "but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would no doubt have continued with us" (1 John 2:19). I do not deny that they have signs of calling similar to those given to the elect. But I in no way admit that they have that sure confirmation of election which I want believers to seek from the word of the gospel. Therefore, let cases like these not move us away from calm confidence in the Lord's promise, when he declares that all who receive him in true faith have been given to him by the Father, and that none of them will perish while he is their Guardian and Shepherd (John 3:16; 6:39). We will speak of Judas shortly (in section 9). Paul does not warn Christians against security as such, but against careless, fleshly security — the kind accompanied by pride, arrogance, and contempt of others, which extinguishes humility and reverence for God and produces forgetfulness of the grace received (Romans 11:20). For he is addressing the Gentiles, showing them that they ought not to exult proudly and cruelly over the Jews, in whose place they had been substituted after the Jews' rejection. He also commands fear — not a fear under which they should waver in alarm, but a fear that, teaching us to receive the grace of God in humility, does not weaken our confidence in it, as has been said elsewhere. We may add that he is not speaking to individuals but to groups in general (see 1 Corinthians 10:12). Since the Church had been divided into two parties, and rivalry was producing dissension, Paul reminds the Gentiles that their having been put in the place of a peculiar and holy people was a reason for modesty and fear. For there were many vainglorious people among them whose empty boasting it was fitting to restrain. But we have seen elsewhere that our hope reaches into the future, even beyond death, and that nothing is more contrary to its nature than to be in doubt about our future destiny.

Scripture cited
1 John 2:19 · John 3:16 · Rom 11:20 · 1 Cor 10:12
8¶

Our Savior's words, "Many are called, but few are chosen" (Matthew 22:14), are also very wrongly interpreted (see Book 3, chapter 2, sections 11-12). There will be no ambiguity in them if we attend to what our earlier remarks should have made clear — namely, that there are two kinds of calling. There is a universal call, by which God, through the external preaching of the Word, invites all people alike, even those for whom he intends the call to be a fragrance of death and the ground of a harsher condemnation. Besides this, there is a special call which, for the most part, God bestows on believers only, when by the inward illumination of the Spirit he causes the preached Word to take deep root in their hearts. Sometimes, however, he grants this also to those whom he enlightens only for a time, and whom afterward, in just punishment for their ingratitude, he abandons and strikes with greater blindness. Now our Lord, seeing that the gospel was published far and wide, was despised by many, and rightly valued by few, portrays God in the character of a King who, preparing a great feast, sends his servants all around to invite a great crowd, but can secure the presence of only very few, because almost all offer excuses. At length, because they refuse, he is compelled to send his servants out into the highways to invite everyone they meet. It is perfectly clear that up to this point the parable is to be understood of external calling. He then adds that God plays the part of a gracious host who goes around his table and graciously receives his guests. But still, if he finds anyone not dressed in a wedding garment, he will by no means allow him to insult the feast by his shabby clothing. I grant that this part of the parable is to be understood of those who, by a profession of faith, enter the Church but are not at all clothed with the sanctification of Christ. Such disgraces to his Church, such cankers, God will not tolerate forever, but will cast them out as their foulness deserves. Few, then, out of the great number of the called are chosen — the calling, however, not being of the kind that enables believers to judge of their election. The former call is common to the wicked; the latter brings with it the spirit of regeneration, which is the down payment and seal of the inheritance to come, by which our hearts are sealed for the day of the Lord (Ephesians 1:13-14). In a word, while hypocrites pretend to godliness, as though they were true worshipers of God, Christ declares that they will in the end be thrown out of the place they wrongly occupy, as the psalm says: "Lord, who shall dwell in your tabernacle? Who shall live on your holy hill? He who walks uprightly, and does what is right, and speaks the truth in his heart" (Psalm 15:1-2). And again in another place: "This is the generation of those who seek him, who seek your face, O Jacob" (Psalm 24:6). And so the Spirit urges believers to patience, not to grumble because Ishmaelites are mixed in with them in the Church, since the mask will at last be torn off and they will be cast out in disgrace.

Mt 22:14 · Eph 1:13, 14 · Psalm 15:1, 2 · Psalm 24:6
9¶

The same explanation applies to the passage just quoted, in which Christ says that none is lost but the son of perdition (John 17:12). The expression is not strictly exact, but it is by no means obscure. For Judas was counted among the sheep of Christ not because he truly was one, but because he held a place among them. Then, in another passage where the Lord says that he was chosen along with the apostles, the reference is only to the office: "Have I not chosen you twelve," he says, "and one of you is a devil?" (John 6:70). That is, he had chosen him to the office of apostle. But when he speaks of election to salvation, he altogether excludes him from the number of the elect: "I do not speak of you all; I know whom I have chosen" (John 13:18). If anyone confuses the word "election" in these two passages, he will hopelessly entangle himself; but if he distinguishes between them, nothing could be plainer. Gregory, therefore, is most seriously and harmfully in error when he says that we are conscious only of our calling but uncertain of our election, and on this basis exhorts everyone to fear and trembling, giving as his reason that although we know what we are today, we do not know what we will be (Gregory, Homily 38). In that passage he plainly shows how he stumbled over that stone. By hanging election on the merit of works, he had good enough reason to dishearten the minds of his readers — while, at the same time, since he did not lead them away from themselves to confidence in the divine goodness, he was unable to reassure them. From this believers may to some degree perceive the truth of what we said at the start: that predestination, rightly considered, does not shake faith but rather offers it the best confirmation. I do not deny, however, that the Spirit sometimes adapts his language to our feeble capacity, as when he says, "They shall not be in the assembly of my people, nor shall they be written in the writing of the house of Israel" (Ezekiel 13:9). As if God were just beginning to write the names of those he counts among his people in the Book of Life — whereas we know, even on the testimony of Christ, that the names of the children of God were written in the Book of Life from the beginning (Luke 10:20). The words simply indicate the abandoning of those who seemed to hold a chief place among the elect, as the psalm says: "Let them be blotted out of the Book of the Living, and not be written with the righteous" (Psalm 69:28).

Scripture cited
John 17:12 · John 6:70 · John 13:18 · Ezek 13:9 · Luke 10:20 · Psalm 69:28
10¶

For the elect are brought by calling into the fold of Christ — not from the very womb, nor all at the same time, but as God sees fit to dispense his grace. Before they are gathered to the supreme Shepherd, they wander scattered in a common wilderness, no different from others, except that by the special mercy of God they are kept from rushing to final destruction. Therefore, if you look at them in themselves, you will see the offspring of Adam giving every sign of the corruption common to the whole mass. That they do not go on to extreme and desperate ungodliness is owing to no inborn goodness in them, but because the eye of God watches over their safety and his hand is stretched out over them. Those who dream of some seed of election planted in their hearts from birth, by whose working they are always inclined toward godliness and the fear of God, are not supported by the authority of Scripture but refuted by experience. They do, indeed, produce a few examples to prove that the elect, before they were enlightened, were not strangers to religion — for instance, that Paul led a blameless life during his time as a Pharisee, that Cornelius was accepted for his prayers and acts of charity, and so on (Philippians 3:5; Acts 10:2). We grant the case of Paul, but we hold that they are mistaken about Cornelius, for it appears that he was already enlightened and regenerate, so that all he lacked was a clear revelation of the gospel. But what can they draw from these few examples? That all the elect were always endowed with the spirit of godliness? One might just as well, after pointing to the integrity of Aristides, Socrates, Xenocrates, Scipio, Curius, Camillus, and others (see Book 2, chapter 4, section 4), conclude that all who are left in the blindness of idolatry are devoted to virtue and holiness. On the contrary, Scripture plainly opposes them in more passages than one. The description Paul gives of the state of the Ephesians before their regeneration shows not one grain of this seed. His words are: "And you he made alive, who were dead in trespasses and sins, in which you once walked according to the course of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, the spirit who now works in the children of disobedience — among whom we all also once conducted ourselves in the lusts of our flesh, fulfilling the desires of the flesh and of the mind, and were by nature children of wrath, just like the others" (Ephesians 2:1-3). And again: "At that time you were without Christ, having no hope and without God in the world" (Ephesians 2:12). Again: "You were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord; walk as children of light" (Ephesians 5:8). But perhaps they will insist that in this last passage the reference is to that ignorance of the true God in which they do not deny the elect lived before they were called. Yet this is utterly inconsistent with the apostle's conclusion that they were no longer to lie or steal (Ephesians 4:28). What answer will they give to other passages, such as the one where, after telling the Corinthians that "neither fornicators, nor idolaters, nor adulterers, nor the effeminate, nor abusers of themselves with mankind, nor thieves, nor the covetous, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor extortioners shall inherit the kingdom of God," he immediately adds, "And such were some of you; but you were washed, but you were sanctified, but you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus and by the Spirit of our God" (1 Corinthians 6:9-11)? Again he says to the Romans: "As you presented your members as slaves to uncleanness and to lawlessness leading to more lawlessness, so now present your members as slaves to righteousness leading to holiness. For when you were slaves of sin, you were free from righteousness. What fruit did you have then in the things of which you are now ashamed?" (Romans 6:19-21).

Phil 3:5 · Acts 10:2 · Eph 2:1-3 · Eph 2:12 · Eph 5:8 · Eph 4:28 · 1 Cor 6:9-11 · Rom 6:19-21
11¶

Tell me, then, what seed of election sprouted in those who, defiled in various ways throughout their whole lives, gave themselves over with desperate wickedness to every kind of abomination? Had Paul meant to express that view, he ought to have shown how much they owed at that time to the kindness of God, by which they had been kept from falling into such pollution. So too Peter ought to have urged his countrymen to be grateful for a perpetual seed of election. On the contrary, his admonition is this: "For the time we have already spent in our past life is enough to have done the will of the Gentiles" (1 Peter 4:3). And what if we turn to examples? Was there any germ of righteousness in Rahab the harlot before she believed (Joshua 2:4)? In Manasseh, when Jerusalem was stained and almost flooded with the blood of the prophets (2 Kings 23:16)? In the thief who only with his dying breath thought of repentance (Luke 23:42)? Have done, then, with those arguments that curious men rashly devise on their own, with no warrant from Scripture. But let us hold fast to what Scripture states — namely, that "all we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned, every one, to his own way" (Isaiah 53:6); that is, to destruction. In this gulf of destruction God leaves those whom he has determined one day to deliver, until his own appointed time arrives; he only keeps them from plunging into irremediable blasphemy.

Scripture cited
1 Pet 4:3 · Josh 2:4 · 2 Kings 23:16 · Luke 23:42 · Isa 53:6
12¶

Just as the Lord, by the power of his calling, accomplishes toward his elect the salvation to which he had destined them by his eternal counsel, so he has judgments against the reprobate, by which he carries out his counsel concerning them. Therefore, those whom he has created for dishonor in life and destruction in death, so that they may be vessels of wrath and examples of his severity, he brings to their doom — at one time depriving them of any means of hearing his Word, at another, through the preaching of it, blinding and stupefying them all the more. Examples of the first case are countless, but let us pick out one of the most remarkable of all. Before the coming of Christ, about four thousand years passed during which he hid the light of saving doctrine from all nations. If anyone answers that he did not put them in possession of this great blessing because he judged them unworthy, then their descendants would be no more worthy. Of this, besides experience, Malachi is a sufficient witness; for while charging them with mixed unbelief and blasphemy, he yet declares that the Redeemer will come. Why, then, is the Redeemer given to the later people rather than the earlier? They will torment themselves in vain seeking a deeper cause than the secret and unsearchable counsel of God. And there is no reason to fear that some disciple of Porphyry might freely arraign the justice of God while we say nothing in its defense. For while we maintain that no one perishes without deserving it, and that it is owing to the free goodness of God that some are delivered, enough has been said to display his glory; there is not the slightest occasion for quibbling. The supreme Disposer, then, makes way for his own predestination when, depriving those he has reprobated of the sharing of his light, he leaves them in blindness. Every day supplies instances of the second case, and many of them are set before us in Scripture. Out of a hundred to whom the same sermon is delivered, perhaps twenty receive it with the ready obedience of faith; the rest set no value on it, or mock it, or scorn it, or detest it. If it is said that this difference is owing to the malice and perversity of the latter group, the answer is not satisfactory — for the same wickedness would grip the minds of the former group as well, if God in his goodness did not correct it. And so we will always be entangled until we call in the help of Paul's question, "Who makes you to differ?" (1 Corinthians 4:7), implying that some excel others not by their own virtue, but by the mere favor of God.

Scripture cited
1 Cor 4:7
13¶

Why, then, while bestowing grace on the one, does he pass by the other? Regarding the former, Luke gives the reason: because they "were appointed to eternal life" (Acts 13:48). What, then, shall we think of the latter, except that they are vessels of wrath fitted for dishonor? Therefore, let us not shrink from saying with Augustine: "God could change the will of the wicked into good, for he is almighty. Clearly he could. Why, then, does he not do it? Because he is unwilling. Why he is unwilling remains with himself." We should not try to be wise beyond what is fitting, and it is far better to take Augustine's explanation than to quibble with Chrysostom, who says "that God draws the one who is willing and stretches out his hand," lest the difference should seem to lie in the judgment of God rather than in the mere will of man. So far is it, in fact, from being placed in the mere will of man, that we may add this: even the godly, and those who fear God, need this special inspiration of the Spirit. Lydia, a seller of purple, feared God, and yet it was necessary that her heart be opened so that she might attend to the teaching of Paul and profit from it (Acts 16:14). This was said not of one woman only, but to teach us that all progress in godliness is the secret work of the Spirit. Nor can it be questioned that God sends his Word to many whose blindness he is pleased to deepen. For why does he order so many messages to be carried to Pharaoh? Was it because he hoped Pharaoh might be softened by the repetition? On the contrary, before he began, God both knew and had foretold the outcome: "The Lord said to Moses, When you go back to Egypt, see that you do all those wonders before Pharaoh which I have put in your hand; but I will harden his heart, so that he will not let the people go" (Exodus 4:21). So too, when he raises up Ezekiel, he warns him in advance: "I send you to the children of Israel, to a rebellious nation that has rebelled against me." "Do not be afraid of their words." "You dwell in the midst of a rebellious house, which has eyes to see and does not see; they have ears to hear and do not hear" (Ezekiel 2:3, 6; 12:2). So he foretells to Jeremiah that the effect of his teaching would be "to root out, and pull down, and to destroy" (Jeremiah 1:10). But the prophecy of Isaiah presses still closer, for he is commissioned by the Lord in this way: "Go and tell this people: Keep on hearing, but do not understand; keep on seeing, but do not perceive. Make the heart of this people fat, and make their ears heavy, and shut their eyes, lest they see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and understand with their heart, and turn and be healed" (Isaiah 6:9-10). Here he directs his voice to them, but so that they may turn an even deafer ear; he kindles a light, but so that they may become more blind; he sets forth a teaching, but so that they may grow more dull; he applies a remedy, but so that they may not be cured. And John, referring to this prophecy, declares that the Jews could not believe the teaching of Christ because this curse from God lay upon them. It is also beyond dispute that to those whom God is not pleased to enlighten, he delivers his teaching wrapped in riddles, so that they may not profit by it but be given over to greater blindness. This is why our Savior declares that the parables in which he had spoken to the crowd he explained to the apostles alone, "because it is given to you to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it is not given" (Matthew 13:11). What, you will ask, does our Lord mean by teaching those whom he takes care not to have understand him? Consider where the fault lies, and then stop asking. However obscure the Word may be, there is always enough light in it to convict the consciences of the ungodly.

Acts 13:48 · Acts 16:14 · Exod 4:21 · Ezek 2:3 · Ezek 2:6 · Jer 1:10 · Isa 6:9, 10 · Mt 13:11
14¶

It now remains to see why the Lord acts in the way that he plainly does. If the answer is given that it is because people deserve this by their ungodliness, wickedness, and ingratitude, that is indeed well and truly said. But still, since it does not yet appear what the cause of the difference is — why some are turned to obedience while others remain hardened — we must, in discussing it, pass to the passage from Moses on which Paul has commented: "For this very purpose I have raised you up, that I might show my power in you, and that my name might be declared throughout all the earth" (Romans 9:17). The reprobate's refusal to obey the Word of God when it is made known to them will rightly be ascribed to the malice and depravity of their hearts — provided it is at the same time added that they were handed over to this depravity because, by the just but unsearchable judgment of God, they were raised up to display his glory by their condemnation. In the same way, when it is said of the sons of Eli that they would not listen to wholesome warnings "because the Lord desired to kill them" (1 Samuel 2:25), it is not denied that their stubbornness was the result of their own wickedness. But at the same time the reason is given why they were left to their stubbornness when the Lord could have softened their hearts — namely, because his unchangeable decree had once for all doomed them to destruction. Hence John's words: "Although he had done so many miracles before them, yet they did not believe in him, that the saying of Isaiah the prophet might be fulfilled which he spoke: Lord, who has believed our report?" (John 12:37-38). For though he does not excuse their perversity, he is content with the reason that the grace of God has no flavor to people until the Holy Spirit gives it its savor. And Christ, in quoting Isaiah's prophecy, "They shall all be taught by God" (John 6:45), means only to show that the Jews were reprobate and strangers to the Church because they would not be taught, and he gives no other reason than that the promise of God does not belong to them. Confirming this are Paul's words: "Christ crucified" was "to the Jews a stumbling block and to the Greeks foolishness, but to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:23). For after mentioning the usual result wherever the gospel is preached — that it provokes some and is despised by others — he says that it is precious only to those who are called. A little before, he had given them the name of believers, but he was unwilling to deny the proper rank to divine grace, which comes before faith; or rather, he added the second term by way of correction, so that those who had embraced the gospel might ascribe the credit for their faith to the calling of God. So, too, he shows shortly afterward that they were elected by God. When the wicked hear these things, they complain that God abuses his unlimited power, making cruel sport of the miseries of his creatures. But let us, who know that all people are liable on so many grounds to the judgment of God that they cannot answer for even one of a thousand of their transgressions (Job 9:3), confess that the reprobate suffer nothing that is not in keeping with the most perfect justice. When we cannot clearly make out the reason, let us not refuse to remain to some extent in ignorance regarding the depths of the divine wisdom.

Scripture cited
Rom 9:17 · 1 Sam 2:25 · John 12:37, 38 · John 6:45 · 1 Cor 1:23 · Job 9:3
15¶

But since an objection is often built on a few passages of Scripture in which God seems to deny that the wicked perish by his ordination, except insofar as they freely bring death upon themselves against his warning, let us briefly explain these passages and show that they are not contrary to the view set out above. One of the passages put forward is this: "Do I have any pleasure at all that the wicked should die? says the Lord God; and not that he should turn from his ways and live?" (Ezekiel 18:23). If we are to extend this to the whole human race, why are not the very many whose minds might be more easily bent to obey urged to repentance, rather than those who by his invitations grow daily more and more hardened? Our Lord declares that the preaching of the gospel and his miracles would have borne more fruit among the people of Nineveh and Sodom than in Judea (Matthew 13:23). How is it, then, that if God wills all to be saved, he does not open a door of repentance for the wretched, who would have more readily received grace? From this we may see that the passage is violently twisted if the will of God which the prophet mentions is set against his eternal counsel, by which he separated the elect from the reprobate. Bernard, in his Sermon on the Nativity, on 2 Corinthians 1:3, quoting the two passages Romans 9:18 and Ezekiel 18:32, admirably reconciles them.

Scripture cited
Ezek 18:23 · Mt 13:23 · 2 Cor 1:3 · Rom 9:18 · Ezek 18:32
16¶

The second passage put forward is the one in which Paul says that "God will have all men to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4). Though the reasoning here differs from the former, the two have something in common. I answer, first, that the way in which God wills this is plain from the context, for Paul joins two things together: a will to save, and a will to bring people to the knowledge of the truth. If from this they would have it fixed by the eternal counsel of God that all are to receive the teaching of salvation, what then is meant by Moses' words, "What nation is there so great, who has God so near to them?" (Deuteronomy 4:7)? How is it that many nations are deprived of the light of the gospel that others enjoy? How is it that the pure knowledge of godly doctrine has never reached some, while others have scarcely tasted some obscure rudiments of it? It will now be easy to draw out the meaning of Paul's statement. He had commanded Timothy that prayers should be regularly offered in the church for kings and princes; but since it seemed somewhat absurd that prayer should be offered for a class of men who were almost hopeless (all of them being not only strangers to the body of Christ but doing their utmost to overthrow his kingdom), he adds that it was acceptable to God, who will have all men to be saved. By this he surely means nothing more than that the way of salvation was not shut against any class of men — that, on the contrary, he had displayed his mercy in such a way that he would have none barred from it. Other passages do not declare what God has, in his secret judgment, determined regarding all, but declare that pardon is prepared for all sinners who only turn to seek after it. For if they persist in pressing the words, "God has shut up all in unbelief, that he might have mercy on all" (Romans 11:32), I will, on the contrary, press what is written elsewhere: "Our God is in the heavens; he has done whatever he pleased" (Psalm 115:3). We must therefore explain the passage so as to reconcile it with this other one: "I will be gracious to whom I will be gracious, and will show mercy on whom I will show mercy" (Exodus 33:19). The one who selects those he will visit in mercy does not extend it to all. But since it clearly appears that Paul is there speaking not of individuals but of classes of men, let us have done with further discussion. At the same time, we ought to observe that Paul does not assert what God does always, everywhere, and in all circumstances, but leaves it free to him to make kings and magistrates partakers of heavenly doctrine, even though in their blindness they rage against it. A stronger objection seems to be founded on the passage in Peter: the Lord is "not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance" (2 Peter 3:9). But the solution of the difficulty is found in the second part of the sentence, for his willing that they should come to repentance cannot be taken in any sense other than the one that is consistently used. Conversion is certainly in the hand of God; whether he intends to convert all can be learned from himself, when he promises that he will give some a heart of flesh and leave others a heart of stone (Ezekiel 36:26). It is true that if he were not willing to receive those who beg for his mercy, it could not have been said, "Turn to me, says the Lord of Hosts, and I will turn to you, says the Lord of Hosts" (Zechariah 1:3). But I hold that no one approaches God unless first moved from above. And if repentance were placed at man's will, Paul would not say, "if perhaps God will grant them repentance" (2 Timothy 2:25). Indeed, if God did not, at the very time he is outwardly exhorting all to repentance, move the elect by the secret working of his Spirit, Jeremiah would not say, "Turn me, and I shall be turned, for you are the Lord my God. Surely, after I was turned, I repented" (Jeremiah 31:18).

Scripture cited
1 Tim 2:4 · Deut 4:7 · Rom 11:32 · Ps 115:3 · Exod 33:19 · 2 Pet 3:9 · Ezek 36:26 · Zech 1:3 · 2 Tim 2:25 · Jer 31:18
17¶

But if this is so (you will say), little trust can be placed in the gospel promises, which, in testifying about the will of God, declare that he wills what is contrary to his inviolable decree. Not at all. For however universal the promises of salvation may be, there is no conflict between them and the predestination of the reprobate, provided we attend to their effect. We know that the promises are effective only when we receive them in faith; but on the contrary, when faith is made void, the promise has no effect. If this is the nature of the promises, let us now see whether there is any inconsistency between these two things: that God, by an eternal decree, fixed the number of those whom he is pleased to embrace in love and on whom he is pleased to display his wrath, and that he offers salvation to all without distinction. I hold that they are perfectly consistent. For all that is meant by the promise is simply that his mercy is offered to all who desire and beg for it — and none do this except those whom he has enlightened. Moreover, he enlightens those whom he has predestined to salvation. Thus the truth of the promises remains firm and unshaken, so that it cannot be said there is any disagreement between the eternal election of God and the testimony of his grace which he offers to believers. But why does he mention all men? So that the consciences of the righteous may rest the more secure when they understand that there is no difference between sinners, provided they have faith, and so that the ungodly may not be able to plead that they have no refuge to which they may flee from the bondage of sin, while they ungratefully reject the offer made to them. Therefore, since by the gospel the mercy of God is offered to both, it is faith — in other words, the illumination of God — which distinguishes between the righteous and the wicked: the former feeling the power of the gospel, the latter gaining no benefit from it. And illumination itself has eternal election for its rule.

Another passage quoted is the lament of our Savior: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, how often I would have gathered your children together, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, and you would not!" (Matthew 23:37). But it gives them no support. I admit that here Christ speaks not only in his character as man, but also reproaches them with having, in every age, rejected his grace. But this will of God of which we speak must be defined. For it is well known what efforts the Lord made to hold on to that people, and how perversely, from the highest to the lowest, they followed their own willful desires and refused to be gathered. But it does not follow that by the wickedness of men the counsel of God was thwarted. They object that nothing is less in keeping with the nature of God than that he should have a double will. This I grant, provided they are sound interpreters. But why do they not attend to the many passages in which God clothes himself with human feelings and stoops beneath his proper majesty (to accommodate himself to our weakness)?

These two statements — "to our perception the will of God is manifold," and "he mysteriously wills what now seems to be against his will" — uncover a difficulty with which Calvin wrestles: namely, the problem of whether God has a double will (or wills contrary things at the same time). Does God reveal one kind of will in the gospel, while willing something else in his secret purpose? Do the gospel promises, "in testifying concerning the will of God, declare that he wills what is contrary to his inviolable decree" (the first line of this section)? Calvin, although insisting that there is no discrepancy, no inconsistency, between the predestination of the reprobate and the indiscriminate offer of the gospel to all (and offering certain reasons for his conviction), nevertheless finds the ultimate solution to this problem in the incomprehensibility of God. God is so great, so far above us, and transcends our senses to such a degree, that we can never hope to comprehend his mystery or the depths of his infinite being. Yet he does not make the absolute distinction that some have made between God as he is in himself (about whom we can know nothing) and God as he appears to us (about whom we can know something), for he asserts, "yet he [God] does not in himself will opposites." Thus Calvin does say something about God as he is in himself — in fact, he asserts that God does not violate the law of contradiction. However, he leaves the final resolution of this apparent discrepancy to the future at the end of all things, when perhaps the mystery involved in this doctrine will be made known to our understanding. For the present, he urges us to "stand in awe with Paul before the great depth" of the wisdom and knowledge of God.

Mt 23:37

Calvin's pastoral aim crowns the doctrine: election, hidden in eternity, is made known to us not by climbing into the abyss of God's secret counsel but by his effectual calling. Christ is the mirror in which we behold our election without deception — for those grafted into his body and kept by his shepherding hand have proof enough that their names are written in the Book of Life. Do not fly above the clouds to learn your destiny; begin with the call of God, and end with it. This is medicine for the anxious soul in 2026, beset by Satan's favorite temptation — endless doubt over whether one is truly chosen. The answer is never found by introspection's bottomless descent, but by looking to Christ offered in the gospel and embraced by faith. Teach your family to test their election by their calling, to rest in the Shepherd who loses none the Father gave him, and to pray simply — never 'Lord, if I am elected,' but trusting the promise he has sworn to keep.
