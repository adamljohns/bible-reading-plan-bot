Book 2 · Chapter 10 · USMC modern English

The Resemblance Between the Old Testament and the New

This chapter consists of four parts. I. The sum, utility, and necessity of this discussion, sec. 1. II. A proof that, generally speaking, the old and new dispensations are in reality one, although differently administered. Three points in which the two dispensations entirely agree, sec. 2–4. III. The Old Testament, as well as the New, had regard to the hope of immortality and a future life, whence two other resemblances or points of agreement follow—viz. that both were established by the free mercy of God, and confirmed by the intercession of Christ. This proved by many arguments, passages of Scripture, and examples, see. 5–23. IV. Conclusion of the whole chapter, where, for fuller confirmation, certain passages of Scripture are produced. Refutation of the cavils of the Sadducees and other Jews.

Sections in this chapter

  2. 2. This similarity in general. Both covenants truly one, though differently administered. Three things in which they entirely agree.
  3. 3. First general similarity, or agreement—viz. that the Old Testament, equally with the New, extended its promises beyond the present life, and held out a sure hope of immortality. Reason for this resemblance. Objection answered.
  4. 4. The other two points of resemblance—viz. that both covenants were established in the mercy of God, and confirmed by the mediation of Christ.
  5. 5. The first of these points of resemblance being the foundation of the other two, a lengthened proof is given of it. The first argument taken from a passage, in which Paul, showing that the sacraments of both dispensations had the same meaning, proves that the condition of the ancient church was similar to ours.
  6. 6. An objection from John 6:49 —viz. that the Israelites ate manna in the wilderness, and are dead, whereas Christians eat the flesh of Christ, and die not. Answer reconciling this passage of the Evangelist with that of the Apostle.
  7. 7. Another proof from the Law and the Prophets—viz. the power of the divine word in quickening souls before Christ was manifested. Hence the believing Jews were raised to the hope of eternal life.
  8. 8. Third proof from the form of the covenant, which shows that it was in reality one both before and after the manifestation of Christ in the flesh.
  9. 9. Confirmation of the former proof from the clear terms in which the form is expressed. Another confirmation derived from the former and from the nature of God.
  10. 10. Fourth proof from examples. Adam, Abel, and Noah, when tried with various temptations, neglecting the present, aspired with living faith and invincible hope to a better life. They, therefore, had the same aim as believers under the Gospel.
  11. 11. Continuation of the fourth proof from the example of Abraham, whose call and whole course of life shows that he ardently aspired to eternal felicity. Objection disposed of.
  12. 12. Continuation of the fourth proof from the examples of Isaac and Jacob.
  13. 13. Conclusion of the fourth proof. Adam, Abel, Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and others under the Law, looked for the fulfilment of the divine promises not on the earth, but in heaven. Hence they termed this life an earthly pilgrimage, and desired to be buried in the land of Canaan, which was a figure of eternal happiness.
  14. 14. A fifth proof from Jacob’s earnestness to obtain the birth-right. This shows a prevailing desire of future life. This perceived in some degree by Balaam.
  15. 15. A sixth proof from David, who expects such great things from the Lord, and yet declares the present life to be mere vanity.
  16. 16. A seventh proof also from David. His descriptions of the happiness of believers could only be realised in a future state.
  17. 17. An eighth proof from the common feeling and confession of all the pious who sought by faith and hope to obtain in heaven what they did not see in the present shadowy life.
  18. 18. A continuation and confirmation of the former proof from the exultation of the righteous, even amid the destruction of the world.
  19. 19. A ninth proof from Job, who spoke most distinctly of this hope. Two objections disposed of.
  20. 20. A tenth proof from the later Prophets, who taught that the happiness of the righteous was placed beyond the limits of the present life.
  21. 21. This clearly established by Ezekiel’s vision of the dry bones, and a passage in Isaiah.
  22. 22. Last proof from certain passages in the Prophets, which clearly show the future immortality of the righteous in the kingdom of heaven.

1¶

From what has been said above, it must now be clear that all whom God, from the beginning of the world, adopted as his own peculiar people were taken into covenant with him on the same conditions, and under the same bond of doctrine, as ourselves. But since it is of no small importance to establish this point, I will add it here by way of appendix. I will show, since the fathers were partakers with us in the same inheritance and hoped for a common salvation through the grace of the same Mediator, how far their condition in this respect differed from our own. For although the passages we have collected from the Law and the Prophets for the purpose of proof make it plain that there never was any other rule of godliness and religion among the people of God, yet because many things are written on the difference between the Old and New Testaments in a way that may perplex ordinary readers, it will be proper to devote a special place here to a better and more exact discussion of this subject. This discussion, which would have been most useful in any case, has been made necessary by that monstrous miscreant Servetus and by some madmen of the sect of the Anabaptists, who think of the people of Israel just as they would of some herd of swine, absurdly imagining that the Lord gorged them with temporal blessings here and gave them no hope of a blessed immortality. The French is, "Veu qu'ils pensent qu notre Seigneur l'ait voulu seulement engraisser enterre comme en une auge, sans seperance aucune de l'immortalité celeste"; that is, seeing they think that our Lord only wished to fatten them on the earth as in a sty, without any hope of heavenly immortality.

2¶

It is possible, indeed, to explain both in one word. The covenant made with all the fathers is so far from differing from ours in reality and substance that it is altogether one and the same; still, the administration differs. But because this brief summary is not enough to give anyone a full understanding of the subject, our explanation, if it is to be useful, must run to greater length. In showing the similarity, or rather the identity, of the two dispensations, however, it would be superfluous to treat again the particulars already discussed, just as it would be out of season to bring in those which are still to be considered elsewhere. What we propose to insist on here may be reduced to three heads. First, that temporal wealth and happiness was not the goal to which the Jews were invited to aspire, but that they were admitted to the hope of immortality, and that assurance of this adoption was given by direct communications, by the Law, and by the Prophets. Second, that the covenant by which they were reconciled to the Lord was founded on no merits of their own, but solely on the mercy of God who called them. And third, that they both had and knew Christ the Mediator, by whom they were united to God and made capable of receiving his promises. The second of these, since it is perhaps not yet sufficiently understood, will be fully considered in its own place (Book 3, chapters 15-18). For we will prove by many clear passages in the Prophets that all which the Lord has ever given or promised to his people is of mere goodness and kindness. The third also has been demonstrated, not obscurely, in various places. Even the first has not been left unnoticed.

3¶

Since the first is most relevant to the present subject and is most disputed, we shall consider it more fully, taking care at the same time, where any of the others requires explanation, to supply it in passing, or to add it afterward in its proper place. The apostle, indeed, removes all doubt when he says that the Gospel which God gave concerning his Son, Jesus Christ, "he had promised aforetime by his prophets in the holy Scriptures" (Romans 1:2). And again, that "the righteousness of God without the law is manifested, being witnessed by the law and the prophets" (Romans 3:21). For the Gospel does not confine the hearts of men to the enjoyment of the present life, but raises them to the hope of immortality. It does not fix them to earthly delights, but, announcing that a treasure is laid up in heaven, carries the heart there as well. For in another place he explains it this way: "After that ye believed [the Gospel,] ye were sealed with that Holy Spirit of promise, which is the earnest of our inheritance unto the redemption of the purchased possession" (Ephesians 1:13, 14). Again, "Since we heard of your faith in Christ Jesus, and of the love which ye have to all the saints, for the hope which is laid up for you in heaven, whereof ye heard before in the word of the truth of the Gospel" (Colossians 1:4). Again, "Whereunto he called you by our Gospel to the obtaining of the glory of our Lord Jesus Christ" (2 Thessalonians 2:14). Hence also it is called the word of salvation, and the power of God unto salvation to everyone who believes, and the kingdom of heaven. Acts 13:26; Romans 1:16; 1 Corinthians 1:18; Matthew 3:2, 4, 17, etc., especially 13.

"Novo populo." French, "au peuple du Nouveau Testament"; that is, the people of the New Dispensation.

4¶

In the same way we infer that the Old Testament was both established by the free mercy of God and confirmed by the intercession of Christ. For the preaching of the Gospel declares nothing more than that sinners, without any merit of their own, are justified by the fatherly kindness of God. It is wholly summed up in Christ. Who, then, will presume to represent the Jews as destitute of Christ, when we know that they were parties to the Gospel covenant, which has its only foundation in Christ? Who will presume to make them strangers to the benefit of free salvation, when we know that they were instructed in the doctrine of justification by faith? And not to dwell on a point that is clear, we have the remarkable saying of our Lord, "Your father Abraham rejoiced to see my day, and he saw it and was glad" (John 8:56). What Christ here declares of Abraham, an apostle shows to apply to all believers when he says that Jesus Christ is the "same yesterday, to-day, and for ever" (Hebrews 13:8). For he is not speaking there merely of the eternal divinity of Christ, but of his power, of which believers always had full proof. Hence both the blessed Virgin "Beata Virgo." French, "la Vierge Marie"; that is, the Virgin Mary.

"Ejus finis." French, "la fin du Vieil Testament"; that is, the end of the Old Testament.

5¶

Indeed, the apostle makes the Israelites our equals not only in the grace of the covenant but also in the meaning of the sacraments. For, using the example of the punishments which Scripture says were inflicted on the Jews of old, in order to deter the Corinthians from falling into similar wickedness, he begins by stating that they have no ground to claim for themselves any privilege that could exempt them from the divine vengeance which overtook the Jews. For the Lord not only visited the Jews with the same mercies, but also marked his grace among them by the same symbols. It is as if he had said: If you think you are out of danger because the Baptism you received and the Supper of which you daily partake have excellent promises, and if in the meantime, despising the goodness of God, you indulge in licentiousness, know that the Jews, on whom the Lord inflicted his severest judgments, possessed similar symbols. They were baptized in passing through the sea, and in the cloud which protected them from the burning heat of the sun. It is said that this passage was a carnal baptism, corresponding in some degree to our spiritual baptism. But if that were so, the apostle's argument would lack force, since his object is to keep Christians from imagining that they excelled the Jews in the matter of baptism. Besides, the cavil cannot apply to what immediately follows, namely, that they did "all eat the same spiritual meat; and did all drink the same spiritual drink: for they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them: and that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:3, 4).

6¶

To take away the force of this passage of Paul, an objection is founded on the words of our Savior, "Your fathers did eat manna in the wilderness, and are dead." "If any man eat of this bread, he shall live for ever" (John 6:49, 51). There is no difficulty in reconciling the two passages. The Lord was addressing hearers who desired only to be filled with earthly food and cared nothing for the true food of the soul. He therefore adapts his speech in some degree to their capacity and, in particular, to meet their carnal view, draws a comparison between the manna and his own body. They called on him to prove his authority by performing some miracle, such as Moses performed in the wilderness when he obtained manna from heaven. In this manna they saw nothing but relief from the bodily hunger from which the people were then suffering; they did not penetrate to the loftier mystery to which Paul refers. Christ, therefore, to show that the blessing they ought to expect from him was more excellent than the celebrated one Moses had bestowed on their fathers, draws this comparison: If, in your opinion, it was a great and memorable miracle when the Lord, through Moses, supplied his people with heavenly food so that they might be sustained for a season and not perish from famine in the wilderness, infer from this how much more excellent is the food which bestows immortality. We see why our Lord left out what was the chief virtue of the manna and mentioned only its lowest use. Since the Jews had, as it were by way of reproach, thrown Moses up to him as one who had relieved the people's need by means of manna, he answers that he was the minister of a far greater grace, compared with which the bodily nourishment of the people, on which they set so high a value, ought to be counted worthless. Paul, on the other hand, knowing that the Lord, when he rained manna from heaven, had not merely supplied their bodies with food, but had also dispensed it as containing a spiritual mystery, to foreshadow the spiritual quickening which is obtained in Christ, does not overlook the quality that most deserved consideration. It is therefore surely and clearly proved that the same promises of heavenly and eternal life which the Lord now gives to us were not only communicated to the Jews, but also sealed by truly spiritual sacraments. Augustine discusses this subject at length in his work against Faustus the Manichee.

7¶

But if my readers would rather have passages quoted from the Law and the Prophets, from which they may see, as we have already shown from Christ and the apostles, that the spiritual covenant was common to the fathers also, I will yield to the wish, and all the more willingly because opponents will thus be more surely convinced, so that from now on there will be no room for evasion. And I will begin with a proof which, though I know it will seem futile and almost ridiculous to supercilious Anabaptists, will carry very great weight with the teachable and sober-minded. I take it for granted that the word of God has such an inherent power that it quickens the souls of all whom he is pleased to favor with the communication of it. Peter's statement has always been true, that it is an incorruptible seed, "which liveth and abideth for ever" (1 Peter 1:23), as he infers from the words of Isaiah (Isaiah 40:6). Now when God in ancient times bound the Jews to himself by this sacred bond, there cannot be any doubt that he set them apart to the hope of eternal life. When I say that they embraced the word which brought them nearer to God, I do not refer to that general mode of communication which is spread through heaven and earth and all the creatures of the world, and which, though it quickens all things, each according to its nature, rescues none from the bondage of corruption. I refer to that special mode of communication by which the minds of the godly are both enlightened in the knowledge of God and, in a manner, joined to him. Since Adam, Abel, Noah, Abraham, and the other patriarchs were united to God by this illumination of the word, I say there cannot be the least doubt that entrance was given them into the immortal kingdom of God. They had that solid participation in God which cannot exist without the blessing of everlasting life.

8¶

If the point still seems somewhat obscure, let us pass to the form of the covenant, which will not only satisfy calm thinkers but will sufficiently expose the ignorance of those who contradict. The covenant which God always made with his servants was this: "I will walk among you, and will be your God, and ye shall be my people" (Leviticus 26:12). These words, as the prophets are accustomed to expound them, include life and salvation and the whole sum of blessedness. For David repeatedly declares, and with good reason, "Happy is that people whose God is the Lord." "Blessed is the nation whose God is the Lord; and the people whom he has chosen for his own inheritance" (Psalm 144:15; 33:12). And this is not merely in respect of earthly happiness, but because he rescues from death, constantly preserves, and visits with eternal mercy those whom he has adopted as his people. As is said in other prophets, "Art not thou from everlasting, O Lord my God, mine Holy One? we shall not die." "The Lord is our judge, the Lord is our lawgiver, the Lord is our king; he will save us." "Happy art thou, O Israel: who is like unto thee, O people saved by the Lord?" (Habakkuk 1:12; Isaiah 33:22; Deuteronomy 33:29). But not to labor needlessly, the prophets constantly remind us that no good thing, and consequently no assurance of salvation, is lacking, provided the Lord is our God. And rightly so. For if his face, the moment it has shone on us, is a perfect pledge of salvation, how can he show himself to anyone as his God without opening to him the treasures of salvation? The terms on which God makes himself ours are that he will dwell in our midst, as he declared through Moses (Leviticus 26:11). But such presence cannot be enjoyed without life being possessed along with it at the same time. And even if nothing more had been expressed, they had a sufficiently clear promise of spiritual life in these words, "I am your God" (Exodus 6:7). For he declared that he would be a God not to their bodies only, but especially to their souls. Souls, however, if they are not united to God by righteousness, remain estranged from him in death. On the other hand, that union, wherever it exists, will bring everlasting salvation with it.

9¶

To this we may add that he not only declared that he was their God, but also promised that he would be. By this their hope was extended beyond present good and stretched forward into eternity. Moreover, that this regard to the future had its effect appears from the many passages in which the faithful console themselves not only in their present evils but also for the future, by calling to mind that God would never desert them. Further, as to the second part of the promise, namely, the blessing of God, its extending beyond the limits of the present life was still more clearly confirmed by the words, I will be the God of your seed after you (Genesis 17:7). If he was to show his favor to the dead by doing good to their posterity, much less would he deny his favor to the dead themselves. God is not like men, who transfer their love to the children of their friends because death has cut off the opportunity of showing kindness to the friends themselves as they wished. But God, whose kindness is not hindered by death, does not deprive the dead of the benefit of his mercy, which for their sake he continues to a thousand generations. God, therefore, was pleased to give a striking proof of the abundance and greatness of the goodness which they were to enjoy after death, when he described it as overflowing to all their posterity (Exodus 20:6). The truth of this promise was sealed, and in a manner fulfilled, when, long after the death of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, he called himself their God (Exodus 20:6). And why? Would the name not have been absurd if they had perished? It would have been just as if he had said, I am the God of men who do not exist. Accordingly, the Evangelists relate that by this very argument our Savior refuted the Sadducees (Matthew 22:23; Luke 20:32), who were therefore unable to deny that the resurrection of the dead was attested by Moses, since he had taught them that all the saints are in his hand (Deuteronomy 33:3). From this it is easy to infer that death is not the extinction of those who are taken under the care, guardianship, and protection of him who is the disposer of life and death.

10¶

Let us now see (and on this the controversy chiefly turns) whether or not believers themselves were so instructed by the Lord as to feel that they had a better life elsewhere, and to aspire to it while disregarding the present one. First, the manner of life which heaven had laid on them was a constant exercise by which they were reminded that if in this world only they had hope, they were of all men the most miserable. Adam, most unhappy even in the mere memory of his lost happiness, barely supplies his needs by anxious labor; and so that the divine curse might not be confined to bodily labor, his only remaining comfort becomes a source of the deepest grief. Of his two sons, one is torn from him by the murderous hand of his brother, while the other, who survives, causes loathing and horror by his very look. Abel, cruelly murdered in the very flower of his days, is an example of the calamity which had come upon man. While the whole world is living securely in luxury, Noah spends a great part of his life, with much toil, building an ark. He escapes death, but through greater troubles than a hundred deaths could have caused. Besides his ten months' stay in the ark, as in a kind of tomb, nothing could have been more unpleasant than to remain so long shut up among the filth of beasts. After escaping these hardships he falls into a new cause of sorrow. He sees himself mocked by his own son, and is forced with his own mouth to curse one whom, by the great kindness of God, he had received safe from the flood.

11¶

Abraham alone ought to be worth tens of thousands to us if we consider his faith, which is set before us as the best model of believing, and to whose race we also must be counted as belonging in order to be the children of God. Calvin, Commentary on Genesis, chapter 12:11-15.

12¶

Isaac is less afflicted, but he enjoys very few of the sweets of life. He too meets with those vexations which do not allow a man to be happy on earth. Famine drives him from the land of Canaan; his wife is torn from his arms; his neighbors are again and again annoying and harassing him in every kind of way, so that he is even obliged to fight for water. At home he suffers great annoyance from his daughters-in-law; he is stung by the strife between his sons, and has no other remedy for this great evil than to send the son whom he had blessed into exile (Genesis 26:27). Jacob, again, is nothing but a striking example of the greatest wretchedness. His boyhood is spent most uncomfortably at home amid the threats and alarms of his elder brother, to which he is at last forced to give way (Genesis 27:28). A fugitive from his parents and his native soil, besides the hardships of exile, he finds the treatment he receives from his uncle Laban in no respect milder or more humane (Genesis 29). As if it were a small thing to spend seven years of hard and rigorous servitude, he is cheated in the matter of a wife. For the sake of another wife he must undergo a new servitude, during which, as he himself complains, the heat of the sun scorches him by day, while in frost and cold he spends sleepless nights (Genesis 31:40, 41). For twenty years he lives this bitter life, and daily suffers new injuries from his father-in-law. Nor is he at peace at home, which he sees disturbed and almost broken up by the hatreds, quarrels, and jealousies of his wives. When he is ordered to return to his native land, he is obliged to depart in a manner resembling a disgraceful flight. Even then he cannot escape the injustice of his father-in-law, but in the middle of his journey is assailed by him with insult and reproach (Genesis 31:20). The French is, "Et encore ne peut il pas ainsi eviter l'iniquité de son beau père, qu'il ne soit de lui persecuté, et atteint au milieu du chemin; et pourceque Dieu ne permettoit point qu'il lui advint pis, il est vexé de beaucoup d'opprobres et contumelies, par eclui du quel il avoit bonne matiere de se plaindre." That is, even so he cannot escape the injustice of his father-in-law, but is persecuted by him and overtaken in the middle of his journey; and because God did not allow anything worse to happen to him, he is assailed with much insult and reproach by one of whom he had good cause to complain.

13¶

If these holy patriarchs expected a happy life from the hand of God (and it is beyond doubt that they did), they looked to and contemplated a happiness different from that of an earthly life. An apostle shows this admirably: "By faith he [Abraham] sojourned in the land of promise, as in a strange country, dwelling in tabernacles with Isaac and Jacob, the heirs with him of the same promise: for he looked for a city which has foundations, whose builder and maker is God." "These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them afar off, and were persuaded of them, and embraced them, and confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth. For they that say such things declare plainly that they seek a country. And truly, if they had been mindful of that country from whence they came out, they might have had opportunity to have returned. But now they desire a better country, that is, an heavenly: wherefore God is not ashamed to be called their God: for he has prepared for them a city" (Hebrews 11:9, 10, 13-16). They would have been duller than blocks to pursue so persistently promises of which no hope appeared on earth, if they had not expected their fulfillment elsewhere. What the apostle especially presses, and not without reason, is that they called this world a pilgrimage, as Moses also relates (Genesis 47:9). If they were pilgrims and strangers in the land of Canaan, where is the promise of the Lord which appointed them its heirs? It is clear, therefore, that the promise of possession they had received looked further. Hence they did not acquire a foot's breadth in the land of Canaan except for burial, thereby testifying that they did not hope to receive the benefit of the promise until after death. And this is why Jacob set so much value on being buried there that he bound Joseph by oath to see it done, and why Joseph wished that his bones, some ages later, long after they had crumbled into dust, should be carried there (Genesis 47:29, 30; 50:25).

14¶

In short, it is plain that throughout the whole course of their lives they had an eye to future blessedness. Why should Jacob have aspired so earnestly to the birthright, and schemed for it at so much risk, if it was to bring him only exile and destitution and no good at all, unless he looked to some higher blessing? And that this was his feeling he declared in one of the last sentences he uttered: "I have waited for thy salvation, O God" (Genesis 49:18). What salvation could he have waited for, when he felt himself breathing his last, if he did not see in death the beginning of a new life? And why speak of saints and the children of God, when even one who otherwise strove to resist the truth was not without some similar impression? For what did Balaam mean when he said, "Let me die the death of the righteous, and let my last end be like his" (Numbers 23:10), unless he was convinced of what David afterward declares, "Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints" (Psalm 116:15; 34:12)? If death were the goal and final limit, no distinction could be observed between the righteous and the wicked. The true distinction is the different lot which awaits them after death.

15¶

We have not yet come further down than the books of Moses, whose only office, according to our opponents, was to induce the people to worship God by setting before them the fertility of the land and its general abundance. And yet to everyone who does not willfully shun the light, there is clear evidence of a spiritual covenant. But if we come down to the Prophets, the kingdom of Christ and eternal life are displayed there in the fullest splendor. First David, being earlier in time, spoke of heavenly mysteries more obscurely than they, in keeping with the order of the divine procedure; and yet with what clearness and certainty he points to it in all he says! The value he set on his earthly dwelling is attested by these words: "I am a stranger with thee, and a sojourner, as all my fathers were. Verily every man at his best estate is altogether vanity. Surely every man walketh in a vain show. And now, Lord, what wait I for? my hope is in thee" (Psalm 39:12, 5, 6, 7). He who confesses that there is nothing solid or stable on earth, and yet firmly keeps his hope in God, undoubtedly contemplates a happiness reserved for him elsewhere. To this contemplation he is accustomed to invite believers whenever he would have them truly comforted. For in another passage, after speaking of human life as a fleeting and vanishing show, he adds, "The mercy of the Lord is from everlasting to everlasting upon them that fear him" (Psalm 103:17). There is a corresponding passage in another psalm: "Of old thou hast laid the foundation of the earth; and the heavens are the work of thy hands. They shall perish, but thou shalt endure; yea, all of them shall wax old like a garment; as a vesture shalt thou change them, and they shall be changed; but thou art the same, and thy years shall have no end. The children of thy servants shall continue, and their seed shall be established before thee" (Psalm 102:25-28). If, notwithstanding the destruction of the heavens and the earth, the godly do not cease to be established before God, it follows that their salvation is bound up with his eternity. But this hope could have had no existence if it did not rest on the promise as expounded by Isaiah: "The heavens shall vanish away like smoke, and the earth shall wax old like a garment, and they that dwell therein shall die in like manner; but my salvation shall be for ever, and my righteousness shall not be abolished" (Isaiah 51:6). Perpetuity is here ascribed to righteousness and salvation, not as they reside in God, but as they are experienced by men.

16¶

Nor can the things said everywhere about the prosperity of believers be understood in any other sense than as referring to the manifestation of heavenly glory. Of this kind are the following passages: "He preserveth the souls of his saints; he delivereth them out of the hand of the wicked. Light is sown for the righteous, and gladness for the upright in heart." "His righteousness endureth for ever; his horn shall be exalted with honour—the desire of the wicked shall perish." "Surely the righteous shall give thanks unto thy name; the upright shall dwell in thy presence." "The righteous shall be in everlasting remembrance." "The Lord redeemeth the soul of his servants." Psalm 97:10, 11; 112:9, 10; 140:13; 112:6; 34:22.

17¶

Therefore, even from this confession of David, let us learn that the holy fathers under the Old Testament were not ignorant that in this world God seldom or never gives his servants the fulfillment of what is promised them, and that he has therefore directed their minds to his sanctuary, where the blessings not displayed in this present shadowy life are stored up for them. This sanctuary was the final judgment of God, which, since they could not discern it at all with the eye, they were content to grasp by faith. Inspired with this confidence, they did not doubt that, whatever might happen in the world, a time would at last come when the divine promises would be fulfilled. This is attested by such expressions as these: "As for me, I will behold thy face in righteousness: I shall be satisfied, when I awake, with thy likeness" (Psalm 17:15). "I am like a green olive tree in the house of God" (Psalm 52:8). Again, "The righteous shall flourish like the palm tree: he shall grow like a cedar in Lebanon. Those that be planted in the house of the Lord shall flourish in the courts of our God. They shall still bring forth fruit in old age; they shall be fat and flourishing" (Psalm 92:12-14). A little before he had exclaimed, "O Lord, how great are thy works! and thy thoughts are very deep." "When the wicked spring as the grass, and when all the workers of iniquity do flourish: it is that they shall be destroyed for ever." Where was this splendor and beauty of the righteous, unless when the face of this world was changed by the manifestation of the heavenly kingdom? Lifting their eyes to the eternal world, they despised the momentary hardships and calamities of the present life, and confidently broke out in these exclamations: "He shall never suffer the righteous to be moved. But thou, O God, shalt bring them down into the pit of destruction: bloody and deceitful men shall not live out half their days" (Psalm 55:22, 23). Where in this world is there a pit of eternal destruction to swallow up the wicked, of whose happiness it is said elsewhere, "They spend their days in wealth, and in a moment go down to the grave" (Job 21:13)? Where, on the other hand, is the great stability of the saints, who, as David complains, are not only troubled but everywhere utterly bruised and oppressed? It is here. He set before his eyes not merely the unstable changes of the world, tossed like a troubled sea, but what the Lord will do when he shall one day sit to fix the eternal order of heaven and earth, as he elegantly describes in another place: "They that trust in their wealth, and boast themselves in the multitude of their riches; none of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him." "For he sees that wise men die, likewise the fool and the brutish person perish, and leave their wealth to others. Their inward thought is, that their houses shall continue for ever, and their dwelling-places to all generations; they call their lands after their own names. Nevertheless, man being in honour abideth not: he is like the beasts that perish. This their way is their folly: yet their posterity approve their sayings. Like sheep they are laid in the grave; death shall feed on them; and the upright shall have dominion over them in the morning; and their beauty shall consume in the grave from their dwelling" (Psalm 49:6, 7, 10-14). By this mockery of the foolish for resting content with the slippery and fickle pleasures of the world, he shows that the wise must seek a very different happiness. But he unfolds the hidden doctrine of the resurrection more clearly when he sets up a kingdom for the righteous after the wicked are cast down and destroyed. For what, I ask, are we to understand by the "morning," unless it is the revelation of a new life, beginning when the present one comes to an end?

18¶

Hence the consideration which believers used as a comfort in their sufferings and a remedy for their patience: "His anger endureth but a moment: in his favour is life" (Psalm 30:5). How did their afflictions, which continued through almost the whole course of life, end in a moment? Where did they see the long duration of the divine kindness, of which they had only the slightest taste? Had they clung to the earth, they could have found nothing of the kind; but looking to heaven, they saw that the time during which the Lord afflicted his saints was only a moment, and that the mercies with which he gathers them are everlasting. On the other hand, they foresaw that for the wicked, who only dreamed of happiness for a day, an eternal and never-ending destruction was reserved. Hence those expressions: "The memory of the just is blessed, but the name of the wicked shall rot" (Proverbs 10:7). "Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints" (Psalm 116:15). Again in Samuel: "The Lord will keep the feet of his saints, and the wicked shall be silent in darkness" (1 Samuel 2:9). These show that they knew well that, however much the righteous might be tossed about, their latter end was life and peace, and that, however pleasant the delights of the wicked, they gradually lead down to the chambers of death. They accordingly called the death of such persons the death "of the uncircumcised," that is, of persons cut off from the hope of resurrection (Ezekiel 28:10; 31:18). Hence David could imagine no greater curse than this: "Let them be blotted out of the book of the living, and not be written with the righteous" (Psalm 69:28).

19¶

The most remarkable passage of all is that of Job: "I know that my Redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth: and though after my skin worms destroy this body, yet in my flesh shall I see God: whom I shall see for myself, and mine eyes shall behold, and not another" (Job 19:25-27). Those who want to make a display of their cleverness pretend that these words are to be understood not of the last resurrection, but of the day when Job expected that God would deal more gently with him. Granting that this is partly meant, we shall nevertheless compel them, whether they will or not, to admit that Job could never have attained such fullness of hope if his thoughts had risen no higher than the earth. It must therefore be confessed that he who saw that the Redeemer would be present with him as he lay in the grave must have raised his eyes to a future immortality. To those who think only of the present life, death is the extremity of despair; but it could not destroy the hope of Job. "Though he slay me," he said, "yet will I trust in him" (Job 13:15). Let no trifler burst in here with the objection that these are the sayings of a few, and by no means prove that there was such a doctrine among the Jews. My immediate answer is that these few did not, in such passages, give voice to some hidden wisdom to which only distinguished individuals were admitted privately and apart from others. Rather, having been appointed by the Holy Spirit to be the teachers of the people, they openly proclaimed the mysteries of God, which all alike were bound to learn as the principles of public religion. When, therefore, we hear that those passages in which the Holy Spirit spoke so distinctly and clearly of the spiritual life were public oracles in the Jewish Church, it would be intolerably perverse to confine them entirely to a carnal covenant relating merely to the earth and earthly riches.

20¶

When we come down to the later prophets, we are free to range at large as in our own field. If the victory was not difficult when David, Job, and Samuel were in question, it is much easier here. For the method and order which God observed in administering the covenant of his mercy was this: the nearer the time of its full display approached, the greater were the additions made daily to the light of revelation. Accordingly, at the beginning, when the first promise of salvation was given to Adam (Genesis 3:15), only a few faint sparks shone forth. As additions were afterward made, a greater degree of light began to appear, and it continued gradually to increase and shine more brightly, until at last, all the clouds being scattered, Christ the Sun of righteousness arose and with full radiance lit up the whole earth (Malachi 4). In appealing to the Prophets, therefore, we need have no fear of any lack of proof. But I see an immense mass of material which would occupy us much longer than suits the nature of our present work (the subject, indeed, would require a large volume), and I trust that by what has already been said I have paved the way, so that every reader of even the least discernment may proceed without stumbling. I will therefore avoid a lengthiness for which there is little need at present. I only remind my readers to ease their entrance by means of the key formerly put into their hands (above, chapter 4, sections 3, 4), namely, that whenever the Prophets mention the happiness of believers (a happiness of which scarcely any traces can be seen in the present life), they must have recourse to this distinction: that the Prophets, the better to commend the divine goodness to the people, used temporal blessings as a kind of outline to shadow it forth, and yet gave such a portrait as might lift their minds above the earth, the elements of this world, and all that will perish, and compel them to think of the blessedness of a future and spiritual life.

21¶

One example will suffice. When the Israelites were carried away to Babylon, their dispersion seemed to be the next thing to death, and they could hardly be dissuaded from thinking that Ezekiel's prophecy of their restoration (Ezekiel 37:4) was a mere fable, because it seemed to them the same as if he had prophesied that rotting carcasses would be raised to life. The Lord, to show that even in that case nothing could prevent him from making room for his kindness, set before the prophet in a vision a field covered with dry bones, to which, by the mere power of his word, he restored life and strength in one moment. The vision served, indeed, to correct the unbelief of the Jews at that time, but it also reminded them how much further the power of the Lord extended than to bringing back the people, since by a single nod it could so easily give life to dry, scattered bones. The passage may therefore fitly be compared with one in Isaiah: "Thy dead men shall live, together with my dead body shall they arise. Awake and sing, ye that dwell in dust: for thy dew is as the dew of herbs, and the earth shall cast out the dead. Come, my people, enter thou into thy chambers, and shut thy doors about thee: hide thyself as it were for a little moment, until the indignation be overpast. For, behold, the Lord cometh out of his place to punish the inhabitants of the earth for their iniquity: the earth also shall disclose her blood, and shall no more cover her slain" (Isaiah 26:19-21).

22¶

It would be absurd, however, to interpret all the passages on the same principle, for there are several which point without any veil to the future immortality that awaits believers in the kingdom of heaven. Some of them we have already quoted, and there are many others, but especially the following two. The one is in Isaiah: "As the new heavens and the new earth, which I will make, shall remain before me, saith the Lord, so shall your seed and your name remain. And it shall come to pass, that from one new moon to another, and from one sabbath to another, shall all flesh come to worship before me, saith the Lord. And they shall go forth, and look upon the carcasses of the men that have transgressed against me: for their worm shall not die, neither shall their fire be quenched; and they shall be an abhorring unto all flesh" (Isaiah 66:22-24). The other passage is in Daniel: "At that time shall Michael stand up, the great prince which standeth for the children of thy people: and there shall be a time of trouble, such as there never was since there was a nation even to that same time: and at that time thy people shall be delivered, every one shall be found written in the book. And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (Daniel 12:1, 2).

23¶

In proving the two remaining points, namely, that the patriarchs had Christ as the pledge of their covenant and placed all their hope of blessing in him, I will be less detailed, since they are clearer and not so much disputed. Let us then lay it down confidently, as a truth which no engines of the devil can destroy, that the Old Testament, or covenant, which the Lord made with the people of Israel was not confined to earthly things, but contained a promise of spiritual and eternal life, the expectation of which had to be impressed on the minds of all who truly consented to the covenant. Let us put far from us the senseless and pernicious notion that the Lord set nothing before the Jews, or that they sought nothing, but full supplies of food, carnal delights, abundance of wealth, outward influence, numerous offspring, and all those things which our animal nature counts valuable. For even now the only kingdom of heaven which our Lord Jesus Christ promises to his followers is one in which they may sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (Matthew 8:11). And Peter declared of the Jews of his day that they were heirs of gospel grace because they were the sons of the prophets and were included in the covenant which the Lord made of old with his people (Acts 3:25). And so that this might not be attested by words alone, our Lord also confirmed it by deed (Matthew 27:52). At the moment when he rose again, he was pleased to make many of the saints partakers of his resurrection, and allowed them to be seen in the city, thus giving a sure pledge that everything he did and suffered in purchasing eternal salvation belonged to believers under the Old Testament just as much as to us. Indeed, as Peter testifies, they were endowed with the same spirit of faith by which we are regenerated to life (Acts 15:8). When we hear that the Spirit, who is, as it were, a kind of spark of immortality in us (and is therefore called the "earnest" of our inheritance, Ephesians 1:14), dwelt in them in the same way, how can we presume to deny them the inheritance? Hence it is all the more astonishing that the Sadducees of old fell into such stupidity as to deny both the resurrection and the substantive existence "Animarum substantiam." French, "immortalité des ames"; that is, immortality of souls.

Calvin labors to prove that the old covenant and the new are, in reality, one — one salvation, one Mediator, one hope of immortality — differently administered across the ages but never two rival ways to God. The fathers under Moses fed on the same Christ we feed on, looked to the same heavenly inheritance, were bound in the same gracious covenant. For a church in 2026, tempted to chop the Scriptures into incompatible eras or to imagine the God of the older books a stranger to the God of the gospel, this unity is steadying ground. The whole Bible is one book because it tells one story of redeeming mercy. Teach your children to read it whole — to find Christ in the manna and the rock as surely as in the upper room — and to trust that the God who kept covenant with patriarchs through fire and exile keeps it still with us. Steadfastness grows in those who see that the faithfulness running through every age is the very faithfulness now pledged to them.
