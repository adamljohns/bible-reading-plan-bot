1¶

From the earlier part of this work we see clearly how completely destitute of all good man is, and how devoid of every means of securing his own salvation. Therefore, if he would obtain help in his need, he must go outside himself and seek it from another quarter. It has further been shown that the Lord kindly and freely reveals himself in Christ, in whom he offers all happiness for our misery and all abundance for our want, opening up the treasures of heaven to us, so that we may turn with full faith to his beloved Son, depend on him with full expectation, rest in him, and cling to him with full hope. This, indeed, is that secret and hidden philosophy which cannot be learned by syllogisms: a philosophy thoroughly understood by those whose eyes God has so opened that they see light in his light (Psalm 36:9). But once we have learned by faith that whatever is necessary for us or lacking in us is supplied in God and in our Lord Jesus Christ, in whom it pleased the Father that all fullness should dwell, so that we may draw from him as from an inexhaustible fountain, it remains for us to seek in him, and in prayer to ask of him, what we have learned is in him. To know God as the sovereign giver of all good, who invites us to present our requests, and yet not to approach him or ask of him, would be so far from profiting us that it would be just as if a man told of a treasure were to leave it buried in the ground. Therefore the Apostle, to show that a faith not accompanied by prayer to God cannot be genuine, states this to be the order: as faith springs from the Gospel, so by faith our hearts are trained to call upon the name of God (Romans 10:14). And this is the very thing he had expressed somewhat earlier, namely, that the Spirit of adoption, who seals the testimony of the Gospel on our hearts, gives us courage to make our requests known to God, calls forth groanings which cannot be uttered, and enables us to cry, Abba, Father (Romans 8:26). Since we have so far only touched on this last point briefly and in passing, it must now be treated more fully.

2¶

It is to prayer, then, that we owe our access to those riches which are stored up for us with our heavenly Father. For there is a kind of communion between God and men by which, having entered the heavenly sanctuary, they appear before him and appeal to his promises, so that when need requires they may learn by experience that what they believed merely on the authority of his word was not in vain. Accordingly, we see that nothing is set before us to expect from the Lord which we are not also commanded to ask of him in prayer; so true is it that prayer digs up those treasures which the Gospel of our Lord reveals to the eye of faith. No words can sufficiently express the necessity and usefulness of this exercise of prayer. Certainly it is not without reason that our heavenly Father declares that our only safety lies in calling upon his name, since by it we summon the presence of his providence to watch over our interests, of his power to sustain us when we are weak and almost fainting, and of his goodness to receive us into favor, though we are miserably weighed down with sin; in short, we call upon him to show himself to us in all his perfections. From this, wonderful peace and calm are given to our consciences; for when the straits that pressed upon us have been laid before the Lord, we rest fully satisfied in the assurance that none of our troubles is unknown to him, and that he is both able and willing to make the best provision for us.

3¶

But someone will say: Does he not know, without anyone to remind him, both what our difficulties are and what is good for us, so that it seems in some measure superfluous to petition him with our prayers, as if he were dozing, or even asleep, until roused by the sound of our voice? French, "Dont il sembleroit que ce fust chose superflue de le soliciter par prieres; veu que nous avons accoustumé de soliciter ceux qui ne pensent à nostre affaire, et qui sont endormis." That is: From which it would seem that it was a superfluous thing to petition him by prayer, seeing that we are accustomed to petition those who are not thinking of our business, and who are asleep.

4¶

Let the first rule of right prayer, then, be that our heart and mind are disposed as befits those who are entering into conversation with God. We shall achieve this with regard to the mind if, laying aside carnal thoughts and cares which might hinder the direct and pure contemplation of God, it is not only wholly intent on prayer but is also, as far as possible, carried and lifted above itself. I do not here demand a mind so free that it feels none of the gnawings of anxiety; on the contrary, it is by much anxiety that the fervor of prayer is kindled. So we see that the holy servants of God show great anguish, not to say distress, when they cause the voice of complaint to rise to the Lord from the deep abyss and the jaws of death. What I am saying is that all foreign and extraneous cares must be driven out, by which the mind might be tossed to and fro in aimless suspense, drawn down from heaven, and kept groveling on the earth. When I say that it must be lifted above itself, I mean that it must not bring into the presence of God any of those things which our blind and foolish reason is accustomed to invent, nor keep itself shut up within the small measure of its own vanity, but must rise to a purity worthy of God.

5¶

Both points deserve special notice. First, let everyone who undertakes to pray turn all his thoughts and feelings to it, and not be distracted (as commonly happens) by wandering thoughts; for nothing is more contrary to the reverence due to God than that frivolity which reveals a mind too much given to license and lacking in fear. In this matter we ought to labor all the more earnestly the more difficult we find it to be; for no one is so intent on prayer that he does not feel many thoughts creeping in, which either break off the course of his prayer or delay it by some detour or digression. Here let us consider how unfitting it is, when God admits us to familiar communion with himself, to abuse his great condescension by mixing sacred things with profane, with no reverence for him holding our minds in check; but, just as if in prayer we were talking with someone like ourselves, to forget him and let our thoughts run to and fro. Let us understand, then, that none prepare themselves rightly for prayer except those who are so impressed with the majesty of God that they come to it free from all earthly cares and affections. The ceremony of lifting up our hands in prayer is meant to remind us that we are far removed from God unless our thoughts rise upward, as it is said in the psalm, "Unto you, O Lord, do I lift up my soul" (Psalm 25:1). And Scripture repeatedly uses the expression to raise our prayer, meaning that those who would be heard by God must not grovel in the mire. The sum is this: the more generously God deals with us, graciously inviting us to unload our cares into his bosom, the less excusable we are if this wonderful and incomparable blessing does not, in our estimation, outweigh all other things and win our affection, so that prayer may seriously engage our every thought and feeling. This cannot be unless our mind, exerting itself vigorously against every obstacle, rises upward. Our second proposition was that we are to ask only so far as God permits. For though he bids us pour out our hearts (Psalm 62:8), he does not indiscriminately give free rein to foolish and depraved desires; and when he promises that he will grant believers their wish, his indulgence does not go so far as to yield to their whims. In both matters serious faults are committed everywhere. For not only do many, without modesty and without reverence, presume to call on God about their trifles, but they shamelessly bring their dreams, whatever they may be, before the judgment seat of God. Such is the folly or stupidity under which they labor that they have the audacity to thrust upon God desires so vile that they would blush deeply to share them with their fellow men. Pagan writers mocked this presumption and even expressed their loathing of it, and yet the vice has always prevailed. So, just as the ambitious adopted Jupiter as their patron, the greedy Mercury, the seekers of learning Apollo and Minerva, the warlike Mars, and the licentious Venus, so in our own day, as I noted a moment ago, men in prayer give freer rein to their unlawful desires than if they were telling jesting stories among their equals. God does not allow his condescension to be mocked in this way, but, asserting his own right, he places our wishes under the restraint of his authority. We must therefore heed the statement of John, "This is the confidence that we have in him, that if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us" (1 John 5:14). But since our faculties are far from able to attain such high perfection, we must look for some means to help them. As the eye of our mind should be fixed on God, so the affection of our heart ought to follow the same course. But both fall far short of this, or rather they faint and fail and are carried in the opposite direction. To help this weakness, God gives us the guidance of the Spirit in our prayers, to dictate what is right and to regulate our affections. For since "we know not what we should pray for as we ought," "the Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered" (Romans 8:26); not that he actually prays or groans, but he stirs up in us sighs, desires, and confidence which our natural powers are wholly unable to conceive. Nor is it without reason that Paul gives the name of groanings which cannot be uttered to the prayers which believers send up under the guidance of the Spirit. For those who are truly practiced in prayer know well that blind anxieties so restrain and confuse them that they can scarcely find what they ought to say; indeed, when they try to stammer something out, they falter and hesitate. From this it is clear that to pray rightly is a special gift. We do not say this to indulge our sloth, as if we were to leave the work of prayer to the Holy Spirit and give way to that carelessness to which we are all too prone. Thus we sometimes hear the impious saying that we are to wait in suspense until he takes possession of our minds while they are busy with other things. Our meaning is that, weary of our own halfheartedness and sloth, we are to long for the help of the Spirit. Nor indeed does Paul, when he commands us to pray in the Spirit (1 Corinthians 14:15), cease to urge us to watchfulness, indicating that while the inspiration of the Spirit is effective in forming prayer, it in no way hinders or slows our own efforts; for in this matter God is pleased to test how effectively faith moves our hearts.

6¶

Another rule of prayer is that in asking we must always truly feel our needs, and, seriously considering that we need all the things we ask for, we must accompany our prayer with a sincere, indeed an ardent, desire to obtain them. Many recite prayers in a perfunctory way from a set form, as if they were discharging a task for God; and though they confess that this is a necessary remedy for the evils of their condition, since it would be fatal to be left without the divine help which they ask for, it is still evident that they perform the duty out of habit, because their minds are cold all the while and they do not consider what they are asking. A general and confused sense of their need leads them to pray, but it does not make them anxious, as in a matter of present urgency, to obtain the supply of their need. Moreover, can we imagine anything more hateful, or even more detestable, to God than this pretense of asking pardon for sins while the one who asks either thinks at that very moment that he is not a sinner, or at least is not thinking that he is a sinner? In other words, it is a pretense by which God is openly mocked. But mankind, as I said a moment ago, is so full of depravity that, by way of perfunctory service, men often ask of God many things which they think come to them without his kindness, or from some other source, or are already securely in their possession. There is another fault which seems less serious, but is not to be tolerated. Some mumble prayers without meditation, their only principle being that God must be appeased by prayer. Believers ought to be especially on guard never to appear in God's presence intending to present a request unless they are moved by some serious conviction and are at the same time eager to obtain what they ask. Indeed, even in those things which we ask only for the glory of God, where at first sight we do not seem to be providing for our own need, we ought to ask with no less fervor and intensity of desire. For instance, when we pray that his name be hallowed, that hallowing must, so to speak, be earnestly hungered and thirsted for.

7¶

If it is objected that the need which drives us to pray is not always equal, I grant it, and this distinction is usefully taught us by James: "Is any among you afflicted? let him pray. Is any merry? let him sing psalms" (James 5:13). Common sense itself, therefore, tells us that, since we are too sluggish, we must be stirred up by God to pray earnestly whenever the occasion calls for it. David calls this a time when God "may be found" (a fitting time); because, as he declares in several other passages, the more severely grievances, troubles, fears, and other kinds of trial press upon us, the freer is our access to God, as if he were inviting us to himself. Yet no less true is Paul's command to pray "always" (Ephesians 6:18); because, however prosperously things may go in our view, and however much we may be surrounded on every side with reasons for joy, there is not a moment of time in which our need does not urge us to prayer. A man has plenty of wheat and wine; but since he cannot enjoy a morsel of bread except by the continual bounty of God, his granaries or cellars will not keep him from asking for daily bread. Then, if we consider how many dangers hang over us every moment, fear itself will teach us that no time ought to be without prayer. This, however, may be better seen in spiritual matters. For when will the many sins of which we are conscious allow us to sit at ease without humbly begging release from guilt and punishment? When will temptation grant us a truce, so that there is no need to hurry for help? Moreover, zeal for the kingdom and glory of God ought not to seize us by fits and starts, but to drive us without ceasing, so that every time should seem fitting. It is not without reason, therefore, that diligence in prayer is so often commanded. I am not now speaking of perseverance, which will be considered later; but Scripture, by reminding us of the necessity of constant prayer, charges us with sloth, because we do not feel how much we stand in need of this care and diligence. By this rule hypocrisy and the device of lying to God are restrained, indeed altogether banished from prayer. God promises that he will be near to those who call upon him in truth, and declares that those who seek him with their whole heart will find him; those, therefore, who delight in their own filth certainly cannot aspire to him. One of the requirements of lawful prayer is repentance. Hence the frequent declaration of Scripture that God does not listen to the wicked, and that their prayers, as well as their sacrifices, are an abomination to him. For it is right that those who seal up their hearts should find the ears of God closed against them, and that those who provoke his severity by their hardness of heart should find him unbending. In Isaiah he threatens thus: "When you make many prayers, I will not hear: your hands are full of blood" (Isaiah 1:15). Likewise in Jeremiah: "Though they shall cry unto me, I will not hearken unto them" (Jeremiah 11:7, 8, 11); because he regards it as the highest insult for the wicked to boast of his covenant while profaning his sacred name by their whole lives. Hence he complains in Isaiah: "This people draw near to me with their mouth, and with their lips do honor me; but have removed their heart far from me" (Isaiah 29:13). Indeed, he does not limit this to prayers alone, but declares that he detests pretense in every part of his worship. Hence the words of James: "You ask, and receive not, because you ask amiss, that you may consume it upon your lusts" (James 4:3). It is true, indeed (as we shall see again shortly), that the godly, in the prayers they offer, do not trust in their own worth; still the warning of John is not superfluous: "Whatsoever we ask, we receive of him, because we keep his commandments" (1 John 3:22); an evil conscience shuts the door against us. It follows that none but the sincere worshipers of God pray rightly or are heard. Let everyone, therefore, who prepares to pray be displeased with what is wrong in his condition, and take on, which he cannot do without repentance, the character and feelings of a poor suppliant.

8¶

The third rule to be added is this: he who comes into the presence of God to pray must strip himself of all vainglorious thoughts and lay aside every notion of his own worth; in short, he must cast off all self-confidence, humbly giving God the whole glory, lest by claiming anything for himself, however little, he should through vain pride cause God to turn away his face. Of this submission, which casts down all haughtiness, we have many examples in the servants of God. The holier they are, the more humbly they bow down when they come into the presence of the Lord. So Daniel, on whom the Lord himself bestowed such high praise, says, "We do not present our supplications before you for our righteousness, but for your great mercies. O Lord, hear; O Lord, forgive; O Lord, hearken and do; defer not, for your own sake, O my God: for your city and your people are called by your name." He does not do this indirectly, in the usual way, as if he were one individual in a crowd; rather, he confesses his guilt on his own account, and, as a suppliant fleeing to the refuge of pardon, he plainly declares that he was confessing his own sin and the sin of his people Israel (Daniel 9:18-20). David also sets us an example of this humility: "Enter not into judgment with your servant: for in your sight shall no man living be justified" (Psalm 143:2). In the same way Isaiah prays: "Behold, you are wroth; for we have sinned: in those is continuance, and we shall be saved. But we are all as an unclean thing, and all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags; and we all do fade as a leaf; and our iniquities, like the wind, have taken us away. And there is none that calls upon your name, that stirs up himself to take hold of you: for you have hidden your face from us, and have consumed us, because of our iniquities. But now, O Lord, you are our Father; we are the clay, and you our potter; and we all are the work of your hand. Be not wroth very sore, O Lord, neither remember iniquity for ever: Behold, see, we beseech you, we are all your people" (Isaiah 64:5-9). You see how they place no confidence in anything except this: that, considering they are the Lord's, they do not despair of being the objects of his care. In the same way Jeremiah says, "O Lord, though our iniquities testify against us, do it for your name's sake" (Jeremiah 14:7). For it was most truly and devoutly written by the uncertain author (whoever he may have been) of the book attributed to the prophet Baruch. French, "Pourtant ce qui est escrit en la prophetie qu'on attribue à Baruch, combien que l'autheur soit incertain, est tres sainctement dit;" that is: However, what is written in the prophecy attributed to Baruch, though the author is uncertain, is very holily said.

9¶

In short, a plea for pardon, with a humble and honest confession of guilt, forms both the preparation for and the beginning of right prayer. For even the holiest of men cannot hope to obtain anything from God until he has been freely reconciled to him. God cannot be favorable to any but those whom he pardons. It is no wonder, then, that this is the key with which believers open the door of prayer, as we learn from several passages in the Psalms. David, when presenting a request on a different subject, says, "Remember not the sins of my youth, nor my transgressions: according to your mercy remember me, for your goodness' sake, O Lord" (Psalm 25:7). Again, "Look upon my affliction and my pain, and forgive my sins" (Psalm 25:18). Here also we see that it is not enough to call ourselves to account for the sins of each passing day; we must also call to mind those which might seem to have been long buried in oblivion. For in another passage the same prophet, confessing one grievous crime, takes the occasion to go back to his very birth: "I was shapen in iniquity, and in sin did my mother conceive me" (Psalm 51:5); not to lessen the fault by appeal to the corruption of his nature, but, as it were, to heap together the sins of his whole life, so that the stricter he was in condemning himself, the more readily God might be appeased. But although the saints do not always ask forgiveness of sins in express words, yet if we carefully weigh their prayers as recorded in Scripture, the truth of what I say will readily appear, namely, that their courage to pray came solely from the mercy of God, and that they always began by seeking to appease him. For when a man questions his own conscience, he is so far from presuming to lay his cares familiarly before God that, if he did not trust in mercy and pardon, he would tremble at the very thought of approaching him. There is, indeed, another, special confession. When believers long for deliverance from punishment, they at the same time pray that their sins may be pardoned. French, "il recognoissent le chastiement qu'ils ont merité;" that is, they acknowledge the punishment which they have deserved.

The French adds, "Ils voudront qu'on leur oste le mal de teste et des reins, et seront contens qu'on ne touche point a la fievre;" that is: They would wish to be rid of the pain in the head and the loins, and would be content to leave the fever untouched.

10¶

Sometimes, however, the saints in their supplications to God seem to appeal to their own righteousness, as when David says, "Preserve my soul; for I am holy" (Psalm 86:2). So also Hezekiah: "Remember now, O Lord, I beseech you, how I have walked before you in truth, and with a perfect heart, and have done that which is good in your sight" (Isaiah 38:2). All they mean by such expressions is that their regeneration shows them to be among the servants and children to whom God pledges that he will show favor. We have already seen how he declares through the Psalmist that his eyes "are upon the righteous, and his ears are open unto their cry" (Psalm 34:16), and again through the apostle that "whatsoever we ask of him we obtain, because we keep his commandments" (John 3:22). In these passages he does not assign a value to prayer as a meritorious work, but intends to establish the confidence of those who are conscious of an unfeigned integrity and innocence, such as all believers ought to have. For the saying of the blind man who had received his sight is in perfect agreement with divine truth, And God heareth not sinners (John 9:31); provided that we take the term sinners in the sense in which Scripture commonly uses it, to mean those who, without any desire for righteousness, are sleeping secure in their sins; for no heart will ever rise to genuine prayer that does not at the same time long for holiness. Those supplications in which the saints refer to their purity and integrity correspond to such promises, so that they may have in their own experience a demonstration of what all the servants of God are taught to expect. Thus they almost always use this form of prayer when they compare themselves before God with their enemies, from whose injustice they long to be delivered by his hand. In making such comparisons, it is no wonder that they bring forward their integrity and simplicity of heart, so that by the justice of their cause the Lord may be all the more disposed to help them. We do not rob the godly heart of the privilege of enjoying a sense of its purity before the Lord, and so of feeling assured of the promises with which he comforts and upholds his true worshipers; but we would have them lay aside every thought of their own merits and base their confidence of success in prayer solely on the divine mercy.

11¶

The fourth rule of prayer is that, even though we are thus brought low and truly humbled, we should be encouraged to pray with the sure hope of success. There is, indeed, an appearance of contradiction between the two things, between a sense of God's just vengeance and a firm confidence in his favor; and yet they agree perfectly, if it is the sheer goodness of God that raises up those who are overwhelmed by their own sins. For as we showed earlier (chapter 3, sections 1, 2) that repentance and faith go hand in hand, joined by an unbreakable bond, the one producing terror and the other joy, so in prayer both must be present. David expresses this union in a few words: "But as for me, I will come into your house in the multitude of your mercy: and in your fear will I worship toward your holy temple" (Psalm 5:7). Under the goodness of God he includes faith, while not excluding fear; for not only does his majesty compel our reverence, but our own unworthiness also strips us of all pride and confidence and keeps us in fear. The confidence I speak of is not one which frees the mind from all anxiety and lulls it in sweet and perfect rest; such rest belongs to those who, while all their affairs are going just as they wish, are troubled by no care, stung by no regret, and shaken by no fear. But the best spur the saints have to prayer is when, because of their own needs, they feel the greatest unrest and are all but driven to despair, until faith comes to their aid at the right moment; for in such straits the goodness of God so shines upon them that, while they groan, burdened by the weight of present calamities and tormented by the fear of greater ones, they still trust in this goodness, and in this way both lighten the hardship of enduring and take comfort in the hope of final deliverance. It is necessary, therefore, that the prayer of the believer should arise from both feelings and show the influence of both; namely, that while he groans under present evils and anxiously dreads new ones, he should at the same time flee to God, not doubting at all that God is ready to stretch out a helping hand to him. For it is hard to say how much God is provoked by our distrust when we ask of his goodness what we do not expect from it. Nothing, therefore, is more in keeping with the nature of prayer than to lay it down as a fixed rule that it is not to burst forth at random, but is to follow in the footsteps of faith. To this principle Christ directs us all in these words: "Therefore I say unto you, What things soever you desire, when you pray, believe that you receive them, and you shall have them" (Mark 11:24). He declares the same thing in another passage: "All things, whatsoever you shall ask in prayer, believing, you shall receive" (Matthew 21:22). In agreement with this are the words of James: "If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God, that giveth to all men liberally, and upbraideth not; and it shall be given him. But let him ask in faith, nothing wavering" (James 1:5). He expresses the power of faith most aptly by setting it against wavering. No less worthy of notice is his further statement, that those who approach God with a doubting, hesitating mind, not feeling sure whether they will be heard or not, gain nothing by their prayers. He compares such people to a wave of the sea, driven by the wind and tossed. Hence in another passage he calls genuine prayer "the prayer of faith" (James 5:15). Again, since God so often declares that he will give to every man according to his faith, he indicates that we cannot obtain anything without faith. In short, it is faith which obtains everything that is granted to prayer. This is Paul's meaning in the well-known passage to which dull men pay too little attention: "How then shall they call on him in whom they have not believed? and how shall they believe in him of whom they have not heard?" "So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God" (Romans 10:14, 17). Tracing the origin of prayer step by step back to faith, he plainly maintains that God cannot be sincerely called upon except by those to whom his mercy and willingness have been made known, indeed familiarly explained, through the preaching of the Gospel.

12¶

Our opponents give no thought at all to this necessity. Therefore, when we say that believers ought to feel firmly assured, they think we are saying the most absurd thing in the world. But if they had any experience of true prayer, they would certainly understand that God cannot be rightly called upon without this firm sense of the divine goodwill. But since no one can truly perceive the power of faith without at the same time feeling it in his heart, what good is there in arguing with men of this kind, who plainly show that they have never had more than an empty imagination? The value and necessity of that assurance for which we contend is learned chiefly from prayer. Anyone who does not see this gives proof of a very dull conscience. Leaving aside, then, those who are so blinded, let us fix our thoughts on the words of Paul, that God can be called upon only by those who have gained a knowledge of his mercy from the Gospel and feel firmly assured that this mercy is ready to be given to them. What kind of prayer would this be? "O Lord, I am indeed in doubt whether or not you are inclined to hear me; but, weighed down with anxiety, I flee to you, so that, if I am worthy, you may help me." None of the saints whose prayers are recorded in Scripture made supplication in this way. Nor are we taught so by the Holy Spirit, who tells us to "come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need" (Hebrews 4:16); and elsewhere teaches us to "have boldness and access with confidence by the faith of Christ" (Ephesians 3:12). This confidence of obtaining what we ask, a confidence which the Lord commands and all the saints teach by their example, we must therefore hold fast with both hands if we would pray with any profit. The only prayer acceptable to God is that which springs (if I may put it so) from this presumption of faith and is founded on the full assurance of hope. He might have been content to use the simple name of faith, but he adds not only confidence but liberty or boldness, so that by this mark he might distinguish us from unbelievers, who indeed pray to God as we do, but pray at random. Therefore the whole Church prays thus: "Let your mercy, O Lord, be upon us, according as we hope in you" (Psalm 33:22). The Psalmist lays down the same condition in another passage: "When I cry unto you, then shall my enemies turn back: this I know; for God is for me" (Psalm 56:9). Again, "In the morning will I direct my prayer unto you, and will look up" (Psalm 5:3). From these words we gather that prayers are poured out into the air in vain unless they are accompanied by faith, in which, as from a watchtower, we may quietly wait for God. The order of Paul's exhortation agrees with this. For before urging believers to pray in the Spirit always, with watchfulness and diligence, he commands them to take up "the shield of faith," "the helmet of salvation, and the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God" (Ephesians 6:16-18). Let the reader here recall what I observed earlier, that faith by no means fails though it is accompanied by an acknowledgment of our wretchedness, poverty, and filth. However much believers may feel that they are weighed down by a heavy load of iniquity, and are not only empty of everything that could win God's favor for them but justly burdened with many sins which make him an object of dread, they still do not cease to present themselves; this feeling does not deter them from appearing in his presence, because there is no other way of access to him. Genuine prayer is not that by which we arrogantly exalt ourselves before God or set a high value on anything of our own, but that by which, while confessing our guilt, we pour out our sorrows before God, just as children familiarly lay their complaints before their parents. Indeed, the vast heap of our sins should rather spur us on and urge us to prayer. The Psalmist gives us an example of this: "Heal my soul; for I have sinned against you" (Psalm 41:4). I admit, indeed, that these stings would prove deadly darts if God did not come to our help; but our heavenly Father, in his unspeakable kindness, has added a remedy by which, calming all our turmoil, soothing our cares, and driving away our fears, he graciously draws us to himself; indeed, by removing all doubts, not to say obstacles, he makes the way smooth before us.

13¶

And first, in commanding us to pray, he by the very command convicts us of impious stubbornness if we do not obey. He could not give a more explicit command than the one contained in the Psalms: "Call upon me in the day of trouble" (Psalm 50:15). But since there is no duty of godliness more often commanded in Scripture, there is no need to dwell on it longer here. "Ask," says our divine Master, "and it shall be given you; seek, and you shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you" (Matthew 7:7). Here, indeed, a promise is added to the command, and this is necessary. For though all admit that we must obey the command, the greater part would still shrink from God's invitation if he did not promise that he would listen and be ready to answer. These two points being laid down, it is certain that all who quibblingly claim that they are not to come to God directly are not only rebellious and disobedient but are also convicted of unbelief, since they distrust the promises. There is all the more reason to note this, because hypocrites, under a pretense of humility and modesty, proudly despise the command and also refuse all belief to God's gracious invitation; indeed, they rob him of a chief part of his worship. For when he rejected sacrifices, in which all holiness then seemed to consist, he declared that the chief thing, that which above all others is precious in his sight, is to be called upon in the day of need. Therefore, when he demands what is his own and urges us to be eager in obeying, no pretexts for doubt, however plausible they may be, can excuse us. Hence all the passages throughout Scripture in which we are commanded to pray are set up before our eyes like so many banners, to inspire us with confidence. It would be presumption to come forward into the presence of God if he did not anticipate us with his invitation. Accordingly, he opens the way for us by his own voice: "I will say, It is my people: and they shall say, The Lord is my God" (Zechariah 13:9). We see how he goes before his worshipers and wants them to follow, and so we need not fear that the song which he himself dictates will prove displeasing. Above all, let us call to mind that noble description of the divine character, by trusting in which we shall easily overcome every obstacle: "O you that hear prayer, unto you shall all flesh come" (Psalm 65:2). What can be more lovely or more comforting than to see God clothed with a title which assures us that nothing is more proper to his nature than to listen to the prayers of suppliants? From this the Psalmist infers that free access is given not to a few individuals but to all men, since God addresses all in these terms: "Call upon me in the day of trouble: I will deliver you, and you shall glorify me" (Psalm 50:15). Accordingly, David appeals to the promise so given in order to obtain what he asks: "For you, O Lord of hosts, God of Israel, have revealed to your servant, saying, I will build you a house: therefore has your servant found in his heart to pray this prayer unto you" (2 Samuel 7:27). From this we infer that he would have been afraid but for the promise which emboldened him. So in another passage he strengthens himself with the general doctrine, "He will fulfill the desire of them that fear him" (Psalm 145:19). Indeed, we may notice in the Psalms how the flow of prayer is broken, and a turn is made at one moment to the power of God, at another to his goodness, at another to the faithfulness of his promises. It might seem that David, by introducing these thoughts, untimely cuts his prayers short; but believers know well by experience that their ardor grows faint unless new fuel is added, and therefore that meditation on the nature as well as on the word of God during prayer is by no means superfluous. Let us not refuse to imitate the example of David, and let us bring in thoughts which may revive our faint minds with new vigor.

14¶

It is strange that these delightful promises affect us so coldly, or hardly at all, that most men prefer to wander up and down, forsaking the fountain of living waters and hewing out for themselves broken cisterns, rather than embrace the divine generosity freely offered to them. "The name of the Lord," says Solomon, "is a strong tower: the righteous runneth into it, and is safe." Joel, after foretelling the terrible disaster that was at hand, adds this memorable sentence: "And it shall come to pass, that whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be delivered." We know that this properly refers to the course of the Gospel. Scarcely one in a hundred is moved to come into the presence of God, though he himself cries out through Isaiah, "And it shall come to pass, that before they call, I will answer; and while they are yet speaking, I will hear." This honor he elsewhere bestows on the whole Church in general, as belonging to all the members of Christ: "He shall call upon me, and I will answer him: I will be with him in trouble; I will deliver him, and honor him." 15¶

Here several questions are raised by way of objection. Scripture relates that God sometimes granted certain prayers which had been prompted by minds that were not properly calmed or governed. It is true that the cause for which Jotham called down on the inhabitants of Shechem the disaster which later befell them was well founded; but he was still inflamed with anger and revenge (Judges 9:20); and so God, by granting the curse, seems to approve of passionate impulses. A similar heat also seized Samson when he prayed, "Strengthen me, I pray you, only this once, O God, that I may be at once avenged of the Philistines for my two eyes" (Judges 16:28). For although there was some mixture of good zeal, his ruling feeling was a fiery, and therefore sinful, longing for vengeance. God grants it, and so it might apparently be inferred that prayers are effective even when they are not framed according to the rule of the word. But I answer, first, that a perpetual law is not abolished by isolated examples; and, secondly, that special promptings have sometimes been given to a few individuals, whose case is thereby different from that of men in general. For we should note the answer which our Savior gave to his disciples when they thoughtlessly wished to imitate the example of Elijah: "You know not what manner of spirit you are of" (Luke 9:55). We must, however, go further and say that the wishes which God grants are not always pleasing to him; but he grants them because it is necessary, by way of example, to give clear proof of the doctrine of Scripture, namely, that he helps the wretched and hears the groans of those who, being unjustly afflicted, beg for his aid; and accordingly he carries out his judgments when the complaints of the needy rise to him, though in themselves they are unworthy of attention. For how often, in punishing the ungodly for cruelty, plunder, violence, lust, and other crimes, in curbing audacity and fury, and also in overthrowing tyrannical power, has he declared that he gives help to those who are undeservedly oppressed, even though by calling on an unknown deity they only beat the air? There is one psalm which clearly teaches that prayers are not without effect even though they do not reach heaven by faith (Psalm 107:6, 13, 19). For it lists the prayers which, by natural instinct, need wrings from unbelievers no less than from believers, and it shows by the outcome that God is nevertheless favorable to them. Is it to testify by such readiness to hear that their prayers are pleasing to him? Not at all; it is, first, to magnify and display his mercy by the fact that even the wishes of unbelievers are not refused; and, secondly, to stir up his true worshipers to more urgent prayer, when they see that sometimes even the wailings of the ungodly are not without effect. This, however, is no reason why believers should depart from the law divinely laid upon them, or envy unbelievers, as if they gained much in obtaining what they wished. We have observed (chapter 3, section 25) that in this way God yielded to the feigned repentance of Ahab, in order to show how ready he is to listen to his elect when they seek his favor with true contrition. Accordingly, he rebukes the Jews because, soon after experiencing his readiness to listen to their prayers, they returned to their own perverse inclinations. It is also plain from the Book of Judges that whenever they wept, though their tears were deceitful, they were delivered from the hands of their enemies. Therefore, as God sends his sun without distinction on the evil and on the good, so he does not despise the tears of those who have a good cause and whose sorrows deserve relief. Meanwhile, though he hears them, this has no more to do with salvation than the supply of food which he gives to other despisers of his goodness. There seems to be a more difficult question concerning Abraham and Samuel, the first of whom, without any instruction from the word of God, prayed on behalf of the people of Sodom, while the other, against an express prohibition, prayed on behalf of Saul (Genesis 18:23; 1 Samuel 15:11). The case of Jeremiah is similar, who prayed that the city might not be destroyed (Jeremiah 32:16). It is true that their prayers were refused, but it seems harsh to assert that they prayed without faith. Modest readers will, I hope, be satisfied with this solution, namely, that, relying on the general principle by which God commands us to be merciful even to the unworthy, they were not wholly without faith, though in this particular case their wish was disappointed. Augustine shrewdly remarks, "How do the saints pray in faith when they ask of God what is contrary to what he has decreed? Namely, because they pray according to his will, not his hidden and unchangeable will, but that which he suggests to them, so that he may hear them in another way; as he wisely distinguishes" (Augustine, City of God, book 22, chapter 2). This is truly said: for in his incomprehensible counsel he so governs events that the prayers of the saints, though they involve a mixture of faith and error, are not in vain. And yet this no more warrants imitation than it excuses the saints themselves, who, I do not deny, went beyond proper bounds. Therefore, whenever no certain promise exists, our request to God must have a condition attached to it. Here we may refer to the prayer of David, "Awake for me to the judgment that you have commanded" (Psalm 7:6); for he reminds us that he had received special instruction to pray for a temporal blessing. The French adds, "dequel il n'eust pas autrement esté asseuré;" that is, of which he would not otherwise have felt assured.

16¶

It is also important to note that the four laws of prayer which I have discussed are not so rigorously enforced that God rejects the prayers in which he does not find perfect faith or repentance, accompanied by fervent zeal and rightly ordered desires. We have said (section 4) that though prayer is the familiar communion of believers with God, reverence and modesty must still be observed: we must not give free rein to our wishes, nor long for anything beyond what God permits; and, moreover, so that the majesty of God may not be despised, our minds must be lifted up to a pure and chaste reverence. No man has ever done this with complete perfection. For, not to speak of men in general, how often do David's complaints smack of excess? Not that he actually means to argue with God or to grumble at his judgments, but, faltering through weakness, he finds no better comfort than to pour his griefs into the bosom of his heavenly Father. Indeed, God bears even with our stammering, and pardon is granted to our ignorance as often as anything rash escapes us; in fact, without this indulgence we should have no freedom to pray. But although it was David's intention to submit himself entirely to the will of God, and he prayed with no less patience than fervor, still disordered emotions appear, indeed sometimes burst out, emotions not a little at odds with the first law we laid down. In particular, we may see in a verse of the thirty-ninth Psalm how this saint was carried away by the force of his grief and was unable to keep within bounds. "O spare me." Latin, "Desine a me." French, "Retire-toy"; that is, Withdraw from me.

18¶

And we must pay careful attention to the matter of time. Christ commands his disciples to have recourse to his intercession after he has ascended to heaven: "At that day you shall ask in my name" (John 16:26). It is certain, indeed, that from the very beginning all who ever prayed were heard only for the sake of the Mediator. For this reason God had commanded in the Law that the priest alone should enter the sanctuary, bearing the names of the twelve tribes of Israel on his shoulders and as many precious stones on his breast, while the people were to stand at a distance in the outer court and then join their prayers with the priest's. Indeed, the sacrifice even served to ratify and confirm their prayers. That shadowy ceremony of the Law therefore taught, first, that we are all shut out from the face of God, and that there is therefore need of a Mediator to appear in our name, to carry us on his shoulders and keep us bound upon his breast, so that we may be heard in his person; and, secondly, that our prayers, which, as has been said, would otherwise never be free from impurity, are cleansed by the sprinkling of his blood. And we see that the saints, when they desired to obtain anything, based their hopes on sacrifices, because they knew that all prayers were ratified by sacrifice: "Remember all your offerings," says David, "and accept your burnt sacrifice" (Psalm 20:3). From this we infer that, in receiving the prayers of his people, God was from the very beginning appeased by the intercession of Christ. Why then does Christ speak of a new period ("at that day") when the disciples were to begin to pray in his name, unless it is that this grace, being now more brightly displayed, ought also to be held in higher esteem by us? In this sense he had said a little before, "Hitherto have you asked nothing in my name: ask." Not that they were wholly ignorant of the office of Mediator (all the Jews were instructed in these first rudiments), but they did not clearly understand that Christ, by his ascension to heaven, would be the advocate of the Church more than before. Therefore, to relieve their grief at his absence by some more than ordinary benefit, he asserts his office of advocate and says that until then they had been without the special benefit which it would be their privilege to enjoy when, aided by his intercession, they would call upon God with greater freedom. In this sense the Apostle says that we have "boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way, which he has consecrated for us" (Hebrews 10:19, 20). All the more inexcusable, therefore, are we if we do not embrace with both hands (as the saying goes) the priceless gift which is properly intended for us.

19¶

Moreover, since he himself is the only way and the only access by which we can draw near to God, those who stray from this way and refuse this access have no other left; his throne offers them nothing but wrath, judgment, and terror. In short, since the Father has consecrated him as our guide and head, those who abandon him or turn aside from him in any way are trying, as far as lies in them, to stain and erase the seal which God has stamped. Christ, therefore, is the only Mediator by whose intercession the Father is made favorable and willing to be entreated (1 Timothy 2:5). For though the saints are still permitted to use intercessions, by which they plead with God for one another's salvation, and which the Apostle mentions (Ephesians 6:18, 19; 1 Timothy 2:1), yet these depend on that one intercession, so far are they from detracting from it. For just as the intercessions which we offer up for one another, as members of one body, spring from the feeling of love, so they look to this one head. Since they are thus also made in the name of Christ, what more do they do than declare that no one can gain the least benefit from any prayers without the intercession of Christ? As there is nothing in the intercession of Christ to prevent the different members of the Church from offering up prayers for one another, so let it be held as a fixed principle that all the intercessions used in this way in the Church must look to that one intercession. Indeed, we must be especially careful to show our gratitude on this very account, that God, pardoning our unworthiness, not only allows each individual to pray for himself but allows all to intercede for one another. Since God has given a place in his Church to intercessors who would deserve to be rejected if they prayed privately on their own account, how presumptuous would it be to abuse this kindness by using it to obscure the honor of Christ?

20¶

Moreover, the Sophists are guilty of the merest trifling when they claim that Christ is the Mediator of redemption, but that believers are mediators of intercession; as if Christ had performed only a temporary mediation and left an eternal and imperishable mediation to his servants. Such, to be sure, is the treatment he receives from those who pretend to take from him only a tiny portion of honor. Very different is the language of Scripture, with whose simplicity every godly man will be satisfied, paying no regard to those impostors. For when John says, "If any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous" (1 John 2:1), does he mean merely that we once had an advocate? Does he not rather ascribe to him a perpetual intercession? What does Paul mean when he declares that he "is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us" (Romans 8:32)? And when in another passage he declares that he is the only Mediator between God and man (1 Timothy 2:5), is he not referring to the supplications he had mentioned a little before? Having first said that prayers were to be offered for all men, he immediately adds, in confirmation of that statement, that there is one God and one Mediator between God and man. Nor does Augustine give a different interpretation when he says: "Christian men commend one another in their prayers. But he for whom no one intercedes, while he himself intercedes for all, is the only true Mediator. Though the Apostle Paul was a principal member under the head, yet because he was a member of the body of Christ, and knew that the most true and High Priest of the Church had entered, not in figure into the inner veil of the holy of holies, but in firm and express truth into the inner sanctuary of heaven, to a holiness not imaginary but eternal, he too commends himself to the prayers of the faithful. He does not make himself a mediator between God and the people, but asks that all the members of the body of Christ should pray for one another, since the members share one another's feelings: if one member suffers, the others suffer with it. And so the mutual prayers of all the members still laboring on earth ascend to the Head, who has gone before into heaven, and in whom there is propitiation for our sins. For if Paul were a mediator, so would the other apostles be, and so there would be many mediators, and Paul's statement could not stand, 'There is one God, and one Mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus'; in whom we also are one if we keep the unity of the faith in the bond of peace." 21¶

As for the saints who, having died in the body, live in Christ, if we attribute prayer to them, let us not imagine that they have any other way of making supplication to God than through Christ, who alone is the way, or that their prayers are accepted by God in any other name. Therefore, since Scripture calls us away from all others to Christ alone, and since our heavenly Father is pleased to gather together all things in him, it would be the height of stupidity, not to say madness, to try to gain access through others, so as to be drawn away from him without whom access cannot be gained. But who can deny that this was the practice for several ages, and is still the practice wherever Popery prevails? To win the favor of God, human merits are thrust forward again and again, and very often, while Christ is passed by, God is petitioned in their name. I ask whether this is not to transfer to them that office of sole intercession which we have claimed above for Christ? Then what angel or devil ever announced one syllable to any human being about that imagined intercession of theirs? There is not a word on the subject in Scripture. What ground, then, was there for the invention? Certainly, when the human mind seeks for itself help that is not sanctioned by the word of God, it plainly displays its distrust (see section 27). But if we appeal to the consciences of all who take pleasure in the intercession of saints, we shall find that their only reason for it is that they are filled with anxiety, as if they supposed that Christ were insufficient or too severe. By this anxiety they dishonor Christ and rob him of his title of sole Mediator, a title which, having been given him by the Father as his special privilege, ought not to be transferred to any other. 27¶

On the whole, since Scripture places the chief part of worship in calling upon God (this being the duty of godliness which he requires of us in preference to all sacrifices), it is plain sacrilege to offer prayer to others. But we have already shown that if we consult the whole volume of Scripture, we shall find that God claims this honor for himself alone. As to the office of intercession, we have also seen that it belongs peculiarly to Christ, and that no prayer is pleasing to God which he, as Mediator, does not sanctify. And though believers offer up prayers to God on behalf of their brethren, we have shown that this in no way detracts from the sole intercession of Christ, because all rely on that intercession in commending themselves as well as others to God. Scripture often urges us to pray for one another, but says not one syllable about the dead; indeed, James implicitly excludes the dead when he joins the two things, to "confess our sins one to another, and to pray one for another" (James 5:16). 28¶

But though prayer is properly confined to vows and supplications, yet so close is the kinship between petition and thanksgiving that both may conveniently be included under one name. For the forms which Paul lists (1 Timothy 2:1) fall under the first member of this division. By prayer and supplication we pour out our desires before God, asking both for those things which serve to promote his glory and display his name, and for the benefits which contribute to our own good. By thanksgiving we rightly celebrate his kindnesses toward us, ascribing to his generosity every blessing that falls to our lot. David accordingly includes both in one sentence: "Call upon me in the day of trouble: I will deliver you, and you shall glorify me" (Psalm 50:15). Not without reason, Scripture commands us to use both continually. We have already described how great our need is, while experience itself proclaims that the straits pressing on us from every side are so many and so great that all have ample reason to send up sighs and groans to God without ceasing and to beg him humbly. For even if they should be free from adversity, the holiest ought still to be driven, first by their sins and secondly by the countless assaults of temptation, to long for a remedy. The sacrifice of praise and thanksgiving can never be interrupted without guilt, since God never ceases to heap favor upon favor on us, so as to compel us to gratitude, however slow and sluggish we may be. In short, so great and so widely spread are the riches of his generosity toward us, so marvelous and wonderful the miracles we behold on every side, that we can never lack a subject and material for praise and thanksgiving. To make this somewhat clearer: since all our hopes and resources are placed in God (this has already been fully proved), so that neither our persons nor our interests can prosper without his blessing, we must constantly submit ourselves and all we have to him. Then whatever we consider, say, or do should be considered, said, and done under his hand and will; in short, in the hope of his help. God has pronounced a curse on all who, trusting in themselves or in others, form plans and resolutions, who, without regard to his will and without calling on his help, either plan or attempt to carry out anything (James 4:14; Isaiah 30:1; 31:1). And since, as has already been observed, he receives the honor due to him when he is acknowledged to be the author of all good, it follows that, as we receive all good from his hand, we ought continually to express our thankfulness, and that we have no right to use the benefits which come from his generosity unless we diligently proclaim his praise and give him thanks, these being the ends for which they are given. When Paul declares that every creature of God "is sanctified by the word of God and prayers" (1 Timothy 4:5), he indicates that without the word and prayers none of them is holy and pure, word being used by metonymy for faith. Therefore David, on experiencing the loving-kindness of the Lord, beautifully declares, "He has put a new song in my mouth" (Psalm 40:3), indicating that our silence is malicious when we leave his blessings unpraised, since every blessing he bestows is a new ground for thanksgiving. So Isaiah, proclaiming the singular mercies of God, says, "Sing unto the Lord a new song" (Isaiah 42:10). In the same sense David says in another passage, "O Lord, open my lips; and my mouth shall show forth your praise" (Psalm 51:15). Likewise Hezekiah and Jonah declare that they will regard it as the purpose of their deliverance "to celebrate the goodness of God with songs in his temple" (Isaiah 38:20; Jonah 2:10). David lays down a general rule for all believers in these words: "What shall I render unto the Lord for all his benefits toward me? Again, "He will regard the prayer of the destitute, and not despise their prayer. This shall be written for the generation to come: and the people which shall be created shall praise the Lord." "To declare the name of the Lord in Zion, and his praise in Jerusalem" (Psalm 102:18, 21). Indeed, whenever believers beseech the Lord to do anything for his own name's sake, just as they declare themselves unworthy of obtaining it in their own name, so they bind themselves to give thanks and promise to make right use of his loving-kindness by being its heralds. So Hosea, speaking of the future redemption of the Church, says, "Take away all iniquity, and receive us graciously: so will we render the calves of our lips" (Hosea 14:2). Not only do our tongues proclaim the kindness of God, but that kindness naturally inspires us with love for him: "I love the Lord, because he has heard my voice and my supplications" (Psalm 116:1). The reason Paul commands, "Pray without ceasing. It is also plain that public prayers are not to be offered in Greek among the Latins, nor in Latin among the French or English (as has been the practice everywhere until now), but in the common language, so that all present may understand them, since they ought to serve for the edification of the whole Church, which cannot be benefited in the least by a sound it does not understand. Those who are not moved by any reason of humanity or love ought at least to be somewhat moved by the authority of Paul, whose words are by no means ambiguous: "When you shall bless with the spirit, how shall he that occupieth the room of the unlearned say, Amen, at your giving of thanks, seeing he understandeth not what you say? For you verily give thanks, but the other is not edified" (1 Corinthians 14:16, 17). How then can one sufficiently marvel at the unbridled license of the Papists, who, while the Apostle publicly protests against it, do not hesitate to bawl out the most wordy prayers in a foreign tongue, prayers of which they themselves sometimes do not understand one syllable, and which they have no wish for others to understand? French, "Qui est-ce donc qui se pourra assez esmerveiller d'une audace tant tent et brayent de langue estrange et inconnue, en laquelle le plus souvent ils n'entendent pas eux mesmes une syllabe, et ne veulent que les autres y entendent?" That is: Who then can sufficiently marvel at the unbridled audacity which the Papists have had, and still have, who, against the prohibition of the Apostle, chant and bray in a foreign and unknown tongue, in which for the most part they do not understand one syllable themselves, and which they have no wish for others to understand?

34¶

We must now consider not only a surer method but also a form of prayer, namely, the one which our heavenly Father has delivered to us through his beloved Son, and in which we may recognize his boundless goodness and condescension (Matthew 6:9; Luke 11:2). Besides warning and urging us to seek him in our every need (as children are accustomed to take refuge under the protection of their parents when weighed down by any anxiety), seeing that we were not fully aware how great our poverty was, or what was right or good for us to ask, he has provided for this ignorance; where our capacity failed, he has amply made up the lack. For he has given us a form in which everything that it is lawful to desire, everything that serves our good, and everything that it is necessary to ask is set before us as in a picture. From his goodness in this respect we gain the great comfort of knowing that, since we ask almost in his words, we ask nothing absurd, or foreign, or out of season; nothing, in short, that is not pleasing to him. Plato, seeing the ignorance of men in presenting their desires to God, desires which, if granted, would often be most harmful to them, declares the best form of prayer to be the one supplied by an ancient poet: "O king Jupiter, give us what is best, whether we wish it or not; but turn away from us what is evil, even though we ask for it" (Plato, Alcibiades 2). This pagan shows his wisdom in seeing how dangerous it is to ask of God what our own passion dictates; while at the same time he reminds us of our unhappy condition, in that we cannot open our lips before God without danger unless his Spirit teaches us how to pray rightly (Romans 8:26). All the higher value, therefore, ought we to set on this privilege, when the only begotten Son of God puts words into our mouths and so relieves our minds of all hesitation.

OUR FATHER WHICH ART IN HEAVEN.

36¶

The first thing brought to our notice at the very outset is, as we have already said (sections 17-19), that all our prayers to God ought to be presented only in the name of Christ, since there is no other name that can commend them. In calling God our Father, we certainly plead the name of Christ. For with what confidence could any man call God his Father? Who would have the presumption to claim for himself the honor of a son of God, if we were not freely adopted as his sons in Christ? He, being the true Son, has been given to us as a brother, so that what he possesses as his own by nature becomes ours by adoption, if we embrace this great mercy with firm faith. As John says, "As many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe in his name" (John 1:12). Therefore he both calls himself our Father and is pleased to be so called by us, freeing us from all distrust by this delightful name, since nowhere can a stronger affection be found than in a father. Hence, too, he could not have given us a stronger testimony of his boundless love than by calling us his sons. But his love toward us is as much greater and more excellent than that of earthly parents as he surpasses all men in goodness and mercy (Isaiah 63:16). Earthly parents, laying aside all fatherly affection, might abandon their children; he will never abandon us (Psalm 27:10), since he cannot deny himself. For we have his promise: "If you then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children, how much more shall your Father which is in heaven give good things to them that ask him?" (Matthew 7:11). Likewise in the prophet: "Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget you" (Isaiah 49:15). But if we are his sons, then, just as a son cannot take refuge under the protection of a stranger and a foreigner without at the same time complaining of his father's cruelty or poverty, so we cannot seek help from any other quarter than from him, unless we would reproach him with poverty, or lack of means, or cruelty and excessive harshness.

37¶

Nor let us claim that we are rightly made timid by a consciousness of sin, by which our Father, though gentle and merciful, is daily offended. For if among men a son can have no better advocate to plead his cause with his father, and can use no better intercessor to regain his lost favor, than if he comes himself, humble and downcast, acknowledging his fault, to beg the mercy of his father, whose fatherly feelings cannot help being moved by such pleas, what will that "Father of all mercies, and God of all comfort" do? (2 Corinthians 1:3). Will he not rather listen to the tears and groans of his children when they plead for themselves (especially since he invites and urges us to do so) than to any advocacy of others to whom the timid resort, not without some show of despair, because they distrust their father's gentleness and mercy? He sets before us the overflowing abundance of his fatherly kindness in the parable (Luke 15:20; see Calvin's Commentary), where the father receives with open arms the son who had gone away from him, wasted his substance in riotous living, and in every way sinned grievously against him. He does not wait until pardon is asked in words, but, anticipating the request, recognizes him far off, runs to meet him, comforts him, and restores him to favor. By setting before us this wonderful example of gentleness in a man, he meant to show how much more abundantly we may expect it from him who is not only a Father but the best and most merciful of all fathers, however ungrateful, rebellious, and wicked sons we may be, provided only that we throw ourselves on his mercy. And the better to assure us that he is such a Father if we are Christians, he has been pleased to be called not only a Father but our Father, as if we were pleading with him in this way: O Father, who have so much affection for your children and are so ready to forgive, we your children approach you and present our requests, fully persuaded that you have no other feelings toward us than those of a father, though we are unworthy of such a parent. French, "Quelque mauvaistié qu'ayons eië, ou quelque imperfection ou poureté qui soit en nous"; that is, whatever wickedness we may have done, or whatever imperfection or poverty there may be in us.

38¶

The instruction given us, however, is not that each individual is to call him his own Father, but rather that we are all in common to call him Our Father. By this we are reminded how strong the feeling of brotherly love among us ought to be, since we are all alike, by the same mercy and free kindness, the children of such a Father. For if he from whom we all receive whatever is good is our common Father (Matthew 23:9), we should be ready to share with one another everything that has been given to us, as far as occasion requires. But if we are as eager as we ought to be to stretch out our hands and help one another, there is nothing by which we can benefit our brethren more than by committing them to the care and protection of the best of parents, since if he is gracious and favorable, nothing more can be desired. And indeed we owe this to our Father as well. For just as he who truly and from the heart loves the father of a family extends the same love and goodwill to his whole household, so it befits us to extend the zeal and affection we feel for our heavenly Parent to his people, his family, and, in short, his inheritance, which he has honored so highly as to give them the name of the "fullness" of his only begotten Son (Ephesians 1:23). Let the Christian, then, so order his prayers as to make them common and to embrace all who are his brethren in Christ; not only those whom he now sees and knows to be such, but all men who are alive on the earth. What God has determined concerning them is beyond our knowledge, but to wish and hope the best for them is both godly and humane. Still, it befits us to regard with special affection those who are of the household of faith, and whom the Apostle has expressly commended to our care in everything (Galatians 6:10). In short, all our prayers ought to look to that community which our Lord has established in his kingdom and family.

39¶

This, however, does not prevent us from praying specially for ourselves and for certain others, provided our mind is not drawn away from the view of this community, does not stray from it, but constantly refers to it. For prayers, though framed in particular terms, do not cease to be common so long as they keep that object in view. All this may easily be understood by analogy. There is a general command from God to relieve the needs of all the poor, and yet this command is obeyed by those who, with that in view, give help to all whom they see or know to be in distress, even though they pass by many whose needs are no less urgent, either because they cannot know them all or are unable to supply them all. In the same way there is nothing contrary to the will of God in those who, while heeding this common fellowship of the Church, still offer particular prayers in which, with a public mind though in particular terms, they commend to God themselves or others whose needs he has been pleased to make more closely known to them. It is true that prayer and the giving of our goods are not alike in every respect. We can bestow the kindness of our generosity only on those whose needs we know, whereas in prayer we can help the greatest strangers, however great the distance that may separate them from us. This is done by that general form of prayer which, by including all the sons of God, includes them as well. To this we may refer the exhortation Paul gave to the believers of his time, to lift up "holy hands, without wrath and doubting" (1 Timothy 2:8). By reminding them that dissension is a barrier to prayer, he shows that it is his wish that they should present their prayers in common with one accord.

40¶

The next words are, WHICH ART IN HEAVEN. From this we are not to infer that he is enclosed and confined within the circle of heaven, as if by boundaries of some kind. Hence Solomon confesses, "The heaven of heavens cannot contain you" (1 Kings 8:27); and he himself says through the Prophet, "The heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool" (Isaiah 66:1), indicating by this that his presence is not confined to any region but is spread throughout all space. But since our dull minds are unable to conceive of his unspeakable glory, it is represented to us by heaven, since nothing our eyes can see is so full of splendor and majesty. While, then, we are accustomed to think of every object as confined to the place where our senses perceive it, no place can be assigned to God; and therefore, if we would seek him, we must rise higher than all bodily or mental perception. Again, this form of expression reminds us that he is far beyond the reach of change or corruption, that he holds the whole universe in his grasp and rules it by his power. The force of the expression, therefore, is the same as if it had been said that he is of infinite majesty, incomprehensible essence, boundless power, and eternal duration. When we speak of God in this way, our thoughts must be raised to their highest pitch; we must not ascribe to him anything earthly or carnal, must not measure him by our petty standards, or suppose that his will is like ours. At the same time, we must put our confidence in him, understanding that heaven and earth are governed by his providence and power. In short, under the name of Father is set before us that God who has appeared to us in his own image, so that we may call upon him with sure faith; the familiar name of Father being given not only to inspire confidence but also to restrain our minds and keep them from straying after doubtful or invented gods. We thus ascend from the only begotten Son to the supreme Father of angels and of the Church. Then, when his throne is set in heaven, we are reminded that he governs the world, and therefore that it is not in vain to approach him whose present care we actually experience. "He that comes to God," says the Apostle, "must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him" (Hebrews 11:6). Here Christ claims both things for his Father: first, that we place our faith in him; and, secondly, that we feel assured that our salvation is not neglected by him, since he condescends to extend his providence to us. By these elementary principles Paul prepares us to pray rightly; for before commanding us to make our requests known to God, he begins in this way: "The Lord is at hand. Be careful for nothing" (Philippians 4:5, 6). From this it appears that doubt and confusion hang over the prayers of those in whose minds the belief is not firmly settled that "the eyes of the Lord are upon the righteous" (Psalm 34:15).

41¶

The first petition is, HALLOWED BE THY NAME. The need to present it reveals our great disgrace. For what could be more shameful than that our ingratitude and malice should impair, and our audacity and insolence should, as far as lies in them, destroy, the glory of God? But even if all the ungodly should burst with sacrilegious rage, the holiness of God's name still shines forth. Rightly does the Psalmist exclaim, "According to your name, O God, so is your praise unto the ends of the earth" (Psalm 48:10). For wherever God has made himself known, his perfections must be displayed, his power, goodness, wisdom, justice, mercy, and truth, which fill us with wonder and stir us to proclaim his praise. Therefore, since the name of God is not rightly hallowed on earth, and we are otherwise unable to vindicate it, it is at least our duty to make it the subject of our prayers. The sum of the whole is this: it must be our desire that God may receive the honor which is his due, and that men may never think or speak of him without the deepest reverence. The opposite of this reverence is profanity, which has always been too common in the world and is very widespread in our own day. Hence the need for this petition, which would be superfluous if godliness had any real existence among us. But if the name of God is rightly hallowed only when, set apart from all other names, it alone is glorified, then in this petition we are commanded to ask not only that God would defend his sacred name from all contempt and insult, but also that he would compel the whole human race to revere it. Then, since God reveals himself to us partly by his word and partly by his works, he is not sanctified unless we ascribe to him what is due in regard to both, and so embrace whatever has come from him, giving no less praise to his justice than to his mercy. On the manifold variety of his works he has inscribed the marks of his glory, and these ought to draw forth from every tongue a tribute of praise. In this way Scripture will gain its due authority with us, and no event will keep us from celebrating the praises of God with regard to every part of his government. On the other hand, the petition expresses a wish that all impiety which pollutes this sacred name may perish and be extinguished, that everything which obscures or impairs his glory, all slander and insult, may cease, and that, all blasphemy being suppressed, the divine majesty may be displayed more and more clearly.

42¶

The second petition is, THY KINGDOM COME. This contains nothing new, and yet there is good reason for distinguishing it from the first. For if we consider our sluggishness in the greatest of all matters, we shall see how necessary it is that what ought in itself to be perfectly well known should be impressed on us at greater length. Therefore, after the command to pray that God would set in order, and at length wholly wipe out, every stain cast upon his sacred name, another petition containing almost the same wish is added, namely, Thy kingdom come. Although a definition of this kingdom has already been given, I now briefly repeat that God reigns when men, denying themselves and despising the world and this earthly life, devote themselves to righteousness and aspire to heaven (see Calvin, Harmony, Matthew 6). Thus this kingdom has two parts: the first is when God, by the working of his Spirit, corrects all the depraved lusts of the flesh, which war against him in troops; and the second, when he brings all our thoughts into obedience to his authority. This petition, therefore, is rightly presented only by those who begin with themselves; in other words, who pray that they may be cleansed from all the corruptions which disturb the peace and impair the purity of God's kingdom. Then, since the word of God is like his royal scepter, we are here commanded to pray that he would subdue all minds and hearts to willing obedience. This is done when, by the secret inspiration of his Spirit, he displays the power of his word and raises it to the place of honor it deserves. We must next come down to the wicked, who perversely and with desperate madness resist his authority. God, therefore, sets up his kingdom by humbling the whole world, though in different ways, taming the wantonness of some and breaking the ungovernable pride of others. We should desire this to be done every day, so that God may gather churches to himself from every part of the world, may extend and increase their numbers, enrich them with his gifts, and establish proper order among them; and, on the other hand, may beat down all the enemies of pure doctrine and religion, scatter their counsels, and defeat their attempts. From this it appears that there is good ground for the command which calls for daily progress, for human affairs are never so prosperous as when the impurities of vice are purged away and integrity flourishes in full vigor. The completion, however, is put off until the final coming of Christ, when, as Paul declares, "God will be all in all" (1 Corinthians 15:28). This prayer, therefore, ought first to draw us away from the corruptions of the world which separate us from God and keep his kingdom from flourishing within us; secondly, it ought to kindle in us an ardent desire for the mortification of the flesh; and lastly, it ought to train us to the endurance of the cross, since this is the way in which God would have his kingdom advanced. It ought not to grieve us that the outward man decays, provided the inner man is renewed. For such is the nature of the kingdom of God that, while we submit to his righteousness, he makes us partakers of his glory. This happens when, by continually adding to his light and truth, by which the lies and darkness of Satan and his kingdom are scattered, extinguished, and destroyed, he protects his people, guides them rightly by the working of his Spirit, and confirms them in perseverance; while, on the other hand, he frustrates the impious conspiracies of his enemies, scatters their tricks and frauds, forestalls their malice, and curbs their insolence, until at length he consumes Antichrist "with the spirit of his mouth," and destroys all impiety "with the brightness of his coming" (2 Thessalonians 2:8, Calvin's Commentary).

43¶

The third petition is, THY WILL BE DONE ON EARTH AS IT IS IN HEAVEN. Though this depends on his kingdom and cannot be separated from it, a separate place is still not wrongly given to it because of our ignorance, which does not readily or at once grasp what is meant by God reigning in the world. This, therefore, may not wrongly be taken as the explanation: that God will be King in the world when all submit themselves to his will. We are not here dealing with that secret will by which he governs all things and directs them to their end (see chapter 24, section 17). For although devils and men rise up in tumult against him, he is able by his incomprehensible counsel not only to turn aside their violence but to make it serve the carrying out of his decrees. What we speak of here is another will of God, namely, that to which willing obedience answers; and therefore heaven is expressly contrasted with earth, because, as is said in the Psalms, the angels "do his commandments, hearkening unto the voice of his word" (Psalm 103:20). We are therefore commanded to pray that, as everything done in heaven is done at the command of God, and the angels are calmly disposed to do all that is right, so the earth may be brought under his authority, all rebellion and depravity having been extinguished. In presenting this request we renounce the desires of the flesh, because he who does not wholly surrender his affections to God does all that lies in him to oppose the divine will, since everything that comes from us is sinful. Again, by this prayer we are taught to deny ourselves, so that God may rule us according to his pleasure; and not only so, but also that, having wiped out our own thoughts and minds, he may create new ones, so that we shall have no desire except that of complete agreement with his will; in short, that we may wish nothing of ourselves, but have our hearts governed by his Spirit, under whose inward teaching we may learn to love the things that please him and hate the things that displease him. Hence we must also desire that he would nullify and suppress all affections that are contrary to his will. Such are the first three heads of the prayer, in presenting which we should have the glory of God alone in view, taking no account of ourselves and paying no regard to our own advantage, which, though it is greatly furthered by them, is not here to be the subject of our request. And though all the things prayed for must come to pass in their own time, without being thought of, wished for, or asked by us, it is still our duty to wish and ask for them. And it is of no small importance to do so, so that we may testify and profess that we are the servants and children of God, eager by every means in our power to promote the honor due to him as our Lord and Father, and truly and wholly devoted to his service. Therefore, if men, in praying that the name of God may be hallowed, that his kingdom may come, and that his will may be done, are not moved by this zeal for the advancement of his glory, they are not to be counted among the servants and children of God; and since all these things will come to pass against their will, so they will turn out to their confusion and destruction.

44¶

Now comes the second part of the prayer, in which we come down to our own interests; not, indeed, that we are to lose sight of the glory of God (to which, as Paul declares, we must have regard even in eating and drinking, 1 Corinthians 10:31) and ask only what is good for ourselves; but the distinction, as we have already observed, is this: God, claiming the first three petitions as especially his own, carries us wholly to himself, so that in this way he may test our godliness. Next he permits us to look to our own advantage, but still on the condition that when we ask anything for ourselves it must be so that all the benefits he confers may display his glory, since nothing is more binding on us than to live and die to him. By the first petition of the second part, GIVE US THIS DAY OUR DAILY BREAD, we pray in general that God would give us all the things the body needs in this earthly state, not only food and clothing but everything which he knows will help us to eat our bread in peace. In this way we briefly cast our care upon him and commit ourselves to his providence, so that he may feed, nourish, and preserve us. For our heavenly Father does not disdain to take our body under his care and protection, so that he may exercise our faith in these small matters, as we look to him for everything, down to a morsel of bread and a drop of water. For since, owing to some strange imbalance, we feel more concern for the body than for the soul, many who can trust the soul to God still remain anxious about the body, still hesitate over what they are to eat and how they are to be clothed, and are in alarm whenever their hands are not filled with grain and wine and oil; so much more value do we set on this shadowy, fleeting life than on a blessed immortality. But those who, trusting in God, have once cast away that anxiety about the flesh immediately look to him for greater gifts, even salvation and eternal life. It is no slight exercise of faith, therefore, to hope in God for things which would otherwise cause us so much concern; nor have we made little progress when we get rid of this unbelief, which clings, as it were, to our very bones. The speculations of some about supersubstantial bread seem to agree very little with our Savior's meaning; for our prayer would be defective if we did not ascribe to God the nourishment even of this fading life. The reason they give is pagan, namely, that it is inconsistent with the character of the sons of God, who ought to be spiritual, not only to occupy their own minds with earthly cares but to suppose that God is occupied with them too. As if his blessing and fatherly favor were not strikingly displayed in giving us food, or as if there were nothing in the declaration that godliness has "the promise of the life that now is, and of that which is to come" (1 Timothy 4:8). But although the forgiveness of sins is of far greater importance than the nourishment of the body, Christ has set the lesser thing first, so that he might raise us step by step to the other two petitions, which properly belong to the heavenly life; in this he makes allowance for our sluggishness. We are commanded to ask for our bread, so that we may be content with the measure our heavenly Father is pleased to give, and may not strive to make gain by unlawful means. Meanwhile, we must hold that the title by which it is ours is gift, because, as Moses says (Leviticus 26:20; Deuteronomy 8:17), neither our industry nor our labor nor our hands gain anything for us unless the blessing of God is present; indeed, not even an abundance of bread would be of the least use if it were not divinely turned into nourishment. Hence this generosity of God is no less necessary to the rich than to the poor, because, though their cellars and barns were full, they would be parched and waste away with want if they did not enjoy his favor along with their bread. The words this day, or, as another Evangelist has it, daily, and also the adjective daily, put a check on our immoderate desire for fleeting goods, a desire which we are extremely prone to indulge to excess, and from which other evils follow; for when our supply is more abundant, we ambitiously squander it on pleasure, luxury, display, or other kinds of extravagance. Therefore we are commanded to ask only for as much as our need requires, and, as it were, for each day, trusting that our heavenly Father, who gives us today's supply, will not fail us tomorrow. However great our abundance may be, however well filled our cellars and granaries, we must still always ask for daily bread; for we must be fully assured that all our goods are nothing except so far as the Lord, by pouring out his blessing, makes them fruitful throughout their whole course; for even what is in our hand is not ours except so far as he portions it out hour by hour and permits us to use it. Since nothing is harder for human pride than to admit this truth, the Lord declares that he gave a special proof of it for all ages when he fed his people with manna in the desert (Deuteronomy 8:3), to remind us that "man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God" (Matthew 4:4). By this it is indicated that our life and strength are sustained by his power alone, though he supplies us through bodily means. Likewise, whenever he pleases, he gives us a proof of the opposite kind, by breaking the strength, or, as he himself calls it, the staff of bread (Leviticus 26:26), and leaving us to waste away with hunger even while we eat, and to be parched with thirst even while we drink. Those who, not content with daily bread, indulge an unrestrained and insatiable greed, or those who are full of their own abundance and trust in their own riches, only mock God by offering this prayer. For the former ask for what they would be unwilling to receive, indeed what they most of all detest, namely, daily bread only, and as far as lies in them they conceal their greed from God, whereas true prayer should pour out the whole soul and every inward feeling before him. The latter, again, ask for what they do not at all expect to receive, namely, what they imagine they already possess in themselves. In its being called ours, God, as we have already said, gives a striking display of his kindness, making that ours to which we have no just claim. Nor must we reject the view I have already mentioned, namely, that this name is given to what is gained by just and honest labor, as opposed to what is gained by fraud and plunder, since nothing is our own which we gain by injuring others. When we ask God to give us, the meaning is that the thing asked for is simply and freely the gift of God, whatever the quarter from which it comes to us, even when it seems to have been specially prepared by our own skill and industry and procured by our own hands, since it is to his blessing alone that all our labors owe their success.

45¶

The next petition is, FORGIVE US OUR DEBTS. In this and the following petition our Savior has briefly included whatever serves the heavenly life, since these two members contain the spiritual covenant which God made for the salvation of his Church: "I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it on their hearts." "I will pardon all their iniquities" (Jeremiah 31:33; 33:8). Here our Savior begins with the forgiveness of sins, and then adds the blessing that follows, namely, that God would protect us by the power and uphold us by the help of his Spirit, so that we may stand unconquered against all temptations. To sins he gives the name of debts, because we owe the punishment due to them, a debt which we could not possibly pay unless we were released by this remission, the fruit of his free mercy, when he freely cancels the debt, accepting nothing in return, but of his own mercy receiving satisfaction in Christ, who gave himself as a ransom for us (Romans 3:24). Therefore those who expect to satisfy God by merits of their own or of others, or to make up for and purchase forgiveness by means of satisfactions, have no share in this free pardon; and while they address God in this petition, they do nothing more than sign their own indictment and seal their condemnation by their own testimony. For they confess that they are debtors unless they are released by means of forgiveness. This forgiveness, however, they do not receive but rather reject, when they thrust their merits and satisfactions upon God, since by doing so they do not plead for his mercy but appeal to his justice. As for those, again, who dream of a perfection which makes it unnecessary to seek pardon, let them find their disciples among those whose itching ears incline them to deception. French, "Telles disciples qu'ils voudront"; that is, such disciples as they please.

47¶

These three petitions, in which we especially commend ourselves and all we have to God, clearly show what we observed earlier (sections 38, 39), that the prayers of Christians should be public and should look to the public edification of the Church and the advancement of believers in spiritual communion. For no one asks that anything be given to him as an individual, but we all ask in common for daily bread and the forgiveness of sins, not to be led into temptation, but to be delivered from evil. Moreover, the reason for our great boldness in asking and our confidence of receiving is added (sections 11, 36). Although this is not found in the Latin copies, yet since it fits so well with the whole, we cannot think of leaving it out. The words are, THINE IS THE KINGDOM, AND THE POWER, AND THE GLORY, FOR EVER. Here is the calm and firm assurance of our faith. For if our prayers had to be commended to God by our own worth, who would dare even to whisper before him? As it is, however wretched we may be, however unworthy, however lacking in anything to commend us, we shall never lack a reason for prayer or a ground of confidence, since the kingdom, the power, and the glory can never be wrested from our Father. The last word is AMEN, by which is expressed the eagerness of our desire to obtain the things we ask, while our hope is confirmed that all things have already been obtained and will certainly be granted to us, since they have been promised by God, who cannot deceive. This agrees with the form of expression we have already mentioned: "Grant, O Lord, for your name's sake, not on account of us or of our righteousness." By this the saints not only express the end of their prayers but confess that they would be unworthy of obtaining anything if God did not find the cause in himself, and if their confidence were not founded entirely on his nature.

48¶

All the things that we ought, indeed all that we are able, to ask of God are contained in this formula, and as it were rule, of prayer delivered by Christ, our divine Master, whom the Father has appointed to be our teacher and to whom alone he would have us listen (Matthew 17:5). For he always was the eternal wisdom of the Father, and, having been made man, was revealed as the Wonderful, the Counselor (Isaiah 11:2). Accordingly, this prayer is complete in all its parts, so complete that whatever is extraneous and foreign to it, whatever cannot be referred to it, is impious and unworthy of God's approval. For he has here summarily laid down what is worthy of him, what is acceptable to him, and what is necessary for us; in short, whatever he is pleased to grant. Those, therefore, who presume to go further and ask for something more from God, first seek to add something of their own to the wisdom of God (which it is insane blasphemy to do); secondly, refusing to confine themselves within the will of God and despising it, they wander wherever their greed leads them; and lastly, they will never obtain anything, since they pray without faith. For there can be no doubt that all such prayers are made without faith, because they are at odds with the word of God, on which faith must always lean if it is to stand at all. Those who disregard the Master's rule and indulge their own wishes not only lack the word of God but, as far as lies in them, oppose it. Hence Tertullian (On Flight in Persecution) has called it, no less truly than elegantly, Lawful Prayer, implicitly indicating that all other prayers are lawless and illicit.

49¶

By this, however, we would not have it understood that we are so bound to this form of prayer that it is unlawful to change a word or a syllable of it. For in Scripture we meet with many prayers differing greatly from it in wording, yet written by the same Spirit and able to be used by us with the greatest profit. Many prayers are also continually suggested to believers by the same Spirit, though in expression they bear no great resemblance to it. All we mean to say is that no one should wish, expect, or ask for anything which is not summarily included in this prayer. Though the words may be very different, there must be no difference in the sense. In this way all prayers, both those contained in Scripture and those that come from godly hearts, must be referred to it; certainly none can ever equal it, much less surpass it in perfection. It leaves out nothing we can conceive in praise of God, nothing we can imagine to be good for man, and the whole is so exact that all hope of improving it may well be given up. In short, let us remember that we have here the teaching of heavenly wisdom. God has taught what he willed; he willed what was necessary.

50¶

But although it has been said above (sections 7, 27, etc.) that we ought always to lift our minds upward toward God and pray without ceasing, yet such is our weakness, which needs to be supported, and such our sluggishness, which needs to be stirred up, that it is necessary for us to set apart special hours for this exercise, hours which are not to pass without prayer and during which all the affections of our minds are to be wholly engaged in it; namely, when we rise in the morning, before we begin our daily work, when we sit down to eat, when by the blessing of God we have eaten, and when we retire to rest. This, however, must not be a superstitious observance of hours, by which, as if discharging a task for God, we think ourselves released with regard to the other hours; it should rather be regarded as a discipline by which our weakness is exercised and stirred up again and again. In particular, it must be our earnest care, whenever we ourselves are hard pressed or see others hard pressed by any difficulty, to flee to him at once, not only with quickened steps but with quickened minds; and again, we must not, in any prosperity of our own or of others, fail to testify by praise and thanksgiving that we recognize his hand. Lastly, in all our prayers we must carefully avoid wanting to confine God to certain circumstances, or to prescribe to him the time, place, or manner of his action. Likewise, we are taught by this prayer not to lay down any law or impose any condition on him, but to leave it entirely to him to take whatever course seems best to him, with regard to method, time, and place. For before we offer any petition for ourselves, we ask that his will may be done, and by doing so we place our will under his, just as if we had put a bridle on it, so that, instead of presuming to lay down the law to God, it may regard him as the ruler and director of all its wishes.

51¶

If, with minds thus trained to obedience, we allow ourselves to be governed by the laws of divine providence, we shall easily learn to persevere in prayer and, holding back our own desires, to wait patiently for the Lord, certain, however little it may appear so, that he is always present with us and will in his own time show how very far he was from turning a deaf ear to prayers which, to the eyes of men, may seem to be ignored. This will be a very present comfort if at any time God does not give an immediate answer to our prayers, keeping us from fainting or giving way to despondency, as those are accustomed to do who, in calling upon God, are so carried away by their own fervor that unless he yields at their first insistence and gives immediate help, they at once imagine that he is angry and offended with them, and, abandoning all hope of success, stop praying. On the contrary, deferring our hope with well-balanced composure, let us press on with that perseverance which is so strongly commended to us in Scripture. We may often see in the Psalms how David and other believers, after they are almost weary of praying and seem to have been beating the air by addressing a God who would not hear, still do not cease to pray; because due authority is not given to the word of God unless the faith placed in it rises above all events. Again, let us not tempt God and provoke his anger against us by wearying him with our insistence. Many have a habit of bargaining with God on certain terms, and, as if he were the servant of their lust, binding him to certain conditions; and if he does not comply at once, they become indignant and peevish, grumble, complain, and make a commotion. Offended in this way, he often in his anger grants to such people what in mercy he kindly denies to others. We have a proof of this in the children of Israel, for whom it would have been better not to have been heard by the Lord than to swallow his indignation with their flesh (Numbers 11:18, 33).

52¶

But if our senses cannot, until after long waiting, perceive what the outcome of prayer is, or feel any benefit from it, our faith will still assure us of what cannot be perceived by the senses, namely, that we have obtained what was good for us, since the Lord has so often and so surely pledged himself to take up all our troubles from the moment they have been laid in his bosom. In this way we shall possess abundance in poverty and comfort in affliction. For though all things fail, God will never abandon us, and he cannot disappoint the expectation and patience of his people. He alone will suffice for all, since he contains all good in himself, and will at last reveal it to us on the day of judgment, when his kingdom shall be plainly displayed. We may add that, although God grants our request, he does not always answer in the very terms of our prayers, but, while apparently holding us in suspense, he still shows in an unknown way that our prayers have not been in vain. This is the meaning of the words of John: "If we know that he hear us, whatsoever we ask, we know that we have the petitions that we desired of him" (1 John 5:15). It might seem that there is a great excess of words here, but the declaration is most useful, namely, that God, even when he does not grant our requests, still listens and is favorable to our prayers, so that our hope founded on his word is never disappointed. But believers always need to be upheld by this patience, since they could not stand long if they did not lean on it. For the trials by which the Lord tests and exercises us are severe; indeed, he often drives us to extremes, and, when we are driven there, lets us stick fast in the mire for a long time before he gives us any taste of his sweetness. As Hannah says, "The Lord killeth, and maketh alive: he bringeth down to the grave, and bringeth up" (1 Samuel 2:6). What could they do here but lose heart and rush into despair, if they were not comforted, when afflicted, desolate, and half dead, by the thought that God has regard for them and that there will be an end to their present evils? But however secure their hopes may stand, they do not cease to pray in the meantime, since prayer not accompanied by perseverance leads to no result.
