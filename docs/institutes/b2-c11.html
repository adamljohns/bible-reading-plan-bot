The Difference Between the Two Testaments

This chapter consists principally of three parts. I. Five points of difference between the Old and the New Testament, sec. 1–11. II. The last of these points being, that the Old Testament belonged to the Jews only, whereas the New Testament belongs to all; the calling of the Gentiles is shortly considered, sec. 12. III. A reply to two objections usually taken to what is here taught concerning the difference between the Old and the New Testaments, sec. 13, 14.

Sections in this chapter

In the Old Testament the heavenly inheritance is exhibited under temporal blessings; in the New, aids of this description are not employed.
  2. 2. Proof of this first difference from the simile of an heir in pupillarity, as in Gal. 4:1 .
  3. 3. This the reason why the Patriarchs, under the Law, set a higher value on this life and the blessings of it, and dreaded the punishments, these being even more striking. Why severe and sudden punishments existed under the Law.
  4. 4. A second difference. The Old Testament typified Christ under ceremonies. The New exhibits the immediate truth and the whole body. The scope of the Epistle to the Hebrews in explaining this difference. Definition of the Old Testament.
  5. 5. Hence the Law our Schoolmaster to bring us unto Christ.
  6. 6. Notwithstanding, among those under the Law, some of the strongest examples of faith are exhibited, their equals being scarcely to be found in the Christian Church. The ordinary method of the divine dispensation to be here attended to. These excellent individuals placed under the Law, and aided by ceremonies, that they might behold and hail Christ afar off.
  7. 7. Third difference. The Old Testament is literal, the New spiritual. This difference considered first generally.
  8. 8. Next treated specially, on a careful examination of the Apostle’s text. A threefold antithesis. The Old Testament is literal, deadly, temporary. The New is spiritual, quickening, eternal. Difference between the letter and the spirit.
  9. 9. Fourth difference. The Old Testament belongs to bondage, the New to liberty. This confirmed by three passages of Scripture. Two objections answered.
  10. 10. Distinction between the three last differences and the first. Confirmation of the above from Augustine. Condition of the patriarchs under the Old Testament.
  11. 11. Fifth difference. The Old Testament belonged to one people only, the New to all.
  12. 12. The second part of the chapter depending on the preceding section. Of the calling of the Gentiles. Why the calling of the Gentiles scented to the Apostles so strange and new.
  13. 13. The last part of the chapter. Two objections considered. 1. God being immutable, cannot consistently disapprove what he once ordered. Answer confirmed by a passage of Scripture.
  14. 14. Objections. 2. God could at first have transacted with the Jews as he now does with Christians. Answer, showing the absurdity of this objection. Another answer founded on a just consideration of the divine will and the dispensation of grace.
1¶

What, then? you will say. Is there no difference between the Old and the New Testaments? What is to become of the many passages of Scripture in which they are contrasted as things differing most widely from each other? I readily admit the differences which Scripture points out, but I still hold that they take nothing away from the unity already established, as will be seen after we have considered them in order. These differences (so far as I have been able to observe them and can remember) seem to be chiefly four, or, if you choose to add a fifth, I have no objection. I hold, and think I shall be able to show, that they all belong to the mode of administration rather than to the substance. In this way there is nothing in them to prevent the promises of the Old and New Testaments from remaining the same, Christ being the foundation of both. The first difference, then, is this. Although in old times the Lord was pleased to direct the thoughts of his people and raise their minds to the heavenly inheritance, yet, so that their hope of it might be better sustained, he held it out to them, and in a manner gave them a foretaste of it, under earthly blessings. Now, however, the gift of the future life, more clearly and plainly revealed by the Gospel, leads our minds directly to meditate on it, and the lower mode of training formerly used with the Jews has been laid aside. Those who do not attend to the divine purpose in this respect suppose that God's ancient people rose no higher than the blessings promised to the body. They hear the land of Canaan so often named as the special, and as it were the only, reward of the divine Law for its worshipers; they hear that the severest punishment the Lord pronounces against transgressors of the Law is expulsion from the possession of that land and dispersion into other countries; they see that this makes up almost the sum of the blessings and curses declared by Moses. From these things they confidently conclude that the Jews were separated from other nations not for their own sake but for another reason, namely, so that the Christian Church might have an emblem in whose outward form evidence of spiritual things might be seen. But since Scripture sometimes shows that the earthly blessings so bestowed were intended by God himself to guide them to a heavenly hope, it shows great lack of skill, not to say dullness, not to attend to this mode of dispensation. The point of controversy is this: our opponents hold that the land of Canaan was regarded by the Israelites as their supreme and final happiness, and that now, since Christ has been manifested, it is a type to us of the heavenly inheritance. We, on the contrary, maintain that in the earthly possession which the Israelites enjoyed they beheld, as in a mirror, the future inheritance which they believed was reserved for them in heaven.

2¶

This will appear better from the comparison which Paul uses in Galatians (Galatians 4:1). He compares the Jewish nation to an heir who is still a minor and, not yet fit to govern himself, follows the direction of a tutor or guide to whose charge he has been committed. Although this comparison refers especially to ceremonies, nothing prevents us from applying it most fittingly here as well. The same inheritance was destined for them as for us, but because of their immaturity they were not able to enter on it and manage it. They had the same Church, though it was still in its childhood. The Lord, therefore, kept them under this guardianship, giving them spiritual promises not clear and simple, but foreshadowed by earthly things. Hence, when he chose Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and their posterity to the hope of immortality, he promised them the land of Canaan as an inheritance, not so that it might be the limit of their hopes, but so that the sight of it might train and strengthen them in the hope of that true inheritance which had not yet appeared. And to guard them against delusion, they received a better promise, which testified that this earth was not the highest measure of the divine kindness. Thus Abraham is not allowed to keep his thoughts down on the promised land; by a greater promise his view is carried upward to the Lord. He is addressed in this way: "Fear not, Abram: I am thy shield, and thy exceeding great reward" (Genesis 15:1). Here we see that the Lord is the final reward promised to Abraham, so that he might not seek a fleeting and vanishing reward in the elements of this world, but look to one that is incorruptible. A promise of the land is added afterward for no other reason than that it might be a symbol of the divine kindness and a type of the heavenly inheritance, as the saints declare they understood it. Thus David rises from temporal blessings to the last and highest of all: "My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever." "My heart and my flesh crieth out for the living God" (Psalm 73:26; 84:2). Again, "The Lord is the portion of mine inheritance and of my cup: thou maintainest my lot" (Psalm 16:5). Again, "I cried unto thee O Lord: I said Thou art my refuge and my portion in the land of the living" (Psalm 142:5). Those who dare to speak this way surely declare that their hope rises beyond the world and worldly blessings. The prophets, however, often describe this future blessedness under a type which the Lord had taught them. In this way we are to understand the many passages in Job (Job 18:17) and Isaiah to the effect that the righteous shall inherit the earth, that the wicked shall be driven out of it, that Jerusalem will abound in all kinds of riches, and that Zion will overflow with every kind of abundance. Strictly speaking, all these things plainly apply not to the land of our pilgrimage, nor to the earthly Jerusalem, but to the true country, the heavenly city of believers, in which the Lord has commanded blessing and life for evermore (Psalm 133:3).

3¶

This is why the saints under the Old Testament set a higher value on this mortal life and its blessings than would now be fitting. For although they knew well that in their race they were not to stop at it as the goal, yet, perceiving that the Lord, accommodating himself to their weakness, had imprinted there the features of his favor, it gave them greater delight than it could have done if considered only in itself. For just as the Lord, in testifying his good will toward believers by means of present blessings, then displayed spiritual happiness under types and emblems, so, on the other hand, by temporal punishments he gave proofs of his judgment against the reprobate. Hence the favor of the Lord was displayed, and his punishment inflicted, by earthly things. The unskilled, not considering this analogy and correspondence (if I may so call it) between rewards and punishments, wonder that there is so much inconsistency in God: that those whom in old times he suddenly visited for their faults with severe and dreadful punishments, he now punishes much more rarely and less severely, as if he had laid aside his former anger. For this reason they can hardly help imagining, like the Manichees, that the God of the Old Testament was different from the God of the New. But we shall easily free ourselves of such doubts if we attend to the mode of divine administration which I have mentioned: that God, at the time when he delivered his covenant to the Israelites as under a kind of veil, was pleased to indicate and foreshadow both the gift of future and eternal happiness by earthly blessings, and the dreadful nature of spiritual death by bodily punishments.

4¶

Another distinction between the Old and New Testaments lies in the types: the former displayed only the image of the truth while the reality was absent, the shadow instead of the substance; the latter displays both the full truth and the whole body. This is usually mentioned whenever the New Testament is contrasted with the Old. The French is, "et ˆ icelle se doivent reduire quasi tous les passages, auxquels le viel Testament est opposé au Nouveau par comparaison." That is, and to this ought in a manner to be referred all the passages in which the Old Testament is, by way of comparison, set against the New.

Here we may see in what respect the legal covenant is compared with the evangelical, and the ministry of Christ with that of Moses. If the comparison referred to the substance of the promises, there would be a great conflict between the two covenants; but since the nature of the case leads to a different view, we must follow it in order to discover the truth. Let us, therefore, bring forward the covenant which God once ratified as eternal and unending. Its fulfillment, by which it is fixed and ratified, is Christ. Until that fulfillment takes place, the Lord, through Moses, prescribes ceremonies which are, as it were, formal symbols of confirmation. The point under discussion was whether or not the ceremonies ordained in the Law had to give way to Christ. Although these were merely accidents of the covenant, or at least additions and appendages, and, as they are commonly called, accessories, yet because they were the means of administering it, the name of covenant is applied to them, just as is done with other sacraments. "Qualiter et aliis Sacramentis dari solet." French, "comme l'Escriture a coustume d'attribuer aux sacremens le nom des choses qu'ils representent"; that is, just as Scripture is accustomed to give sacraments the names of the things which they represent.

5¶

It is now clear in what sense the apostle said (Galatians 3:24; 4:1) that the Jews were led to Christ by the guardianship of the Law before he was displayed in the flesh. He admits that they were sons and heirs of God, though because of their immaturity they were placed under the care of a tutor. It was fitting, since the Sun of Righteousness had not yet risen, that there should be neither so much light of revelation nor such clear understanding. The Lord so dispensed the light of his word that they could see it only at a distance and obscurely. Accordingly, Paul calls this slender measure of understanding by the term childhood, which the Lord was pleased to train by the elements of this world and outward observances until Christ should appear. Through him the knowledge of believers was to be brought to maturity. Our Savior himself noted this distinction when he said that the Law and the Prophets were until John, and that from that time the gospel of the kingdom was preached (Matthew 11:13). What did the Law and the Prophets deliver to the men of their time? They gave a foretaste of that wisdom which was one day to be clearly manifested, and showed it from afar. But where Christ can be pointed to with the finger, there the kingdom of God is manifested. In him are contained all the treasures of wisdom and understanding, and by these we penetrate almost to the very sanctuary of heaven.

6¶

Nothing contrary to this follows from the fact that in the Christian Church scarcely one can be found who can be compared to Abraham in excellence of faith, and that the prophets were so distinguished by the power of the Spirit that even today they give light to the whole world. For the question here is not what grace the Lord conferred on a few, but what was the ordinary method he followed in teaching the people, a method used even with those very prophets who were endowed with special knowledge above others. For their preaching was both obscure, as relating to distant things, and wrapped in types. Moreover, however wonderful the knowledge displayed in them, since they were obliged to submit to the guardianship common to all the people, they too must be ranked among children. Lastly, none of them ever had such a degree of discernment as not to taste somewhat of the obscurity of the age. Hence the words of our Savior: "Many kings and prophets have desired to see the things which you see, and have not seen them, and to hear the things which ye hear, and have not heard them. Blessed are your eyes, for they see, and your ears, for they hear" (Matthew 13:17). And it was right that the presence of Christ should have this distinguishing mark, that by means of it the revelation of heavenly mysteries should be made clearer. To the same effect is the passage we quoted earlier from the First Epistle of Peter, that it was revealed to them that their labor would be useful not so much to themselves as to our age.

7¶

I proceed to the third distinction, which Jeremiah expresses this way: "Behold, the days come, saith the Lord, that I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah; not according to the covenant that I made with their fathers, in the day that I took them by the hand, to bring them out of the land of Egypt; (which my covenant they brake, although I was an husband unto them, saith the Lord); but this shall be the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel; After those days, saith the Lord, I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and will be their God, and they shall be my people. And they shall teach no more every man his neighbour, and every man his brother, saying, know the Lord: for they shall all know me, from the least of them unto the greatest of them" (Jeremiah 31:31-34). From these words the apostle took occasion to draw a comparison between the Law and the Gospel, calling the one a teaching of the letter and the other a teaching of the spirit; describing the one as engraved on tables of stone, the other on tables of the heart; the one the preaching of death, the other of life; the one of condemnation, the other of justification; the one done away, the other permanent (2 Corinthians 3:5, 6). Since the apostle's object is to explain the meaning of the prophet, the words of the one furnish us with the means of determining what both meant. And yet there is some difference between them. For the apostle speaks of the Law more disparagingly than the prophet does. He does this not simply with respect to the Law itself; rather, because there were certain false zealots of the Law who, by a perverse zeal for ceremonies, obscured the clearness of the Gospel, he treats the nature of the Law with reference to their error and foolish attachment. It will therefore be proper to note this peculiarity in Paul. Both, however, since they are contrasting the Old and New Testaments, consider nothing in the Law but what is peculiar to it. For example, the Law everywhere "Passim." French, "cá et lá"; that is, here and there.

8¶

Let us now explain the apostle's contrast step by step. The Old Testament is literal, because it was proclaimed without the working of the Spirit; the New is spiritual, because the Lord has engraved it on the heart. The second antithesis is a kind of explanation of the first. The Old is deadly, because it can do nothing but involve the whole human race in a curse; the New is the instrument of life, because it restores to favor with God those who are freed from the curse. The former is the ministry of condemnation, because it charges all the sons of Adam with transgression; the latter is the ministry of righteousness, because it unfolds the mercy of God by which we are justified. The last antithesis must be referred to the Ceremonial Law. Being a shadow of things to come, it had in time to perish and vanish away; whereas the Gospel, since it displays the very body, is firmly established forever. Jeremiah, indeed, calls the Moral Law also a weak and fragile covenant, but for another reason, namely, because it was immediately broken by the sudden defection of an ungrateful people. But since the blame for such violation lies in the people themselves, it is not properly charged against the covenant. The ceremonies, on the other hand, since through their very weakness they were dissolved by the coming of Christ, had the cause of their weakness within themselves. Moreover, the difference between the spirit and the letter must not be understood as if the Lord had delivered his Law to the Jews without any good result, that is, as if none had been converted to him. It is used by way of comparison, to commend the riches of the grace with which the same Lawgiver, assuming as it were a new character, honored the preaching of the Gospel. When we consider the multitude of those whom, by the preaching of the Gospel, he has regenerated by his Spirit and gathered out of all nations into the communion of his Church, we may say that those of ancient Israel who embraced the covenant of the Lord with sincere and heartfelt affection were few or none, though the number is great when they are considered in themselves, without comparison.

9¶

Out of the third distinction a fourth arises. In Scripture the term bondage is applied to the Old Testament because it produces fear, and the term freedom to the New, because it produces confidence and security. Thus Paul says to the Romans, "Ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption whereby we cry, Abba, Father" (Romans 8:15). To the same effect is the passage in Hebrews: "For ye are not come unto the mount that might be touched, and that burned with fire, nor unto blackness, and darkness, and tempest, and the sound of a trumpet, and the voice of words; which voice they that heard entreated that the word should not be spoken to them any more: (for they could not endure that which was commanded, And if so much as a beast touch the mountain, it shall be stoned, or thrust through with a dart: and so terrible was the sight, that Moses said, I exceedingly fear and quake); but ye are come unto mount Sion, and unto the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem," etc. (Hebrews 12:18-22). What Paul briefly touches on in the passage we have quoted from Romans, he explains more fully in the Epistle to the Galatians, where he makes an allegory of the two sons of Abraham in this way: "Agar is mount Sinai in Arabia, and answereth to Jerusalem which now is, and is in bondage with her children. But Jerusalem which is above is free, which is the mother of us all" (Galatians 4:25, 26). As the offspring of Hagar was born in slavery and could never attain to the inheritance, while that of Sarah was free and entitled to the inheritance, so by the Law we are made subject to slavery, and by the Gospel alone are we regenerated into liberty. The sum of the matter comes to this: the Old Testament filled the conscience with fear and trembling; the New fills it with gladness. By the former the conscience is held in bondage; by the latter it is set free and made free. If it is objected that the holy fathers among the Israelites, since they were endowed with the same spirit of faith, must also have shared in the same liberty and joy, we answer that neither was derived from the Law. Rather, feeling that by the Law they were oppressed like slaves and troubled with a restless conscience, they fled for refuge to the gospel. Accordingly, it was the peculiar advantage of the Gospel that, contrary to the common rule of the Old Testament, it exempted those who were under it from those evils. Further, we deny that they possessed the spirit of liberty and security in such a degree as not to experience some measure of fear and bondage. For however much they might enjoy the privilege they had obtained through the grace of the Gospel, they were under the same bonds and burdens of observances as the rest of their nation. Therefore, since they were bound to the anxious observance of ceremonies (which were the symbols of a guardianship bordering on slavery, and handwritings by which they acknowledged their guilt but did not escape from it), they are rightly said to have been, comparatively, under a covenant of fear and bondage, in respect of that common dispensation under which the Jewish people were then placed.

10¶

The three last contrasts we have mentioned (sections 4, 7, 9) are between the Law and the Gospel, and therefore in these the Law is called by the name of the Old Testament and the Gospel by that of the New. The first is of wider extent (section 1), including under it the promises which were given even before the Law. When Augustine maintained that these were not to be included under the name of the Old Testament (Augustine, To Boniface, book 3, chapter 14), he took a most correct view and meant nothing different from what we have now taught; for he had in view those passages of Jeremiah and Paul in which the Old Testament is distinguished from the word of grace and mercy. In the same passage Augustine very shrewdly remarks that from the beginning of the world the sons of promise, the divinely regenerated, who through faith working by love obeyed the commandments, belonged to the New Testament. They hoped not for carnal, earthly, and temporal blessings, but for spiritual, heavenly, and eternal ones, believing especially in a Mediator through whom they did not doubt both that the Spirit was given to them, enabling them to do good, and that pardon was granted as often as they sinned. What he thus intended to assert was that all the saints mentioned in Scripture from the beginning of the world as having been specially chosen by God were partakers with us, equally, of the blessing of eternal salvation. The only difference between our division and that of Augustine is this: ours (in keeping with the words of our Savior, "All the prophets and the law prophesied until John," Matthew 11:13) distinguishes between the gospel light and the more obscure dispensation of the word which preceded it, while his division simply distinguishes between the weakness of the Law and the strength of the Gospel. And here also it is to be observed, with regard to the holy fathers, that although they lived under the Old Testament, they did not stop there, but always aspired to the New, and so entered into sure fellowship with it. Those who, content with the shadows before them, did not carry their thoughts to Christ, the apostle charges with blindness and curse. To say nothing of other matters, what greater blindness can be imagined than to hope for the expiation of sin from the sacrifice of a beast, or to seek purification of the mind in outward washing with water, or to try to appease God with cold ceremonies, as if he were greatly delighted with them? Such are the absurdities into which those fall who cling to legal observances without regard to Christ.

11¶

The fifth distinction which we have to add consists in this: until the coming of Christ, the Lord set apart one nation, to which he confined the covenant of his grace. Moses says, "When the Most High divided to the nations their inheritance, when he separated the sons of Adam, he set the bounds of the people according to the number of the children of Israel. For the Lord's portion is his people; Jacob is the lot of his inheritance" (Deuteronomy 32:8, 9). In another passage he addresses the people this way: "Behold, the heaven and the heaven of heavens is the Lord's thy God, the earth also, with all that therein is. Only the Lord had a delight in thy fathers to love them, and he chose their seed, after them, even you, above all people, as it is this day" (Deuteronomy 10:14, 15). That people, therefore, as if they had been the only part of mankind belonging to him, he favored exclusively with the knowledge of his name, depositing his covenant, as it were, in their bosom, manifesting to them the presence of his divinity, and honoring them with every privilege. But to say nothing of other favors, the only one considered here is his binding them to himself by the communion of his word, so that he was called and regarded as their God. Meanwhile he allowed other nations, as if they had had no kind of dealings with him, to wander in vanity, not even supplying them with the only means of preventing their destruction, namely, the preaching of his word. Israel was thus the Lord's favorite child; the others were strangers. Israel was known and received into his trust and guardianship; the others were left in darkness. Israel was made holy; the others were profane. Israel was honored with the presence of God; the others were kept far away from him. But in the fullness of the time appointed for renewing all things, when the Mediator between God and man was manifested, the middle wall of partition, which had long kept the divine mercy within the borders of Israel, was broken down, and peace was preached to those who were far off as well as to those who were near, so that, being reconciled to God together, they might unite as one people. Therefore there is now no respect of Jew or Greek, of circumcision or uncircumcision, but Christ is all and in all. To him the heathen have been given for his inheritance, and the uttermost parts of the earth for his possession (Psalm 2:8), so that he may rule without distinction "from sea to sea, and from the river unto the ends of the earth" (Psalm 72:8).

12¶

The calling of the Gentiles, therefore, is a distinguishing mark that shows the superiority of the New Testament over the Old. This, it is true, had been declared earlier by the prophets in passages both numerous and clear, but its fulfillment was still deferred to the reign of the Messiah. Even Christ did not proclaim it at the very outset of his ministry, but delayed it until, having completed the whole work of redemption in all its parts and finished the time of his humiliation, he received from the Father "a name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow" (Philippians 2:9, 10). Hence, the time being not yet completed, he declared to the woman of Canaan, "I am not sent but unto the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matthew 15:24). Nor in his first commission to the apostles does he allow them to pass the same limits: "Go not into the way of the Gentiles, and into any city of the Samaritans enter ye not: but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matthew 10:5, 6). However plainly the thing may have been declared in numerous passages, when it was announced to the apostles it seemed to them so new and extraordinary that they recoiled from it in horror as something monstrous. When at last they did act on it, they did so timidly and not without reluctance. Nor is this strange, for it seemed by no means reasonable that the Lord, who for so many ages had set Israel apart from the rest of the nations, should suddenly, as it were, change his purpose and abandon his choice. Prophecy, indeed, had foretold it, but they could not be so attentive to prophecies as not to be somewhat startled by the new sight now presented to their eyes. It was not enough that God had in old times given samples of the future calling of the Gentiles. Those whom he had so called were very few in number, and moreover he in a manner adopted them into the family of Abraham before allowing them to approach his people. But by this public call the Gentiles were not only made equal to the Jews, but seemed to be put in their place, as if the Jews had been dead. "In demortuorum locum." The French is simply, "en leur lieu"; that is, into their place.

13¶

The whole difference between the Old and New Testaments has, I think, been fully and faithfully explained under these four or five heads, so far as is needed for ordinary instruction. But since this variety in governing the Church, this diversity in the manner of teaching, and this great change in rites and ceremonies is regarded by some as an absurdity, we must answer them before passing to other matters. And this can be done briefly, because the objections are not so strong "Firmæ." French, "Ne si fortes, ne si urgentes"; that is, neither so strong nor so pressing.

14¶

But it is said: Where does this diversity come from, except that God chose to make it? Would it not have been as easy for him from the first, as after the coming of Christ, to reveal eternal life in clear terms without any figures, to instruct his people by a few clear sacraments, to bestow his Holy Spirit, and to spread his grace over the whole globe? This is very much the same as bringing a charge against God because he created the world so late, when he could have done it at the first, or because he appointed the alternating changes of summer and winter, of day and night. With the feeling common to every godly mind, let us not doubt that everything God has done has been done wisely and justly, even though we may not know the cause which required that it be done so. We would claim too much for ourselves if we did not grant to God that he may have reasons for his counsel which we are unable to discern. It is strange, they say, that he now rejects and abhors the sacrifices of beasts and the whole apparatus of that Levitical priesthood in which he once delighted. As if those outward and passing things could delight God, or affect him in any way! "Aut ullo modo affciant." French, "ou comme si jamais il s'y fust arreté"; that is, or as if he could ever have stopped at them.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Having proved the covenants one in substance, Calvin now marks where they genuinely differ — the old held earthly types and shadows, spoke through ceremonies, addressed a single nation in its pupillage; the new exhibits the substance plainly, pours out the Spirit, and throws the doors open to all peoples. This balance is itself the discipline. We neither flatten the testaments into sameness nor sever them into contradiction, but discern, soberly, what changed and what abides. For the church in 2026, the lesson is humility before our own season of redemptive history: the calling of the Gentiles means we sit at a table once reserved, grafted in by sheer mercy, not native to the root. Let that breed no boasting against the older branches but a watchful gratitude. Teach the household to honor the shadows that prepared the way and to rejoice in the substance we have received — knowing that the God who matured his people from childhood to fullness is maturing his church still, and asks of us the obedience that befits those who have seen the light openly.
