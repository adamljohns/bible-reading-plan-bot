Book 3 · Chapter 13 · USMC modern English

Two Things to Be Observed in Gratuitous Justification

The divisions of this chapter are,—I. The glory of God, and peace of conscience, both secured by gratuitous justification. An insult to the glory of God to glory in ourselves and seek justification out of Christ, whose righteousness, apprehended by faith, is imputed to all the elect for reconciliation and eternal salvation, sec. 1, 2. II. Peace of conscience cannot be obtained in any other way than by gratuitous justification. This fully proved, sec. 3-5. 1. The glory of God remains untarnished, when he alone is acknowledged to be just. This proved from Scripture. 2. Those who glory in themselves glory against God. Objection. Answer, confirmed by the authority of Paul and Peter. 3. Peace of conscience obtained by free justification only. Testimony of Solomon, of conscience itself, and the Apostle Paul, who contends that faith is made vain if righteousness come by the law. 4. The promise confirmed by faith in the mercy of Christ. This is confirmed by Augustine and Bernard, is in accordance with what has been above stated, and is illustrated by clear predictions of the prophets. 5. Farther demonstration by an Apostle. Refutation of a sophism.

1¶

Here two ends must be kept especially in view: namely, that the glory of God be maintained undiminished, and that our consciences, in view of his tribunal, be secured in peaceful rest and calm tranquility. When the question concerns righteousness, we see how often and how earnestly Scripture urges us to give the whole praise of it to God. Accordingly, the Apostle testifies that the Lord's purpose in conferring righteousness on us in Christ was to demonstrate his own righteousness. He immediately adds what kind of demonstration this is, namely, "that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus" (Romans 3:25). Observe that the righteousness of God is not sufficiently displayed unless He alone is held to be righteous and freely communicates righteousness to the undeserving. For this reason it is his will that "every mouth may be stopped, and all the world may become guilty before God" (Romans 3:19). For so long as a man has anything, however small, to say in his own defense, so long he takes something away from the glory of God. So we are taught in Ezekiel how greatly we glorify his name by acknowledging our iniquity: "Then shall ye remember your ways and all your doings, wherein ye have been defiled; and ye shall loathe yourselves in your own sight, for all your evils that ye have committed. And ye shall know that I am the Lord, when I have wrought with you for my name's sake, not according to your wicked ways, nor according to your corrupt doings" (Ezekiel 20:43, 44). If part of the true knowledge of God consists in being weighed down by a consciousness of our own iniquity, and in recognizing him as one who does good to those who are unworthy of it, why do we try, to our great injury, to steal from the Lord even one particle of the praise of his unmerited kindness? In the same way, when Jeremiah exclaims, "Let not the wise man glory in his wisdom, neither let the mighty man glory in his might, let not the rich man glory in his riches: but let him that glorieth glory" in the Lord (Jeremiah 9:23, 24), does he not indicate that the glory of the Lord is infringed when man glories in himself? Paul, indeed, applies the words to this very purpose when he says that all the parts of our salvation are stored up with Christ, so that we may glory only in the Lord (1 Corinthians 1:29). For he indicates that whoever imagines he has anything of his own rebels against God and obscures his glory.

2¶

So indeed it is: we never truly glory in him until we have utterly cast off our own glory. It must therefore be regarded as a universal proposition that whoever glories in himself glories against God. Paul, indeed, holds that the whole world is not made subject to God until every ground of glorying has been taken away from men (Romans 3:19). Accordingly Isaiah, when he declares that "in the Lord shall all the seed of Israel be justified," adds, "and shall glory" (Isaiah 45:25), as if he had said that the elect are justified by the Lord in order that they may glory in him and in no one else. He had explained in the preceding verse how we are to glory in the Lord: "Unto me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear"; "Surely, shall one say, in the Lord have I righteousness and strength, even to him shall men come." Observe that what is required is not a simple confession, but confession confirmed by an oath, so that it might not be imagined that any kind of pretended humility would suffice. And let no one allege here that he does not glory when, without arrogance, he recognizes his own righteousness; such a recognition cannot take place without producing confidence, nor such confidence without giving birth to boasting. Let us remember, therefore, that in the whole discussion of justification the great thing to attend to is that God's glory be maintained whole and undiminished; since, as the Apostle declares, it was in demonstration of his own righteousness that he poured out his favor on us; it was "that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus" (Romans 3:26). Hence, in another passage, having said that the Lord conferred salvation on us in order to show forth the glory of his name (Ephesians 1:6), he afterward, as if repeating the same thing, adds, "By grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast" (Ephesians 2:8). And Peter, when he reminds us that we are called to the hope of salvation "that ye should show forth the praises of him who has called you out of darkness into his marvelous light" (1 Peter 2:9), no doubt intends to sound in the ears of believers only the praises of God, so that they may bury all arrogance of the flesh in deep silence. The sum is this: man cannot claim a single particle of righteousness for himself without at the same time taking away from the glory of the divine righteousness.

3¶

If we now ask in what way the conscience can be quieted in the sight of God, we shall find that the only way is by having righteousness bestowed on us freely by the gift of God. Let us always remember the words of Solomon: "Who can say I have made my heart clean, I am free from my sin?" (Proverbs 20:9). Undoubtedly there is not one man who is not covered with countless pollutions. Let the most perfect man go down into his own conscience and call his actions to account, and what will the result be? Will he feel calm and at rest, as if all matters were well settled between himself and God; or will he not rather be stung with dreadful torment, when he sees that the ground of condemnation is within him if he is judged by his works? Conscience, when it looks upon God, must either have sure peace with his justice or be besieged by the terrors of hell. We gain nothing, therefore, by discoursing about righteousness unless we hold it to be a righteousness stable enough to support our souls before the tribunal of God. When the soul is able to appear without fear in the presence of God and receive his sentence without dismay, then only let us know that we have found a righteousness that is not counterfeit. It is not without cause, therefore, that the Apostle insists on this matter. I prefer to give it in his words rather than my own: "If they which are of the law be heirs, faith is made void, and the promise made of no effect" (Romans 4:14). He first infers that faith is made void if the promise of righteousness has regard to the merit of our works or depends on the keeping of the law. No one could ever rest securely in it, for no one could ever feel fully assured that he had fully satisfied the law; and it is certain that no man ever fully satisfied it by works. Not to go far for proof of this, everyone who will use his eyes rightly may be his own witness. From this it appears how deep and dark the abyss is into which hypocrisy plunges the minds of men, when they indulge themselves so securely as to set their flattery, without hesitation, against the judgment of God, as if they were relieving him of his office as judge. Very different is the anxiety that fills the hearts of believers, who sincerely examine themselves. The two previous sentences are omitted in the French.

4¶

Paul, moreover, adds that the promise itself would be made null and void. For if its fulfillment depends on our merits, when, pray, will we ever get so far as to merit the favor of God? Indeed, the second clause follows from the first, since the promise will not be fulfilled except to those who put faith in it. If faith fails, therefore, no power will remain in the promise. "Therefore it is of faith, that it might be by grace, to the end the promise might be sure to all the seed" (Romans 4:16). It was abundantly confirmed when it was made to rest on the mercy of God alone, for mercy and truth are joined by an unbreakable bond; that is, whatever God has mercifully promised he faithfully performs. Thus David, before he asks for salvation according to the word of God, first places its source in his mercy: "Let, I pray thee, thy merciful kindness be for my comfort, according to thy word unto thy servant" (Psalm 119:76). And rightly so, for nothing but mere mercy moves God to promise. Here, then, we must place and, as it were, firmly fix our whole hope, paying no regard to our works and asking no help from them. And so that you do not suppose there is anything new in what I say, Augustine also tells us to act in this way. "Christ," he says, "will reign forever among his servants. This God has promised, God has spoken; if this is not enough, God has sworn. Therefore, since the promise stands firm not in respect of our merits but in respect of his mercy, no one ought to tremble in announcing that of which he cannot doubt" (Augustine on Psalm 88, Tractate 50). So Bernard also: "Who can be saved? ask the disciples of Christ. He replies, With men it is impossible, but not with God. This is our whole confidence, this our only consolation, this the whole ground of our hope. But being assured of the possibility, what are we to say about his willingness? Who knows whether he is deserving of love or hatred? (Ecclesiastes 9:1). 'Who has known the mind of the Lord that he may instruct him?' (1 Corinthians 2:16). Here it is plain that faith must come to our aid; here we must have the help of truth, so that the secret purpose of the Father concerning us may be revealed by the Spirit, and the Spirit, by his testimony, may persuade our hearts that we are the sons of God. But let him persuade us by calling and justifying freely by faith: in these there is a kind of passage from eternal predestination to future glory" (Bernard, On the Dedication of the Temple, Sermon 5). Let us conclude briefly, then: Scripture indicates that the promises of God are not sure unless they are grasped with full assurance of conscience; it declares that wherever there is doubt or uncertainty, the promises are made void; and, on the other hand, that they can only waver and fluctuate if they depend on our works. Therefore either our righteousness must perish, or, without any consideration of our works, place must be given to faith alone, whose nature it is to prick up the ear and shut the eye; that is, to be intent on the promise only and to give up all idea of any dignity or merit in man. Thus is fulfilled the celebrated prophecy of Zechariah: "I will remove the iniquity of that land in one day. In that day, saith the Lord of hosts, shall ye call every man his neighbor under the vine, and under the fig-tree" (Zechariah 3:9, 10). Here the prophet indicates that the only way in which believers can enjoy true peace is by obtaining the remission of their sins. For we must attend to this peculiarity in the prophets, that when they speak of the kingdom of Christ, they set forth the outward mercies of God as types of spiritual blessings. Hence Christ is called the Prince of Peace and our peace (Isaiah 9:6; Ephesians 2:14), because he calms all the agitations of conscience. If it is asked how, we must come to the sacrifice by which God was appeased; for no man will ever cease to tremble until he holds that God is propitiated solely by that expiation in which Christ endured his anger. In short, peace must be sought nowhere but in the agonies of Christ our Redeemer.

5¶

But why use a more obscure testimony? Paul consistently declares that the conscience can have no peace or quiet joy until it is held for certain that we are justified by faith. And at the same time he declares where this certainty comes from, namely, when "the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost" (Romans 5:5); as if he had said that our souls cannot have peace until we are fully assured that we are pleasing to God. Hence he exclaims elsewhere, in the person of believers in general, "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?" (Romans 8:35). Until we have reached that harbor, the slightest breeze will make us tremble; but so long as the Lord is our Shepherd, we shall walk without fear in the valley of the shadow of death (Psalm 23). Thus those who pretend that justification by faith consists in being regenerated and made just by living spiritually have never tasted the sweetness of grace in trusting that God will be favorable to them. Hence, too, they know no more about praying rightly than the Turks or any other heathen people. For, as Paul declares, faith is not true unless it suggests and dictates the delightful name of Father; indeed, unless it opens our mouths and enables us freely to cry, Abba, Father. He expresses this more clearly in another passage: "In whom we have boldness and access with confidence by the faith of him" (Ephesians 3:12). This, certainly, is not obtained by the gift of regeneration, which, since it is always defective in the present state, contains within it many grounds for doubt. Therefore we must resort to this remedy: we must hold that the only hope believers have of the heavenly inheritance is that, being grafted into the body of Christ, they are justified freely. For, with regard to justification, faith is merely passive, bringing nothing of our own to win the favor of God, but receiving from Christ everything that we lack.

Two things hang on free justification, Calvin teaches: the undivided glory of God, who alone is righteous and freely makes the undeserving righteous, and the settled peace of a conscience that can stand intrepid before his judgment seat. Claim a particle of righteousness for yourself and you steal that much glory from God and forfeit that much peace; whoever glories in himself glories against God. In 2026, when the church is pressed to prove her relevance and the believer to justify his own existence by output and reputation, this chapter calls us back to a quieter and sturdier ground. The family that learns to glory only in the Lord will not be shaken when the world withdraws its applause. Build the home on the firm rock of Christ's finished work, so that conscience, beholding God, finds not the terrors of hell but sure peace — and teach your children to cry Abba, Father, not from the merit of their walking, but from the mercy that will never let them go.
