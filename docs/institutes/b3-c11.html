2¶

So that we do not stumble at the very threshold (which we would do if we began the discussion without knowing what the subject is), let us first explain the meaning of the expressions, To be justified in the sight of God, and To be justified by faith or by works. A man is said to be justified in the sight of God when in the judgment of God he is deemed righteous, and is accepted on account of his righteousness; for as iniquity is abominable to God, so neither can the sinner find grace in his sight, so far as he is a sinner and so long as he is regarded as a sinner. Hence, wherever sin is, there also are the wrath and vengeance of God. He, on the other hand, is justified who is regarded not as a sinner but as righteous, and as such stands acquitted at the judgment-seat of God, where all sinners are condemned. As an innocent man, when charged before an impartial judge who decides according to his innocence, is said to be justified by the judge, so a man is said to be justified by God when, removed from the roll of sinners, he has God as the witness and defender of his righteousness. In the same way, a man will be said to be justified by works if in his life there can be found a purity and holiness that merits a testimony of righteousness at the throne of God, or if by the perfection of his works he can answer and satisfy the divine justice. On the contrary, a man will be justified by faith when, shut out from the righteousness of works, he lays hold by faith of the righteousness of Christ, and, clothed in it, appears in the sight of God not as a sinner but as righteous. Thus we simply interpret justification as the acceptance with which God receives us into his favor as if we were righteous; and we say that this justification consists in the forgiveness of sins and the imputation of the righteousness of Christ (see sections 21 and 23).

3¶

There are many clear passages of Scripture that confirm this. First, it cannot be denied that this is the proper and most usual meaning of the term. But since it would be too tedious to collect all the passages and compare them with one another, let it be enough to have called the reader's attention to the fact: he will easily convince himself of its truth. I will mention only a few passages in which the justification we are speaking of is expressly treated. First, when Luke relates that all the people who heard Christ "justified God" (Luke 7:29), and when Christ declares that "Wisdom is justified of all her children" (Luke 7:35), Luke does not mean that they conferred righteousness, which always dwells in perfection with God even if the whole world should try to wrest it from him; nor does Christ mean that the doctrine of salvation is made just, for this it is in its own nature. Rather, both expressions amount to giving due praise to God and to his doctrine. On the other hand, when Christ rebukes the Pharisees for justifying themselves (Luke 16:15), he does not mean that they acquired righteousness by acting rightly, but that they ambitiously courted a reputation for a righteousness they did not have. Those acquainted with Hebrew understand the meaning better: for in that language the name of wicked is given not only to those who are conscious of wickedness, but to those who receive a sentence of condemnation. Thus, when Bathsheba says, "I and my son Solomon shall be counted offenders," she is not confessing a crime, but complaining that she and her son will be exposed to the disgrace of being numbered among reprobates and criminals (1 Kings 1:21). 4¶

Without saying more about the term, we shall have no doubt about the thing meant if we attend to the description that is given of it. For Paul certainly describes justification by the term acceptance when he says to the Ephesians, "Having predestinated us unto the adoption of children by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will, to the praise of the glory of his grace, wherein he has made us accepted in the Beloved" (Ephesians 1:5, 6). His meaning is the very same as where he says elsewhere, "being justified freely by his grace" (Romans 3:24). In the fourth chapter of the Epistle to the Romans, he first calls it the imputation of righteousness, and does not hesitate to place it in the forgiveness of sins: "Even as David also describeth the blessedness of the man unto whom God imputeth righteousness without works, saying, Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven," etc. (Romans 4:6-8). There, indeed, he is speaking not of a part of justification, but of the whole. He declares, moreover, that a definition of it was given by David when he pronounced him blessed who has obtained the free pardon of his sins. From this it appears that the righteousness of which he speaks is simply set against judicial guilt. French, "Dont il appert qu'il note ces deux choses comme opposites, Estre justifies et Estre tenu coulpable; à ce que le proces soit fait à l'homme qui aura failli;" whence it appears that he sets down as opposites the two things, To be justified and To be held guilty, in that the process is brought against the man who has failed.

5¶

But since Osiander has introduced a kind of monstrosity called essential righteousness, by which, although he did not intend to abolish free righteousness, he wraps it in darkness, and by that darkness robs pious minds of a serious sense of divine grace French, "Que les poures ames ne sauroyent comprendre en telle obscurité la grace de Christ;" that poor souls cannot in such obscurity understand the grace of Christ.

French, "C'est, que l'ame est de l'essence de Dieu;" that is, that the soul is of the essence of God.

6¶

Had he said only that Christ, by justifying us, becomes ours by an essential union, and that he is our head not only insofar as he is man, but that the essence of the divine nature is poured into us, he might indulge his dreams with less harm, and perhaps there would be less need to contest the matter with him; but since this principle is like a cuttlefish, which, by squirting out dark and inky blood, conceals its many tails, French, "Mais comme le principe qu'il prend est comme une seche, laquelle en jettant son sang qui est noir comme encre, trougle l'eau d'alentour pour cacher une grande multitude de queuse;" But as the principle he adopts is like a cuttlefish, which, casting out its blood that is black as ink, troubles the water all around to hide a great multitude of tails.

7¶

When he objects that the power of justifying does not lie in faith considered in itself, but only as it receives Christ, I gladly admit it. For if faith justified of itself, or (as they put it) by its own intrinsic virtue, then, since it is always weak and imperfect, its efficacy would be partial, and so our righteousness, being maimed, would give us only a portion of salvation. We, indeed, imagine nothing of the kind, but say that, properly speaking, God alone justifies. We likewise ascribe the same thing to Christ, because he was given to us for righteousness; while we compare faith to a kind of vessel, because we are incapable of receiving Christ unless we are emptied and come with open mouth to receive his grace. It follows that we do not take the power of justifying away from Christ when we hold that, before his righteousness is received, he himself is received by faith. Still, I do not admit the sophist's twisted figure of speech, that faith is Christ; as if a vessel of clay were a treasure because gold is stored in it. French, "Quant à d'autres folies extravangantes d'Osiander, tout homme de sain jugement les rejettera; comme quand il dit que la foy est Jesus Christ, autant que s'il disoit, qu'un pot de terre est le thresor qui est caché dedans;" As to the other extravagant follies of Osiander, every man of sound judgment will reject them; for instance, when he says that faith is Jesus Christ, which is as much as if he said that an earthen pot is the treasure hidden in it.

8¶

Osiander goes still further regarding the manner of receiving Christ, holding that by the ministry of the external word the internal word is received; so that he may thus lead us away from the priesthood of Christ and his office of Mediator to his eternal divinity. French, "Faisant samblant de les rauir à la divinité d'icelui;" under pretense of leading them to his divinity.

9¶

If he objects that this work by its excellence transcends human nature, and can therefore be ascribed only to the divine nature, I grant the first point, but maintain that on the second he is ignorantly deceived. For although Christ could neither cleanse our souls by his own blood, nor appease the Father by his sacrifice, nor acquit us of the charge of guilt, nor, in short, perform the office of priest, unless he had been true God, because no human ability was equal to such a burden, it is nevertheless certain that he performed all these things in his human nature. If it is asked in what way we are justified, Paul answers: by the obedience of Christ. Did he obey in any other way than by taking the form of a servant? We infer, therefore, that righteousness was manifested to us in his flesh. In the same way, in another passage (which I greatly wonder that Osiander does not blush to quote again and again), he places the fountain of righteousness entirely in the incarnation of Christ: "He has made him to be sin for us who knew no sin, that we might be made the righteousness of God in him" (2 Corinthians 5:21). Osiander, in swollen sentences, seizes on the expression righteousness of God and shouts victory, as if he had proved it to be his own phantom of essential righteousness. French, "Il magnifie la justice de Dieu tant et plus; mais c'est pour triompher comme s'il auoit gagné ce poinct, que la justice de Dieu nous est essencielle;" He magnifies the righteousness of God beyond measure; but it is in order to triumph, as if he had gained this point, that the righteousness of God is essential to us.

The French adds "signifiant, que ceux desquels il parle ont nagé entre deux eaux; pource qu'ils aimoyent mieux garder leur bonne reputation au monde, qu d'etre priser devant Dieu;" meaning that those of whom he speaks were swimming between two streams, because they preferred keeping their good reputation in the world to being prized in the sight of God.

French, "Pour ceste cause j'ay accoustume de dire que Christ nous est comme une fontaine, dont chacun peut puiser et boire à son aise et à souhait; et que par son moyen les biens celestes sourdent et decoulent à nous, lesquels ne nous profiteroyent rien demeurans en la majesté de Dieu, qui est comme une source profonde;" For this reason I am accustomed to say that Christ is to us like a fountain, from which every man may draw and drink at his ease and to the full; and that through him heavenly blessings rise and flow to us, blessings which would profit us nothing if they remained in the majesty of God, which is, as it were, a deep abyss.

10¶

Moreover, so that he may not deceive the unwary by his cavils, I acknowledge that we are without this incomparable gift until Christ becomes ours. Therefore, to that union of the head and members, the dwelling of Christ in our hearts, in short, the mystical union, we assign the highest rank: Christ, when he becomes ours, making us partners with him in the gifts with which he was endowed. So we do not look on him as at a distance and outside us, but because we have put him on and been grafted into his body, he deigns to make us one with himself; and therefore we glory in having a fellowship of righteousness with him. This disposes of Osiander's slander that we regard faith as righteousness, as if we were robbing Christ of his rights when we say that, destitute in ourselves, we draw near to him by faith to make way for his grace, so that he alone may fill us. But Osiander, spurning this spiritual union, insists on a gross mixture of Christ with believers; and accordingly, to stir up prejudice, he gives the name of Zuinglians The Latin, "ideo Zuinglianos odiose nominat;" is in the French simply, "condamne furieusement;" furiously condemns.

Latin, "crassa mixtura;" French, "mixtion telle que les viandes qu nous mangeons;" a mixture such as the food we eat.

The French adds, "Osiander tire de la que Dieu a meslée son essence avec la nostre;" Osiander infers from this that God has mingled his essence with ours.

11¶

But more poison lurks in the second branch, when he says that we are righteous together with God. I think I have already sufficiently proved that, even if the dogma were not so pestilent, yet because it is cold and empty and falls by its own vanity, it must justly be distasteful to all sound and pious readers. But it is impossible to tolerate the impiety which, under the pretense of a twofold righteousness, undermines our assurance of salvation and, hurrying us into the clouds, tries to keep us from embracing the gift of expiation by faith and calling on God with quiet minds. Osiander derides us for teaching that to be justified is a forensic term, because, he says, we must be just in reality; and there is nothing he opposes more than the idea that we are justified by a free imputation. Tell me, then: if God does not justify us by acquitting and pardoning, what does Paul mean when he says, "God was in Christ reconciling the world unto himself, not imputing their trespasses unto them"? "He made him to be sin for us who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him" (2 Corinthians 5:19, 21). Here I learn, first, that those who are reconciled to God are regarded as righteous; then the method is stated: God justifies by pardoning. And so in another place justification is set against accusation (Romans 8:33), and this antithesis clearly shows that the expression is taken from the usage of the courts. And, indeed, no man moderately versed in the Hebrew tongue (provided he also has a sober brain) is ignorant that the phrase arose in this way and drew its tendency and force from it. Now, then, when Paul says that David "describeth the blessedness of the man unto whom God imputeth righteousness without works, saying, Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven" (Romans 4:6, 7; Psalm 32:1), let Osiander say whether this is a complete or only a partial definition. Paul certainly does not bring forward the Psalmist as a witness that pardon of sins is a part of righteousness, or that it combines with something else in justifying; rather, he includes the whole of righteousness in free forgiveness, declaring those to be blessed "whose iniquities are forgiven, and whose sins are covered," and "to whom the Lord will not impute sin." He measures and judges a man's happiness by this, that in this way he is righteous not in reality but by imputation.

Osiander objects that it would be an insult to God, and contrary to his nature, to justify those who still remain wicked. But it ought to be remembered, as I have already observed, that the gift of justification is not separated from regeneration, though the two things are distinct. But since experience shows all too well that the remains of sin always exist in the righteous, justification must be something very different from reformation to newness of life. This latter God begins in his elect and carries on through the whole course of life, gradually and sometimes slowly, so that if they were placed at his judgment-seat they would always deserve the sentence of death. He justifies not partially but freely, so that they can appear in the heavens as if clothed with the purity of Christ. No portion of righteousness could pacify the conscience. It must be settled that we are pleasing to God as being righteous in his sight without exception. It follows that the doctrine of justification is perverted and completely overthrown whenever doubt is instilled into the mind, confidence in salvation is shaken, and free and fearless prayer is hindered; indeed, whenever rest and tranquility with spiritual joy are not established. Hence Paul argues against objectors that "if the inheritance be of the law, it is no more of promise" (Galatians 3:18). that in this way faith would be made void; for if works are taken into account it fails, since in that case the holiest of men find nothing in which they can trust. This distinction between justification and regeneration (Osiander, confounding the two, calls them a twofold righteousness) is admirably expressed by Paul. Speaking of his real righteousness, or the integrity bestowed on him (which Osiander calls his essential righteousness), he mournfully exclaims, "O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death?" (Romans 7:24). But taking refuge again in the righteousness that is founded solely on the mercy of God, he breaks out magnificently into the language of triumph: "Who shall lay any thing to the charge of God's elect? It is God that justifieth." "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? shall tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword?" (Romans 8:33, 35). He clearly declares that the only righteousness for him is the one that alone suffices for complete salvation in the presence of God, so that the miserable bondage, the awareness of which made him lament his lot a little earlier, takes nothing from his confidence and is no obstacle in his way. This difference is well known, and indeed is familiar to all the saints who groan under the burden of sin and yet with victorious assurance rise above all fears. Osiander's objection, that this is inconsistent with the nature of God, falls back on himself; for though he clothes the saints with a twofold righteousness as with a coat of skins, he is nevertheless forced to admit that without forgiveness no man is pleasing to God. If this is so, let him at least admit that, with respect to what is called the proportion of imputation, those are regarded as righteous who are not so in reality. But how far shall the sinner extend this free acceptance, which is put in the place of righteousness? Will it amount to the whole pound, or only to an ounce? He will remain in doubt, swinging to this side and to that, because he will be unable to claim for himself as much righteousness as is needed to give confidence. It is well that he who would prescribe a law to God is not the judge in this cause. But this saying will always stand true: "That thou mightest be justified when thou speakest, and be clear when thou judgest" (Psalm 51:4). What arrogance it is to condemn the Supreme Judge when he acquits freely, and to try to keep this answer from taking effect: "I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy." And yet the intercession of Moses, which God quieted by this answer, was not for pardon to some individual, but to all alike, by wiping away the guilt to which all were liable. And we, indeed, say that the lost are justified before God by the burial of their sins; for (since he hates sin) he can love only those whom he justifies. But herein lies the wonderful method of justification: clothed with the righteousness of Christ, they do not dread the judgment of which they are worthy, and while they justly condemn themselves, they are yet deemed righteous outside themselves.

12¶

I must warn the reader to pay careful attention to the mystery which he boasts he is unwilling to hide from them. For after contending at great length that we do not obtain favor with God through the mere imputation of the righteousness of Christ, because (to use his own words) it would be impossible for God to hold as righteous those who are not so, he at last concludes that Christ was given to us for righteousness in respect not of his human but of his divine nature; and though this can be found only in the person of the Mediator, it is nevertheless the righteousness not of man but of God. He does not now twist his rope of two righteousnesses, but plainly strips the human nature of Christ of the office of justifying. It is worthwhile to understand what kind of argument he uses. It is said in the same passage that Christ is made unto us wisdom (1 Corinthians 1:30); but this is true only of the eternal Word, and therefore, he says, it is not the man Christ who is made righteousness. I answer that the only begotten Son of God was indeed his eternal wisdom, but that Paul applies this title to him in a different way, namely, because "in him are hid all the treasures of wisdom and righteousness" (Colossians 2:3). What he had with the Father, therefore, he manifested to us; and so Paul's expression refers not to the essence of the Son of God but to our use, and is fitly applied to the human nature of Christ. For although the light shone in darkness before he was clothed with flesh, he was yet a hidden light until he appeared in human nature as the Sun of Righteousness; and hence he calls himself the light of the world. Osiander also foolishly objects that justifying far surpasses the power both of men and of angels, since it depends not on the dignity of any creature but on the ordination of God. If angels were to attempt to give satisfaction to God, they could have no success, because they are not appointed for this purpose, it being the peculiar office of Christ, who "has redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13). Those who deny that Christ is our righteousness in respect of his divine nature are wickedly charged by Osiander with leaving only a part of Christ, and (what is worse) with making two Gods; because, while admitting that God dwells in us, they still insist that we are not justified by the righteousness of God. For though we call Christ the author of life inasmuch as he endured death so that he might destroy him who had the power of death (Hebrews 2:14), we do not thereby rob him of this honor in his whole character as God manifested in the flesh. We only make a distinction as to the manner in which the righteousness of God comes to us and is enjoyed by us, a matter on which Osiander shamefully erred. We do not deny that what was openly displayed to us in Christ flowed from the secret grace and power of God; nor do we dispute that the righteousness which Christ confers on us is the righteousness of God and proceeds from him. What we constantly maintain is that our righteousness and life are in the death and resurrection of Christ. I say nothing of that absurd heap of passages with which, without discrimination or common sense, he has burdened his readers in trying to show that whenever righteousness is mentioned, this essential righteousness of his is to be understood; as when David implores help from the righteousness of God. David does this more than a hundred times, and just as often Osiander does not hesitate to pervert his meaning. Not a whit more solid is his objection that the name of righteousness is rightly and properly given to that by which we are moved to act rightly, but that it is God alone who works in us both to will and to do (Philippians 2:13). For we do not deny that God by his Spirit forms us anew to holiness and righteousness of life; but we must first see whether he does this of himself, immediately, or by the hand of his Son, in whom he has deposited all the fullness of the Holy Spirit, so that out of his own abundance he may supply the needs of his members. When, although righteousness comes to us from the secret fountain of the Godhead, it does not follow that Christ, who sanctified himself in the flesh for our sake, is our righteousness in respect of his divine nature (John 17:19). No less frivolous is his remark that the righteousness by which Christ himself was righteous was divine, because had not the will of the Father moved him, he could not have fulfilled the office assigned to him. For although it has been said elsewhere that all the merits of Christ flow from the mere good pleasure of God, this lends no support to the phantom with which Osiander bewitches both his own eyes and those of the simple. For who will allow him to infer that, because God is the source and beginning of our righteousness, we are essentially righteous, and the essence of the divine righteousness dwells in us? In redeeming us, says Isaiah, "he (God) put on righteousness as a breastplate, and an helmet of salvation upon his head" (Isaiah 59:17). Was this to strip Christ of the armor which he had given him, and keep him from being a perfect Redeemer? All the Prophet meant was that God borrowed nothing from outside, that in redeeming us he received no outside help. Paul expresses the same thing briefly in different terms when he says that God set him forth "to declare his righteousness for the remission of sins." This is not in the least at odds with his teaching in another place, that "by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous" (Romans 5:19). In short, everyone who, by the entanglement of a twofold righteousness, keeps miserable souls from resting entirely on the mere mercy of God mocks Christ by putting on him a crown of plaited thorns.

13¶

But since a great part of mankind imagine a righteousness made up of faith and works, let us show here that there is so wide a difference between justification by faith and justification by works that establishing the one necessarily overthrows the other. The Apostle says, "Yea doubtless, and I count all things but loss for the excellency of the knowledge of Christ Jesus my Lord: for whom I have suffered the loss of all things, and do count them but dung, that I may win Christ, and be found in him, not having mine own righteousness, which is of the law, but that which is through the faith of Christ, the righteousness which is of God by faith" (Philippians 3:8, 9). You see here a comparison of contraries, and an indication that everyone who would obtain the righteousness of Christ must renounce his own. Hence he declares elsewhere that the cause of the rejection of the Jews was that "they being ignorant of God's righteousness, and going about to establish their own righteousness, have not submitted themselves unto the righteousness of God" (Romans 10:3). If we destroy the righteousness of God by establishing our own righteousness, then, in order to obtain his righteousness, our own must be entirely abandoned. He shows this also when he declares that boasting is excluded not by the Law but by faith (Romans 3:27). It follows that so long as the smallest portion of our own righteousness remains, we still have some ground for boasting. Now if faith utterly excludes boasting, the righteousness of works cannot in any way be joined with the righteousness of faith. This meaning is so clearly expressed in the fourth chapter of Romans as to leave no room for cavil or evasion. "If Abraham were justified by works he has whereof to glory"; and then it is added, "but not before God" (Romans 4:2). The conclusion, therefore, is that he was not justified by works. He then uses another argument from contraries, namely: when reward is paid to works, it is paid of debt, not of grace; but the righteousness of faith is of grace; therefore it is not of the merit of works. Away, then, with the dream of those who invent a righteousness made up of faith and works (see Calvin, Ad Concilium Tridentinum).

14¶

The Sophists, who delight in toying with Scripture and in empty cavils, think they have a subtle evasion when they explain works to mean those which unregenerate men do according to the letter and by the power of free will, without the grace of Christ, and deny that these have any reference to spiritual works. French, "Ainsi ils disent que cela n'appartient de rien aux bonnes œuvres des fideles qui se font par la vertu du Sainct Esprit;" Thus they say that this has no reference at all to the good works of believers, which are done by the power of the Holy Spirit.

15¶

The Schoolmen treat the matter somewhat more grossly by mixing their preparations into it; and yet the others instill into the simple and unwary a dogma no less pernicious when, under cover of the Spirit and grace, they hide the divine mercy, which alone can give peace to the trembling soul. We, indeed, hold with Paul that those who fulfill the Law are justified by God; but because we are all far from keeping the Law, we infer that the works which should be most effective for justification are of no use to us, because we do not have them. As for the common Papists or Schoolmen, they are doubly wrong here: both in calling faith an assurance of conscience in waiting to receive from God the reward of merits, and in interpreting divine grace to mean not the imputation of free righteousness but the help of the Spirit in the pursuit of holiness. They quote from an Apostle: "He that comes to God must believe that he is, and that he is the rewarder of them that diligently seek him" (Hebrews 11:6). But they do not observe what the manner of seeking is. Then, as to the term grace, it is plain from their writings that they labor under a delusion. For Lombard holds that justification is given to us by Christ in two ways. "First," he says (Lombard, Sentences, book 3, distinction 16, chapter 11), "the death of Christ justifies us when by means of it the love by which we are made righteous is stirred up in our hearts; and second, when by means of it sin is extinguished, the sin by which the devil held us captive, but by which he cannot now procure our condemnation." You see here that he considers the chief office of divine grace in our justification to be its directing us to good works through the agency of the Holy Spirit. He meant, no doubt, to follow the opinion of Augustine, but he follows it at a distance and even wanders far from a true imitation of him, both obscuring what Augustine stated clearly and making more corrupt what in him was less pure. The Schools have always gone from bad to worse, until at length, on their downward path, they have sunk into a kind of Pelagianism. Even the view of Augustine, or at least his way of expressing it, cannot be entirely approved. For although he is admirable in stripping man of all merit of righteousness and transferring the whole praise of it to God, he still classes the grace by which we are regenerated to newness of life under the head of sanctification.

16¶

Scripture, when it treats of justification by faith, leads us in a very different direction. Turning our eyes away from our own works, it bids us look only to the mercy of God and the perfection of Christ. The order of justification that it sets before us is this: first, God of his mere free goodness is pleased to embrace the sinner, in whom he sees nothing that can move him to mercy except wretchedness, because he sees him altogether naked and destitute of good works. He therefore seeks the cause of his kindness in himself, so that he may touch the sinner with a sense of his goodness and lead him, distrusting his own works, to cast himself entirely on his mercy for salvation. This is the meaning of faith, by which the sinner comes into possession of salvation, when, according to the doctrine of the Gospel, he perceives that he is reconciled by God; when, through the intercession of Christ, he obtains the pardon of his sins and is justified; and, though renewed by the Spirit of God, considers that instead of leaning on his own works he must look solely to the righteousness that is stored up for him in Christ. When these things are weighed one by one, they will clearly explain our view, though they might be arranged in a better order than the one in which they are presented here. But that is of little consequence, provided they are so connected with one another as to give us a full exposition and solid confirmation of the whole subject.

17¶

Here it is proper to remember the relation we established earlier between faith and the Gospel: faith is said to justify because it receives and embraces the righteousness offered in the Gospel. By the very fact that this righteousness is said to be offered by the Gospel, all consideration of works is excluded. Paul declares this repeatedly, and in two passages in particular demonstrates it most clearly. In the Epistle to the Romans, comparing the Law and the Gospel, he says, "Moses describeth the righteousness which is of the law, That the man which does those things shall live by them. But the righteousness which is of faith speaketh on this wise, If thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart that God has raised him from the dead, thou shalt be saved" (Romans 10:5, 6 :9). Do you see how he makes the distinction between the Law and the Gospel to be this, that the former gives justification to works, whereas the latter bestows it freely without any help from works? This is a notable passage, and it may free us from many difficulties if we understand that the justification given us by the Gospel is free from any terms of the Law. It is for this reason that he more than once sets the promise in direct opposition to the Law: "If the inheritance be of the law, it is no more of promise" (Galatians 3:18). Expressions of similar meaning occur in the same chapter. Undoubtedly the Law also has its promises; and therefore between them and the Gospel promises there must be some distinction and difference, unless we are to hold that the comparison is pointless. And in what can the difference consist, if not in this, that the promises of the Gospel are free and founded on the mere mercy of God, whereas the promises of the Law depend on the condition of works? But let no pest here allege that only the righteousness which men would thrust upon God by their own strength and free will is rejected; for Paul declares without exception that the Law gained nothing by its commands, since they are such as none can fulfill, not only of mankind in general, but not even the most perfect. Love assuredly is the chief commandment in the Law, and since the Spirit of God trains us to love, it cannot but be a cause of righteousness in us; yet that righteousness, even in the saints, is defective, and therefore of no value as a ground of merit.

18¶

The second passage is: "That no man is justified by the law in the sight of God, it is evident: for, The just shall live by faith. And the law is not of faith: but, The man that does them shall live in them" (Galatians 3:11, 12; Habakkuk 2:4). How could the argument hold unless it is true that works are not to be taken into account, but are to be altogether set apart? The Law, he says, is different from faith. Why? Because to obtain justification by it, works are required; and so it follows that to obtain justification by the Gospel they are not required. From this statement it appears that those who are justified by faith are justified independently of, indeed in the absence of, the merit of works, because faith receives the righteousness that the Gospel bestows. But the Gospel differs from the Law in this, that it does not tie justification to works, but places it entirely in the mercy of God. In the same way Paul contends in the Epistle to the Romans that Abraham had no ground for glorying, because faith was imputed to him for righteousness (Romans 4:2); and he adds in confirmation that the proper place for justification by faith is where there are no works to which reward is due: "To him that worketh is the reward not reckoned of grace, but of debt." What is given to faith is free; this is the force of the words he uses there. Shortly after he adds, "Therefore it is of faith, that it might be by grace" (Romans 4:16); and from this he infers that the inheritance is free because it is obtained by faith. How so, except that faith, without the aid of works, leans entirely on the mercy of God? And in the same sense, no doubt, he teaches elsewhere that the righteousness of God without the Law was manifested, being witnessed by the Law and the Prophets (Romans 3:21); for in excluding the Law he declares that it is not aided by works, that we do not obtain it by working, but are empty-handed when we draw near to receive it.

19¶

The reader now sees with what fairness the Sophists of the present day cavil at our doctrine when we say that a man is justified by faith alone (Romans 4:2). They dare not deny that he is justified by faith, since Scripture declares it so often; but because the word alone is nowhere expressly used, they will not tolerate its being added. French, "Mais pource que ce mot Seule, n'y est point exprimé, ils nous reprochent qu'il est adjousté du notre;" but because this word Alone is not expressed, they reproach us for having added it on our own.

20¶

Should anyone wonder why the Apostle, not content with having named works, uses this addition, the explanation is easy. However highly works may be valued, they have their whole worth more from the approval of God than from their own dignity. For who will presume to pride himself before God on the righteousness of works, except insofar as He approves of them? Who will presume to demand a reward of Him, except insofar as He has promised it? It is owing entirely to the goodness of God that works are counted worthy of the honor and reward of righteousness; and therefore their whole value consists in this, that by them we seek to show obedience to God. For this reason, in another passage, the Apostle, to prove that Abraham could not be justified by works, declares "that the covenant, that was confirmed before of God in Christ, the law, which was four hundred and thirty years after, cannot disannul, that it should make the promise of none effect" (Galatians 3:17). The unskilled would ridicule the argument, saying that there could be righteous works before the Law was given; but the Apostle, knowing that works could derive this value solely from the testimony and honor conferred on them by God, takes it for granted that before the Law they had no power of justifying. We see why he expressly calls them works of the Law when he would deny them the power of justifying, namely, because it was only with regard to such works that a question could be raised; although at times, without any addition, he excludes all kinds of works whatever, as when, on the testimony of David, he speaks of the man to whom the Lord imputeth righteousness without works (Romans 4:5, 6). No cavils, therefore, can enable them to prove that the exclusion of works is not general. In vain do they seize on the frivolous subtlety that the faith alone by which we are justified "worketh by love," and that love is therefore the foundation of justification. We indeed acknowledge with Paul that the only faith which justifies is that which works by love (Galatians 3:6); but love does not give it its justifying power. On the contrary, its only way of justifying consists in its bringing us into communion with the righteousness of Christ. Otherwise the whole argument on which the Apostle insists so earnestly would fall: "To him that worketh is the reward not reckoned of grace, but of debt. But to him that worketh not, but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness." Could he express more clearly than in this word that there is justification in faith only where there are no works to which reward is due, and that faith is imputed for righteousness only when righteousness is conferred freely, without merit?

21¶

Let us now consider the truth of what was said in the definition, namely, that justification by faith is reconciliation with God, and that this consists solely in the remission of sins. We must always return to the axiom that the wrath of God lies on all men so long as they continue sinners. Isaiah expresses this elegantly in these words: "Behold, the Lord's hand is not shortened, that it cannot save; neither his ear heavy, that it cannot hear: but your iniquities have separated between you and your God, and your sins have hid his face from you, that he will not hear" (Isaiah 59:1, 2). We are told here that sin is a separation between God and man; that His face is turned away from the sinner; and that it cannot be otherwise, since to have any dealings with sin is contrary to his righteousness. Hence the Apostle shows that man is at enmity with God until he is restored to favor by Christ (Romans 5:8-10). When the Lord, therefore, admits him to union, he is said to justify him, because he can neither receive him into favor nor unite him to himself without changing his condition from that of a sinner into that of a righteous man. We add that this is done by the remission of sins. For if those whom the Lord has reconciled to himself are judged by their works, they will still prove to be sinners in reality, while they ought to be pure and free from sin. It is evident, therefore, that the only way in which those whom God embraces are made righteous is by having their defilements wiped away through the remission of sins, so that this justification may be called, in one word, the remission of sins.

22¶

Both of these become perfectly clear from the words of Paul: "God was in Christ reconciling the world unto himself, not imputing their trespasses unto them; and has committed unto us the word of reconciliation." He then adds the sum of his embassy: "He has made him to be sin for us who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him" (2 Corinthians 5:19-21). He uses righteousness and reconciliation interchangeably here, to make us understand that the one includes the other. The way of obtaining this righteousness, he explains, is that our sins are not imputed to us. Therefore you cannot doubt from now on how God justifies us, when you hear that he reconciles us to himself by not imputing our faults. In the same way, in the Epistle to the Romans, he proves by the testimony of David that righteousness is imputed without works, because David declares the man to be blessed "whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered," and "unto whom the Lord imputeth not iniquity" (Romans 4:6; Psalm 32:1, 2). There he undoubtedly uses blessedness for righteousness; and since he declares that it consists in the forgiveness of sins, there is no reason why we should define it otherwise. Accordingly, Zacharias, the father of John the Baptist, sings that the knowledge of salvation consists in the forgiveness of sins (Luke 1:77). Paul followed the same course when, addressing the people of Antioch, he gave them a summary of salvation. Luke states that he concluded in this way: "Through this man is preached unto you the forgiveness of sins, and by him all that believe are justified from all things from which ye could not be justified by the law of Moses" (Acts 13:38, 39). Thus the Apostle joins forgiveness of sins with justification in such a way as to show that they are altogether the same; and hence he rightly argues that justification, which we owe to the indulgence of God, is free. Nor should it seem an unusual way of speaking to say that believers are justified before God not by works but by free acceptance, since it is often used in Scripture, and sometimes also by ancient writers. Thus Augustine says: "The righteousness of the saints in this world consists more in the forgiveness of sins than in the perfection of virtue" (Augustine, De Civitate Dei, book 19, chapter 27). To this corresponds the well-known saying of Bernard: "Not to sin is the righteousness of God, but the righteousness of man is the indulgence of God" (Bernard, Sermons 22, 23 on the Song of Songs). He asserts earlier that Christ is our righteousness in absolution, and therefore that those only are just who have obtained pardon through mercy.

23¶

Hence it is also proved that it is entirely by the intervention of Christ's righteousness that we obtain justification before God. This is the same as saying that man is not just in himself, but that the righteousness of Christ is communicated to him by imputation, while he himself strictly deserves punishment. Thus vanishes the absurd dogma that man is justified by faith inasmuch as it brings him under the influence of the Spirit of God, by whom he is made righteous. This is so contrary to the doctrine above that it can never be reconciled with it. There can be no doubt that he who is taught to seek righteousness outside himself does not already possess it in himself. French, "Ceci est fort contraire a la doctrine ci dessus mise: car il n'y a nulle doute que celui qui doit cercher justice hors de soy-mesme, ne soit desnué de la sienne propre;" This is quite contrary to the doctrine laid down above; for there is no doubt that he who is to seek righteousness outside himself is devoid of righteousness in himself.

French, "Sous la robbe;" under the robe.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin's burden here is the great hinge of the gospel: to be justified is to be accounted righteous before God's tribunal not by any worth in us but by the imputed righteousness of Christ, laid hold of by faith — a forensic acquittal, sins forgiven and his obedience reckoned as ours, while we ourselves remain sinners freely pardoned. Guard this distinction, household by household, in 2026. The Christian who confuses his slow sanctification with his standing before God will live anxious or proud by turns; teach your children, at the supper table and at the bedside, that they come to the Father with open hands, emptied of their own record, clothed in another's. In an age that worships self-improvement and measures worth by performance, let the home be the place where a weary conscience hears that God justifies the ungodly. Rest is not won; it is received — and received righteousness is the only kind that will stand.
