Christ, Though Known to the Jews Under the Law, Yet Only Manifested Under the Gospel

There are three principal heads in this chapter. I. Preparatory to a consideration of the knowledge of Christ, and the benefits procured by him; the 1st and 2nd sections are occupied with the dispensation of this knowledge, which, after the manifestation of Christ in the flesh, was more clearly revealed than under the Law. II. A refutation of the profane dream of Servetus, that the promises are entirely abrogated, sec. 3. Likewise, a refutation of those who do not properly compare the Law with the Gospel, sec. 4. III. A necessary and brief exposition of the ministry of John Baptist, which occupies an intermediate place between the law and the Gospel.

Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. The holy fathers under the Law saw the day of Christ, though obscurely. He is more fully revealed to us under the Gospel. A reason for this, confirmed by the testimony of Christ and his Apostles.
  2. 2. The term Gospel, used in its most extensive sense, comprehends the attestations of mercy which God gave to the fathers. Properly, however, it means the promulgation of grace exhibited in the God-man Jesus Christ.
  3. 3. The notion of Servetus, that the promises are entirely abolished, refuted. Why we must still trust to the promises of God. Another reason. Solution of a difficulty.
  4. 4. Refutation of those who do not properly compare the Law and the Gospel. Answer to certain questions here occurring. The Law and the Gospel briefly compared.
  5. 5. Third part of the chapter. Of the ministry of John the Baptist.
1¶

Since God was pleased (and not in vain) to testify in ancient times, by means of expiations and sacrifices, that he was a Father, and to set apart a chosen people for himself, he was doubtless known even then in the same character in which he is now fully revealed to us. Accordingly Malachi, having commanded the Jews to attend to the Law of Moses (because after his death there was to be an interruption of the prophetic office), immediately declares that the Sun of righteousness would arise (Malachi 4:2). In this way he indicates that, although the Law had the effect of keeping the godly in expectation of the coming Messiah, there was ground to hope for much greater light at his coming. For this reason Peter, speaking of the ancient prophets, says, "Unto whom it was revealed, that not unto themselves, but unto us, they did minister the things which are now reported unto you by them that have preached the gospel unto you, with the Holy Ghost sent down from heaven" (1 Peter 1:12). Not that the prophetic teaching was useless to the ancient people, or of no benefit to the prophets themselves, but that they did not obtain possession of the treasure which God has handed down to us by their hands. The grace of which they testified is now set plainly before our eyes. They had only a slight foretaste; to us a fuller enjoyment is given. Accordingly our Savior, while he declares that Moses testified of him, extols the greater measure of grace bestowed on us (John 5:46). Addressing his disciples, he says, "Blessed are your eyes, for they see, and your ears, for they hear. For verily I say unto you, That many prophets and righteous men have desired to see those things which ye see, and have not seen them, and to hear those things which ye hear, and have not heard them" (Matthew 13:16; Luke 10:23). It is no small commendation of the gospel revelation that God has preferred us to holy men of old, so greatly distinguished for godliness. There is nothing in this view inconsistent with another passage, in which our Savior says, "Your father Abraham rejoiced to see my day, and he saw it and was glad" (John 8:56). For although, the event being remote, his view of it was obscure, he had full assurance that it would one day be accomplished; and hence the joy which the holy patriarch felt even to his death. Nor does John the Baptist, when he says, "No man has seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he has declared him" (John 1:18), exclude the godly who had died before from a share in the knowledge and light which are manifested in the person of Christ. Rather, comparing their condition with ours, he indicates that the mysteries which they beheld only dimly under shadows are made clear to us. This is well explained by the author of the Epistle to the Hebrews in these words: "God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, has in these last days spoken unto us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1, 2). Hence, although this only begotten Son, who is now to us the brightness of his Father's glory and the express image of his person, was formerly made known to the Jews (as we have elsewhere shown from Paul that he was the Deliverer under the old dispensation), it is nevertheless true, as Paul himself declares elsewhere, that "God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, has shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6). For when he appeared in this his image, he in a manner made himself visible, whereas his previous appearance had been shadowy and obscure. All the more shameful and detestable, therefore, is the ingratitude of those who walk blindfolded in this noonday light. Accordingly Paul says that "the god of this world has blinded their minds, lest the light of the glorious gospel of Christ should shine unto them" (2 Corinthians 4:4).

2¶

By the Gospel I understand the clear manifestation of the mystery of Christ. I admit, indeed, that since the term Gospel is applied by Paul to the doctrine of faith (2 Timothy 4:10), it includes all the promises by which God reconciles men to himself, and which occur throughout the Law. For Paul there sets faith against those terrors which vex and torment the conscience when salvation is sought by means of works. Hence it follows that Gospel, taken in a broad sense, includes the evidences of mercy and fatherly favor which God bestowed on the patriarchs. Still, in its preeminent sense, it is applied to the proclamation of the grace manifested in Christ. This rests not only on common usage, but has the sanction of our Savior and his apostles. Hence it is described as one of his distinctive marks that he preached the Gospel of the kingdom (Matthew 4:23; 9:35; Mark 1:14). Mark, in the preface to his Gospel, calls it "The beginning of the Gospel of Jesus Christ." There is no point in collecting passages to prove what is already perfectly well known. Christ at his coming "brought life and immortality to light through the Gospel" (2 Timothy 1:10). By these words Paul does not mean that the fathers were plunged in the darkness of death before the Son of God became incarnate. Rather, he claims for the Gospel the honorable distinction of being a new and extraordinary kind of embassy, by which God fulfilled what he had promised, these promises being realized in the person of the Son. For although believers at all times experienced the truth of Paul's declaration that "all the promises of God in him are yea and amen," inasmuch as these promises were sealed on their hearts, yet because he has in his flesh completed all the parts of our salvation, this vivid manifestation of the realities themselves was rightly given this new and special distinction. Accordingly Christ says, "Hereafter ye shall see heaven open, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of man." For although he seems to allude to the ladder which the patriarch Jacob saw in a vision, he commends the excellence of his coming in this: that it opened the gate of heaven and gave us familiar access to it.

3¶

Here we must guard against the diabolical imagination of Servetus, who, from a wish, or at least the pretense of a wish, to extol the greatness of Christ, abolishes the promises entirely, as if they had come to an end at the same time as the Law. He pretends that by the faith of the Gospel all the promises have been fulfilled, as if there were no distinction between us and Christ. I observed a moment ago that Christ has not left any part of our salvation incomplete; but from this it is wrongly inferred that we are now put in possession of all the blessings he purchased. That would imply that Paul was mistaken in saying, "We are saved by hope" (Romans 3:24). I admit, indeed, that by believing in Christ we pass from death to life; but at the same time we must remember the words of John, that although we know we are "the sons of God," "it does not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2). Therefore, although Christ offers us in the Gospel a present fullness of spiritual blessings, the enjoyment of them remains in the keeping of hope, "Sub custodia spei." French, "sous la garde, et comme sous le cachet d'espoir"; under the guard and, as it were, under the seal of hope.

4¶

Hence, also, we see the error of those who, in comparing the Law with the Gospel, represent it merely as a comparison between the merit of works and the free imputation of righteousness. The contrast so drawn is by no means to be rejected, because by the term Law Paul frequently understands that rule of holy living in which God demands what is his due, giving no hope of life unless we obey in every respect, and, on the other hand, pronouncing a curse on the slightest failure. Paul does this when showing that we are freely accepted by God and accounted righteous by being pardoned, because that obedience of the Law to which the reward is promised is nowhere to be found. Hence he fittingly represents the righteousness of the Law and that of the Gospel as opposed to each other. But the Gospel has not succeeded the whole Law in such a way as to introduce a different method of salvation. Rather, it confirms the Law and proves that everything it promised is fulfilled. What was shadow, it has made substance. When Christ says that the Law and the Prophets were until John, he does not consign the fathers to the curse which, as slaves of the Law, they could not escape. He indicates that they were instructed only in the rudiments, and remained far below the height of the Gospel teaching. Accordingly Paul, after calling the Gospel "the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth," shortly afterward adds that it was "witnessed by the Law and the Prophets" (Romans 1:16; 3:21). And at the end of the same Epistle, although he describes "the preaching of Jesus Christ" as "the revelation of the mystery which was kept secret since the world began," he qualifies the expression by adding that it is "now made manifest" "by the scriptures of the prophets" (Romans 16:25, 26). From this we infer that when the whole Law is spoken of, the Gospel differs from it only in clearness of manifestation. Still, on account of the inestimable riches of grace set before us in Christ, there is good reason for saying that by his coming the kingdom of heaven was set up on earth (Matthew 12:28).

5¶

John stands between the Law and the Gospel, holding an intermediate office related to both. For although he gave a summary of the Gospel when he declared Christ to be "the Lamb of God who taketh away the sin of the world," yet, since he did not unfold the incomparable power and glory which shone forth in Christ's resurrection, Christ says that he was not equal to the apostles. For this is the meaning of the words: "Among them that are born of woman, there has not risen a greater than John the Baptist: notwithstanding, he that is least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he" (Matthew 11:28). He is not there commending the persons of men; rather, after preferring John to all the prophets, he gives the first place to the preaching of the Gospel, which is elsewhere called the kingdom of heaven. When John himself, answering the Jews, says that he is only "a voice" (John 1:23), as if he were inferior to the prophets, it is not out of pretended humility. He means to teach that the proper embassy was not entrusted to him, and that he only performed the office of a messenger, as Malachi had foretold: "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophets before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the Lord" (Malachi 4:5). And indeed, throughout the whole course of his ministry he did nothing more than prepare disciples for Christ. He even proves from Isaiah that this was the office to which he had been divinely appointed. In this sense Christ says that he was "a burning and a shining light" (John 5:35), because full day had not yet appeared. And yet this does not prevent us from counting him among the preachers of the gospel, since he used the same baptism which was afterward committed to the apostles. Still, he only began what had freer course under the apostles, after Christ was taken up into heavenly glory.

Calvin shows that Christ, though truly known to the fathers under the law, was only manifested in full light under the gospel — the patriarchs saw his day from afar and obscurely, while we behold it clearly in the flesh. He refutes the dream that the older promises were swept away, insisting instead on one continuous mercy unfolding from shadow to substance. For the believer in 2026, this breeds a sober gratitude that resists chronological pride. We are not wiser than Abraham; we are merely later, given fuller light by no merit of our own — and to whom much is given, much is required. Let no household treat the Old Testament as a discarded husk, for the same Christ walks its pages, dimly seen, faithfully promised. And let none of us, standing in the noon of revelation, prove duller than those who walked by dawn. The privilege of clear sight is a charge to watchfulness: we have seen the substance, and we will answer for what we have seen.
