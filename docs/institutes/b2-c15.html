Book 2 · Chapter 15 · USMC modern English

Three Things Briefly to Be Regarded in Christ — His Offices of Prophet, King, and Priest

The principal parts of this chapter are—I. Of the Prophetical Office of Christ, its dignity and use, sec. 1, 2. II. The nature of the Kingly power of Christ, and the advantage we derive from it, sec. 3–5. III. Of the Priesthood of Christ, and the efficacy of it, sec. 6.

Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. Among heretics and false Christians, Christ is found in name only; but by those who are truly and effectually called of God, he is acknowledged as a Prophet, King, and Priest. In regard to the Prophetical Office, the Redeemer of the Church is the same from whom believers under the Law hoped for the full light of understanding.
  2. 2. The unction of Christ, though it has respect chiefly to the Kingly Office, refers also to the Prophetical and Priestly Offices. The dignity, necessity, and use of this unction.
  3. 3. From the spirituality of Christ’s kingdom its eternity is inferred. This twofold, referring both to the whole body of the Church, and to its individual members.
  4. 4. Benefits from the spiritual kingdom of Christ. 1. It raises us to eternal life. 2. It enriches us with all things necessary to salvation. 3. It makes us invincible by spiritual foes. 4. It animates us to patient endurance. 5. It inspires confidence and triumph. 6. It supplies fortitude and love.
  5. 5. The unction of our Redeemer heavenly. Symbol of this unction. A passage in the apostle reconciled with others previously quoted, to prove the eternal kingdom of Christ.
  6. 6. What necessary to obtain the benefit of Christ’s Priesthood. We must set out with the death of Christ. From it follows, 1. His intercession for us. 2. Confidence in prayer. 3. Peace of conscience. 4. Through Christ, Christians themselves become priests. Grievous sin of the Papists in pretending to sacrifice Christ.
1¶

Although heretics make a pretense of the name of Christ, Augustine truly affirms (Enchiridion to Laurentius, chapter 5) that the foundation is not something they share with the godly, but belongs exclusively to the Church. For if the things that belong to Christ are carefully considered, it will be found that Christ is with them in name only, not in reality. So in our own day, although the Papists have the words Son of God and Redeemer of the world sounding in their mouths, yet because they are content with an empty name, they rob him of his power and dignity; what Paul says of "not holding the head" truly applies to them (Colossians 2:19). Therefore, so that faith may find in Christ a solid ground of salvation and so rest in him, we must begin with this principle: that the office he received from the Father consists of three parts. For he was appointed Prophet, King, and Priest; though little would be gained by holding the names without a knowledge of their end and use. These names are spoken of in the Papacy too, but coldly and without much benefit, since the full meaning contained under each title is not understood. We observed earlier that although God, by supplying an unbroken succession of prophets, never left his people without useful doctrine such as might suffice for salvation, yet the minds of believers were always impressed with the conviction that the full light of understanding was to be expected only at the coming of the Messiah. This expectation had accordingly reached even the Samaritans, to whom the true religion had never been made known. This is plain from the words of the woman: "I know that Messiah cometh, which is called Christ: when he is come, he will tell us all things" (John 4:25). Nor was this a mere random assumption that had entered the minds of the Jews. They believed what sure oracles had taught them. One of the most remarkable passages is that of Isaiah: "Behold, I have given him for a witness to the people, a leader and commander to the people" (Isaiah 55:4); that is, in the same way in which he had earlier, in another place, called him "Wonderful, Counsellor" (Isaiah 9:6). Calvin translates, "Angelum vel Interpretem magni consilii," "the Angel or interpreter of the great counsel."

2¶

Moreover, it should be observed that the name Christ refers to these three offices, for we know that under the law prophets as well as priests and kings were anointed with holy oil. From this, too, the celebrated name of Messiah was given to the promised Mediator. But although I grant (as indeed I have shown elsewhere) that he was so called with a view to the nature of the kingly office, the prophetic and priestly anointings still have their proper place and must not be overlooked. The former is expressly mentioned by Isaiah in these words: "The Spirit of the Lord God is upon me: because the Lord has anointed me to preach good tidings unto the meek; he has sent me to bind up the broken-hearted, to proclaim liberty to the captive, and the opening of the prison to them that are bound; to proclaim the acceptable year of the Lord" (Isaiah 60:1, 2). We see that he was anointed by the Spirit to be a herald and witness of his Father's grace, and not in the ordinary way; for he is set apart from other teachers who held a similar office. And here again it should be observed that the anointing he received in order to perform the office of teacher was not for himself but for his whole body, so that a corresponding power of the Spirit might always accompany the preaching of the Gospel. This, however, remains certain: by the perfection of the doctrine he brought, an end was put to all the prophecies, so that those who, not content with the Gospel, attach something foreign to it detract from its authority. The voice that thundered from heaven, "This is my beloved Son, hear him," gave him a special privilege above all other teachers. Then from him, as the head, this anointing is spread through the members, as Joel foretold: "Your sons and your daughters shall prophesy, your old men shall dream dreams, and your young men shall see visions" (Joel 2:28). Paul's expressions, that he was "made unto us wisdom" (1 Corinthians 1:30), and elsewhere, that in him "are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Colossians 2:3), have a somewhat different meaning: namely, that apart from him there is nothing worth knowing, and that those who by faith grasp his true character possess the boundless immensity of heavenly blessings. For this reason he says elsewhere, "I determined not to know any thing among you, save Jesus Christ and him crucified" (1 Corinthians 2:2). And most rightly, for it is not lawful to go beyond the simplicity of the Gospel. The purpose of this prophetic dignity in Christ is to teach us that the doctrine he delivered contains in substance a wisdom that is perfect in all its parts.

3¶

I come to the kingly office, of which it would be useless to speak without first reminding the reader that its nature is spiritual; for it is from this that we learn its power, the benefits it confers, and its whole strength and eternity. Eternity, moreover, which an angel in Daniel attributes to the office of Christ (Daniel 2:44), an angel in Luke rightly applies to the salvation of his people (Luke 1:33). But this too is twofold and must be viewed in two ways: the one concerns the whole body of the Church, the other is proper to each member. To the former must be referred what is said in the Psalms: "Once have I sworn by my holiness, that I will not lie unto David. His seed shall endure for ever, and his throne as the sun before me. It shall be established for ever, as the moon, and as a faithful witness in heaven" (Psalm 89:35, 37). There can be no doubt that God here promises that he will be, by the hand of his Son, the eternal governor and defender of the Church. The fulfillment of this prophecy will be found in no one but Christ, since immediately after Solomon's death the kingdom lost its dignity in great measure and, to the disgrace of the family of David, was transferred to a private individual. Afterward, decaying by degrees, it at last came to a sad and dishonorable end. In the same sense we are to understand the exclamation of Isaiah, "Who shall declare his generation?" (Isaiah 53:8). For he asserts that Christ will so outlive death as to be joined with his members. Therefore, whenever we hear that Christ is armed with eternal power, let us learn that the permanence of the Church is thereby effectively secured; that amid the violent upheavals by which it is constantly harassed, and the grievous and fearful commotions that threaten countless disasters, it still remains safe. So when David mocks the audacity of the enemies who try to throw off the yoke of God and his anointed, and says that kings and nations rage "in vain" (Psalm 2:2–4) because he who sits in heaven is strong enough to repel their assaults, he assures believers of the perpetual preservation of the Church and encourages them to keep good hope whenever it is at times oppressed. So too in another place, speaking in the person of God, he says, "The Lord said unto my Lord, Sit thou at my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool" (Psalm 110:1), and so reminds us that however many and powerful the enemies who conspire to attack the Church, they do not have strength enough to prevail against the immortal decree by which he appointed his Son eternal King. From this it follows that the devil, with the whole power of the world, can never possibly destroy the Church, which is founded on the eternal throne of Christ. Then, as to the particular use each believer should make of it, this same eternity ought to lift us up to the hope of a blessed immortality. For we see that everything earthly and of the world is temporary and soon fades away. Christ, therefore, to raise our hope to heaven, declares that his kingdom is not of this world (John 18:36). In short, let each of us, when he hears that the kingdom of Christ is spiritual, be roused by the thought to cherish the hope of a better life, and to expect that, as it is now protected by the hand of Christ, so it will be fully realized in a future life.

4¶

That the strength and usefulness of the kingdom of Christ cannot, as we have said, be fully perceived without recognizing it as spiritual is plain enough even from this: that since we must fight under the cross through the whole course of our lives, our condition here is bitter and wretched. What, then, would it profit us to be ranked under the government of a heavenly King if its benefits were not realized beyond this present earthly life? We must know, therefore, that the happiness promised to us in Christ does not consist in outward advantages, such as leading a joyful and peaceful life, abounding in wealth, being safe from all harm, and having a wealth of delights such as the flesh is accustomed to crave, but belongs properly to the heavenly life. In the world, the prosperous and desirable condition of a people consists partly in an abundance of temporal goods and peace at home, and partly in the strong protection that gives security against violence from outside. So also Christ enriches his people with all things necessary for the eternal salvation of their souls, and strengthens them with courage to stand unshaken against all the attacks of spiritual enemies. From this we infer that he reigns more for us than for himself, and that both within us and outside us: so that, being filled, as far as God knows to be good for us, with the gifts of the Spirit, which by nature we lack, we may feel from their firstfruits that we are truly united to God for perfect blessedness; and then, trusting in the power of the same Spirit, may not doubt that we shall always be victorious over the devil, the world, and everything that can do us harm. To this effect was our Savior's reply to the Pharisees: "The kingdom of God is within you." "The kingdom of God cometh not with observation" (Luke 17:21, 22). It is likely that, when he declared himself to be that King under whom the highest blessing of God was to be expected, they had mockingly asked him to show his insignia. But to keep those who were already more than enough inclined toward the earth from dwelling on its pomp, he bids them enter into their consciences, for "the kingdom of God" is "righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost" (Romans 14:17). These words teach briefly what the kingdom of Christ bestows on us. Since it is not earthly or carnal, and so subject to corruption, but spiritual, it raises us even to eternal life, so that we can live patiently for the present under toil, hunger, cold, contempt, disgrace, and other troubles; content with this, that our King will never abandon us, but will supply our needs until our warfare is over and we are called to triumph. For such is the nature of his kingdom that he shares with us whatever he received from his Father. Since, then, he arms and equips us by his power, adorns us with splendor and magnificence, and enriches us with wealth, we find here the most abundant cause for glorying, and are also inspired with boldness, so that we can fight fearlessly against the devil, sin, and death. In short, clothed with his righteousness, we can bravely rise above all the insults of the world; and as he fills us generously with his gifts, so we in turn can bring forth fruit to his glory.

5¶

Accordingly, his royal anointing is not set before us as made of oil or fragrant perfumes; rather, he is called the Christ of God because "the Spirit of the Lord" rested upon him, "the Spirit of wisdom and understanding, the Spirit of counsel and might, the Spirit of knowledge and of the fear of the Lord" (Isaiah 11:2). This is the oil of gladness with which the Psalmist declares he was anointed above his fellows (Psalm 45:7). For, as has been said, he was not enriched privately for himself, but so that he might refresh the parched and hungry out of his abundance. For just as the Father is said to have given the Spirit to the Son without measure (John 3:34), so the reason is stated: that we might all receive of his fullness, and grace for grace (John 1:16). From this fountain flows that abundant supply (which Paul mentions, Ephesians 4:7) by which grace is distributed variously to believers according to the measure of the gift of Christ. Here we have ample confirmation of what I said: that the kingdom of Christ consists in the Spirit, not in earthly delights or pomp, and that therefore, in order to share with him, we must renounce the world. A visible sign of this grace was displayed at the baptism of Christ, when the Spirit rested upon him in the form of a dove. To designate the Spirit and his gifts by the term "unction" is nothing new and ought not to seem absurd (see 1 John 2:20, 27), because this is the only source from which we draw life. But especially in what concerns the heavenly life, there is not a drop of strength in us except what the Holy Spirit pours in, who has chosen his seat in Christ so that from there the heavenly riches we lack might flow to us in rich abundance. And because believers stand unconquerable in the strength of their King, and his spiritual riches overflow toward them, they are not improperly called Christians. Moreover, nothing in the expression of Paul detracts from this eternity of which we have spoken: "Then cometh the end, when he shall have delivered up the kingdom to God, even the Father" (1 Corinthians 15:24); and also, "Then shall the Son also himself be subject unto him that put all things under him, that God may be all in all" (1 Corinthians 15:28). For the meaning is simply that in that perfect glory the administration of the kingdom will not be such as it is now. For the Father has given all power to the Son, so that by his hand he may govern, cherish, and sustain us, keep us under his guardianship, and come to our aid. So, while we wander far from God as pilgrims, Christ steps in between, so that he may gradually bring us to full communion with God. And indeed, his sitting at the right hand of the Father means the same as if he were called the Father's deputy, entrusted with the whole power of government. For God is pleased to rule and defend the Church mediately (so to speak), in his person. So also Paul, in the Epistle to the Ephesians, explains his being seated at the right hand of the Father to mean that "he is the head over all things to the Church, which is his body" (Ephesians 1:20, 22). Nor is this different in substance from what he teaches elsewhere, that God has "given him a name which is above every name; that at the name of Jesus every knee shall bow, of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth, and that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father" (Philippians 2:9–11). For in these words too he commends an arrangement in the kingdom of Christ which is necessary for our present weakness. So Paul rightly infers that God will then be the only Head of the Church, because the office of Christ in defending the Church will then have been completed. For the same reason Scripture everywhere calls him Lord, since the Father appointed him over us for the express purpose of exercising his government through him. For although many lordships are celebrated in the world, Paul says, "To us there is but one God, the Father, of whom are all things, and we in him; and one Lord Jesus Christ, by whom are all things, and we by him" (1 Corinthians 8:6). From this it is rightly inferred that he is the same God who declared by the mouth of Isaiah, "The Lord is our Judge, the Lord is our Lawgiver, the Lord is our King: he will save us" (Isaiah 33:22). For although he everywhere describes all the power he possesses as the benefit and gift of the Father, the meaning is simply that he reigns by divine authority, because his reason for taking on the office of Mediator was that, coming down from the bosom and incomprehensible glory of the Father, he might draw near to us. All the more reason, then, that we should all with one accord prepare ourselves to obey, and yield unreserved obedience to his will with the greatest eagerness. For just as he unites the offices of King and Shepherd toward believers who submit to him willingly, so on the other hand we are told that he wields an iron scepter to break and crush all the rebellious like a potter's vessel (Psalm 2:9). We are also told that he will be the Judge of the Gentiles, that he will cover the earth with dead bodies, and bring down every opposing height (Psalm 110:6). Examples of this are seen at present, but full proof will be given at the final judgment, which may properly be regarded as the last act of his reign.

6¶

With regard to his priesthood, we must briefly hold its end and use to be this: that as a Mediator free from all taint he may, by his own holiness, obtain the favor of God for us. But because a deserved curse bars the entrance, and God in his character as Judge is hostile to us, expiation must necessarily intervene, so that as a priest employed to appease the wrath of God he may restore us to his favor. Therefore, in order for Christ to fulfill this office, he had to appear with a sacrifice. For even under the law of the priesthood it was forbidden to enter the sanctuary without blood, to teach the worshiper that, however the priest might intervene to plead, God could not be appeased without the expiation of sin. The Apostle discusses this subject at length in the Epistle to the Hebrews, from the seventh chapter almost to the end of the tenth. The sum of it is this: that the honor of the priesthood belonged to no one but Christ, because by the sacrifice of his death he wiped away our guilt and made satisfaction for sin. We are reminded of the great importance of this matter by that solemn oath which God uttered, and of which he declared he would not repent: "Thou art a priest for ever, after the order of Melchizedek" (Psalm 110:4). For without doubt his purpose was to confirm the point on which he knew our salvation chiefly turned. For, as has been said, there is no access to God for us or for our prayers until the priest, cleansing away our defilements, sanctifies us and obtains for us that favor of which the impurity of our lives and hearts deprives us. So we see that if the benefit and power of Christ's priesthood is to reach us, the beginning must be made with his death. From this it follows that he by whose help we obtain favor must be a perpetual intercessor. From this, again, arises not only confidence in prayer but also the peace of godly minds, as they rest safely on the fatherly kindness of God and feel assured that whatever has been consecrated by the Mediator is pleasing to him. But whereas God under the Law commanded sacrifices of beasts to be offered to him, there was a different and new arrangement with regard to Christ: namely, that he should be at once victim and priest, because no other fitting satisfaction for sin could be found, nor was anyone worthy of the honor of offering an only begotten son to God. Christ now bears the office of priest not only so that, by the eternal law of reconciliation, he may make the Father favorable and gracious to us, but also so that he may admit us into this most honorable partnership. For we, though polluted in ourselves, being priests in him (Revelation 1:6), offer ourselves and all we have to God, and freely enter the heavenly sanctuary, so that the sacrifices of prayer and praise we present are acceptable and a sweet fragrance before him. To this effect are the words of Christ, "For their sakes I sanctify myself" (John 17:19). For, being clothed with his holiness, since he has dedicated us to the Father together with himself (otherwise we were an abomination before him), we please him as if we were pure and clean, and even sacred. Hence that anointing of the sanctuary which is mentioned in Daniel (Daniel 9:24). For we must note the contrast between this anointing and the shadowy one then in use; as if the angel had said that when the shadows were scattered, there would be a clear priesthood in the person of Christ. All the more detestable, therefore, is the invention of those who, not content with the priesthood of Christ, have dared to take it upon themselves to sacrifice him, a thing attempted daily in the Papacy, where the mass is represented as an immolation of Christ.

Christ is Prophet, King, and Priest — and where these offices are not known in their use, only an empty name remains. As Prophet he is the final Word, so that we annex nothing to the gospel and seek no wisdom outside him; as King he reigns spiritually and eternally, securing the Church through every storm and arming each believer against the devil, the world, and despair; as Priest he has by his own blood opened the way to a God who would otherwise be our Judge. In 2026, when restless souls chase new revelations and the powers of the age rage as if the throne were empty, settle your heart on this threefold ministry. Let the family altar rest on his prophetic Word, not the noise of the hour; let the harassed church remember its King reigns from a throne no enemy can topple; let the trembling conscience flee to the one Priest whose intercession never fails. He was anointed not for himself but to pour his fullness upon us — and in him every believer is made, in turn, a priest who offers his whole life as a sacrifice of praise.
