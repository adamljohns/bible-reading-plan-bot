The Beginning of Justification — In What Sense Progressive

To illustrate what has been already said, and show what kind of righteousness man can have during the whole course of his life, mankind are divided into four classes. I. First class considered, sec. 1-6. II. Second and third classes considered together, sec. 7, 8. III. Fourth class considered, sec. 9 to end. 1. Men either idolatrous, profane, hypocritical, or regenerate. 1. Idolaters void of righteousness, full of unrighteousness, and hence in the sight of God altogether wretched and undone. 2. Still a great difference in the characters of men. This difference manifested. 1. In the gifts of God. 2. In the distinction between honorable and base. 3. In the blessings of he present life. 3. All human virtue, how praiseworthy soever it may appear, is corrupted. 1. By impurity of heart. 2. By the absence of a proper nature. 4. By the want of Christ, without whom there is no life. 5. Natural condition of man as described by Scripture. All men dead in sins before regeneration. 6. Passages of Scripture to this effect. Vulgar error confounding the righteousness of works with the redemption purchased by Christ. 7. The second and third classes of men, comprehending hypocrites and Christians in name only. Every action of theirs deserves condemnation. Passage from Haggai. Objection. Answer. 8. Other passages. Quotations from Augustine and Gregory. 9. The fourth class—viz. the regenerate. Though guided by the Spirit, corruption adheres to all they do, especially when brought to the bar of God. 10. One fault sufficient to efface all former righteousness. Hence they cannot possibly be justified by works. 11. In addition to the two former arguments, a third adduced against the Sophists, to show that whatever be the works of the regenerate, they are justified solely by faith and the free imputation of Christ’s righteousness. 12. Sophism of the Schoolmen in opposition to the above doctrine. Answer. 13. Answer explained. Refutation of the fiction of partial righteousness, and compensation by works of supererogation. This fiction necessarily falls with that of satisfaction. 14. Statement of our Savior—viz. that after we have done all, we are still unprofitable servants. 15. Objection founded on Paul’s boasting. Answer, showing the Apostle’s meaning. Other answers, stating the general doctrine out of Chrysostom. Third answer, showing that supererogation is the merest vanity. 16. Fourth answer, showing how Scripture dissuades us from all confidence in works. Fifth answer, showing that we have no ground of boasting. 17. Sixth answer, showing, in regard to four different classes, that works have no part in procuring our salvation. 1. The efficient cause is the free love of the Father. 2. The material cause is Christ acquiring righteousness for us. 3. The instrumental cause is faith. 4. The final cause the display of the divine justice and praise of the divine goodness. 18. A second objection, founded on the glorying of saints. An answer, explaining these modes of expression. How the saints feel in regard to the certainty of salvation. The opinion they have of their own works as in the sight of God. 19. Another answer—viz. that the elect, by this kind of glorying, refer only to their adoption by the Father as proved by the fruits of their calling. The order of this glorying. Its foundation, structure, and parts. 20. Conclusion. The saints neither attribute anything to the merits of works, nor derogate in any degree from the righteousness which they obtain in Christ. Confirmation from a passage of Augustine, in which he gives two reasons why no believer will presume to boast before God of his works. 21. A third objection—viz. that the good works of believers are the causes of divine blessings. Answer. There are inferior causes, but these depend on free justification, which is the only true cause why God blesses us. These modes of expression designate the order of sequence rather than the cause.

1¶

To illustrate the subject further, let us consider what kind of righteousness man can have during the whole course of his life, and for this purpose let us make a fourfold division. Either men, endowed with no knowledge of God, are sunk in idolatry; or, initiated in the sacraments but denying by the impurity of their lives him whom they confess with their mouths, they are Christians in name only; or they are hypocrites, who hide the wickedness of the heart with empty pretenses; or they are regenerated by the Spirit of God and aspire to true holiness. In the first place, when men are judged by their natural endowments, not one iota of good will be found from the crown of the head to the sole of the foot, unless we are to charge Scripture with falsehood when it describes all the sons of Adam in terms such as these: "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked." "The imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth." "The Lord knows the thoughts of man, that they are vanity." "They are all gone aside: they are altogether become filthy; there is none that does good, no, not one." In short, Scripture says that they are flesh, a name under which are included all those works which Paul lists: adultery, fornication, uncleanness, lasciviousness, idolatry, witchcraft, hatred, variance, emulation, wrath, strife, seditions, heresies, envyings, murders, drunkenness, revelings, and every kind of pollution and abomination that can be imagined. Jer. 17:9; Gen. 7:21; Ps. 94:11; 36:2; 14:2, 3; Gen. 6:3; Gal. 5:19.

2¶

First, then, I do not deny that whatever excellent endowments appear in unbelievers Latin, "in incredulis." French, "en la vie des infideles et idolatres" — in the life of infidels and idolaters.

3¶

Still, the observation of Augustine is true, that all who are strangers to the true God, however excellent they may be thought on account of their virtues, are more deserving of punishment than of reward, because by the pollution of their heart they defile the pure gifts of God (Augustine, Against Julian, book 4). For though they are instruments of God for preserving human society by justice, continence, friendship, temperance, fortitude, and prudence, yet they carry out these good works of God in the worst manner, because what keeps them from acting wickedly is not a sincere love of goodness, but merely ambition or self-love or some other crooked motive. Since, then, these actions are polluted at their very source by impurity of heart, they have no better claim to be counted among the virtues than those vices which deceive us by their likeness or resemblance to virtue. In short, when we remember that the end at which righteousness always aims is the service of God, whatever tends elsewhere rightly forfeits the name. Hence, since they have no regard for the end which the divine wisdom prescribes, although the act seems good in its performance, yet because of the perverse motive it is sin. Augustine therefore concludes that all the Fabriciuses, the Scipios, and the Catos, Latin, "omnes Fabricios, Scipiones, Catones." French, "tous ceux qui ont esté prisez entre les Pagans" — all those who have been prized among the heathen.

4¶

Moreover, if it is true, as John says, that there is no life without the Son of God (1 John 5:12), then those who have no part in Christ, whoever they may be and whatever they do or devise, are hurrying on throughout their whole course to destruction and the judgment of eternal death. For this reason Augustine says, "Our religion distinguishes the righteous from the wicked by the law, not of works, but of faith, without which works that seem good are turned into sins" (Augustine, To Boniface, book 3, chapter 5). He expresses the same idea finely in another passage, where he compares the zeal of such men to runners in a race who mistake the course (Augustine, Preface to Psalm 31). The man who is off the course is the farther from the goal the more swiftly he runs, and is therefore the more unhappy. It is better to limp along the way than to run outside the way. Lastly, since there is no sanctification without union with Christ, it is evident that they are bad trees, which may be beautiful and fair to look upon, and may even produce fruit sweet to the taste, but are still very far from good. Hence we easily see that everything which man thinks, plans, and does before he is reconciled to God by faith is cursed, and is not only of no use for justification but merits certain damnation. And why do we speak of this as if it were doubtful, when it has already been proved by the testimony of an apostle that "without faith it is impossible to please God" (Heb. 11:6)?

5¶

But the proof will be clearer still if divine grace is set against the natural condition of man. For Scripture everywhere proclaims that God finds nothing in man to move him to show kindness, but that he goes before man by his free generosity. What can a dead man do to obtain life? Yet when God enlightens us with the knowledge of himself, he is said to raise us from the dead and to make us new creatures (John 5:25). On this ground we see that the kindness of God toward us is often commended, especially by the apostle: "God," he says, "who is rich in mercy, for his great love with which he loved us, even when we were dead in sins, has quickened us together with Christ" (Eph. 2:4). In another passage, when treating of the general calling of believers under the type of Abraham, he says, "God quickens the dead, and calls those things which be not as though they were" (Rom. 4:17). If we are nothing, what, I ask, can we do? Therefore in the Book of Job the Lord sternly puts down all arrogance in these words: "Who has prevented me, that I should repay him? Whatever is under the whole heaven is mine" (Job 41:11). Paul, explaining this saying, applies it in this way: let us not imagine that we bring to the Lord anything but the bare disgrace of want and destitution (Rom. 11:35). Therefore, in the passage quoted above, to prove that we attain to the hope of salvation not by works but by grace alone, he affirms that "we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God has before ordained that we should walk in them" (Eph. 2:10). It is as if he had said: Which of us can boast of having laid a claim on God by his own righteousness, seeing that our first power to act rightly comes from regeneration? For as we are formed by nature, oil will sooner be pressed out of a stone than good works out of us. It is truly astonishing that man, convicted of such disgrace, still dares to claim anything as his own. Let us acknowledge, therefore, with that chosen vessel, that God "has called us with a holy calling, not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace," and that "the kindness and love of God our Savior toward men appeared, not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us," so that, being justified by his grace, we might become heirs of eternal life (2 Tim. 1:9; Titus 3:4, 5). By this confession we strip man of every particle of righteousness until, by mere mercy, he is regenerated to the hope of eternal life, since it is not true to say that we are justified by grace if works contribute in any degree to our justification. The apostle had certainly not forgotten himself in declaring that justification is free, seeing that he argues in another place that if works are of any avail, "grace is no more grace" (Rom. 11:6). And what else does our Lord mean when he declares, "I am not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance" (Matt. 9:13)? If sinners alone are admitted, why do we seek admission by means of a fictitious righteousness?

6¶

The thought keeps recurring to me that I am in danger of insulting the mercy of God by laboring with so much anxiety to defend it, as if it were doubtful or obscure. Such, however, is our perversity in refusing to grant God what belongs to him until we are most strongly pressed, that I am obliged to insist on it at greater length. But since Scripture is clear enough on this subject, I will contend in its words rather than in my own. Isaiah, after describing the universal ruin of the human race, finely adds the method of restoration: "The Lord saw it, and it displeased him that there was no judgment. And he saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no intercessor: therefore his arm brought salvation unto him; and his righteousness, it sustained him" (Isaiah 59:15, 16). Where is our righteousness, if the prophet says truly that no man gives the Lord any help in recovering salvation? So another prophet, introducing the Lord as dealing with the reconciliation of sinners, says, "I will betroth thee unto me for ever; yea, I will betroth thee unto me in righteousness, and in judgment, and in loving-kindness, and in mercies." "I will have mercy upon her that had not obtained mercy" (Hosea 2:19, 23). If a covenant of this kind, which plainly forms our first union with God, depends on mercy, no foundation is left for our righteousness. And indeed I would like to know from those who pretend that man meets God with some righteousness of works whether they imagine there is any kind of righteousness other than that which is acceptable to him. If it would be insane to think so, can anything pleasing to God come from his enemies, whom he abhors together with all their deeds? The truth declares that we are all the open and inveterate enemies of God until we are justified and received into his friendship (Rom. 5:6; Col. 1:21). If justification is the beginning of love, how can the righteousness of works come before it? Hence John, to put down this arrogant idea, carefully reminds us that God first loved us (1 John 4:10). The Lord had taught the same thing long before by his prophet: "I will love them freely: for mine anger is turned away from him" (Hosea 14:4). Surely he is not moved by works if his love turns to us of its own accord. But the crude and common notion is that we did not merit the intervention of Christ for our redemption, but that we are helped by our works in obtaining possession of it. On the contrary, though we may be redeemed by Christ, still, until we are grafted into union with him by the calling of the Father, we are darkness, heirs of death, and enemies of God. For Paul declares that we are not cleansed and washed from our impurities by the blood of Christ until the Spirit accomplishes that cleansing in us (1 Cor. 6:11). Peter, meaning to say the same thing, declares that the sanctification of the Spirit avails "unto obedience and sprinkling of the blood of Jesus Christ" (1 Pet. 1:2). If the sprinkling of the blood of Christ by the Spirit gives us purification, let us not think that before this sprinkling we are anything but sinners without Christ. Let us, therefore, hold it as certain that the beginning of our salvation is, as it were, a resurrection from death to life, because only when it is given to us on behalf of Christ to believe on him (Phil. 1:29) do we begin to pass from death to life.

7¶

Under this head are included the second and third classes of men noted in the division above. Impurity of conscience proves that neither of these classes is yet regenerated by the Spirit of God. And again, the fact that they are not regenerated proves their lack of faith. From this it is clear that they are not yet reconciled, not yet justified, since it is only by faith that these blessings are obtained. What can sinners, estranged from God, produce except what is abominable in his sight? Such, however, is the stupid confidence of all the wicked, and especially of hypocrites, that however conscious they are that their whole heart teems with impurity, they still consider whatever counterfeit works they may perform to be worthy of God's approval. Hence the ruinous consequence that, though convicted of a wicked and impious mind, they cannot be brought to confess that they are devoid of righteousness. Even when they acknowledge themselves to be unrighteous, because they cannot deny it, they still claim for themselves some degree of righteousness. The Lord admirably refutes this vanity by the prophet: "Ask now the priests concerning the law, saying, If one bear holy flesh in the skirt of his garment, and with his skirt do touch bread, or pottage, or wine, or oil, or any meat, shall it be holy? And the priests answered and said, No. Then said Haggai, If one that is unclean by a dead body touch any of these, shall it be unclean? And the priests answered and said, It shall be unclean. Then answered Haggai, and said, So is this people, and so is this nation before me, says the Lord; and so is every work of their hands; and that which they offer there is unclean" (Haggai 2:11-14). I wish these words could gain full belief among us and be deeply fixed in our memories. For there is no man, however shameful the whole course of his life may be, who will allow himself to be convinced of what the Lord here so clearly declares. As soon as anyone, even the most wicked, has performed some single duty of the law, he does not hesitate to impute it to himself for righteousness; but the Lord declares that no degree of holiness is gained by it unless the heart has first been made pure. And not content with this, he declares that all the works performed by sinners are defiled by impurity of heart. Let us stop, then, giving the name of righteousness to works which the mouth of the Lord condemns as polluted. How well this is shown by that elegant comparison! It might be objected that what the Lord has commanded is inviolably holy. But he replies, on the contrary, that it is no wonder if the things which are sanctified in the law are defiled by the impurity of the wicked, since the unclean hand profanes what is sacred by handling it.

8¶

The same argument is admirably pursued by Isaiah: "Bring no more vain oblations; incense is an abomination unto me; the new moons and sabbaths, the calling of assemblies, I cannot away with; it is iniquity, even the solemn meeting. Your new moons and your appointed feasts my soul hateth: they are a trouble unto me; I am weary to bear them. And when ye spread forth your hands I will hide mine eyes from you; yea, when ye make many prayers, I will not hear: your hands are full of blood. Wash you, make you clean; put away the evil of your doings from before mine eyes" (Isaiah 1:13-16, compared with chapter 58). What does the Lord mean by loathing in this way the observance of his own law? In truth, he does not reject anything belonging to the genuine observance of the law, the beginning of which, as he constantly declares, is the sincere fear of his name. When this is lacking, all the services offered to him are not only worthless but vile and abominable. Let hypocrites go now, and, while keeping depravity wrapped up in their hearts, try to put God under obligation by their works. In this way they will only offend him more and more. "The sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination to the Lord; but the prayer of the upright is his delight" (Prov. 15:8). We hold it, therefore, as beyond doubt, and indeed it should be well known to all who are even moderately versed in the Scriptures, that the most splendid works performed by men who are not yet truly sanctified are so far from being righteousness in the sight of the Lord that he regards them as sins. And therefore it is taught with perfect truth that no man gains favor with God by means of works, but that, on the contrary, works are not pleasing to God unless the person has first found favor in his sight. See Augustine, On Penitence, and Gregory, whose words are quoted in the Sentences, book 3, question 7.

9¶

Let us now see what kind of righteousness belongs to those whom we have placed in the fourth class. We admit that when God reconciles us to himself through the righteousness of Christ, and, bestowing on us the free pardon of sins, regards us as righteous, his goodness is at the same time joined with mercy, so that he dwells in us by his Holy Spirit, by whose working the lusts of our flesh are every day more and more mortified, while we ourselves are sanctified; that is, consecrated to the Lord for true purity of life, our hearts being trained to obedience to the law. It thus becomes our chief desire to obey his will and in all things to advance his glory alone. Still, while we walk in the ways of the Lord under the guidance of the Holy Spirit, lest we should become unduly puffed up and forget ourselves, there are still remnants of imperfection in us which serve to keep us humble: "There is no man that sinneth not," says Scripture (1 Kings 8:46). What righteousness, then, can men obtain by their works? First, I say that the best thing they can produce is always tainted and corrupted by the impurity of the flesh, and has, as it were, some mixture of dross in it. Let the holy servant of God, I say, choose from the whole course of his life the action he thinks most excellent, and let him weigh it in all its parts; he will doubtless find in it something that smells of the rottenness of the flesh, since our eagerness in doing good is never what it ought to be, but our course is always slowed by much weakness. Although we see that the stains which blemish the works of the righteous are by no means hidden, still, granting that they are the smallest possible, will they give no offense to the eye of God, before whom even the stars are not clean? We see, then, that even the saints cannot perform one work which, if judged on its own merits, does not deserve condemnation.

10¶

Even if it were possible for us to perform works that were absolutely pure, yet one sin is enough to blot out and extinguish all remembrance of former righteousness, as the prophet says (Ezek. 18:24). James agrees with this: "Whosoever shall keep the whole law, and yet offend in one point, is guilty of all" (James 2:10). And since this mortal life is never entirely free from the taint of sin, whatever righteousness we could acquire would again and again be corrupted, overwhelmed, and destroyed by later sins, so that it could not stand the scrutiny of God or be imputed to us for righteousness. In short, whenever we speak of the righteousness of works, we must look not to the work done under the law but to the command itself. Therefore, when righteousness is sought by the law, it is useless to bring forward one or two single works; we must show an unbroken obedience. God does not (as many foolishly imagine) impute that forgiveness of sins once for all as righteousness, so that, having obtained pardon for our past life, we may afterward seek righteousness in the law. That would only be to mock and delude us with false hopes. For since perfection is altogether beyond our reach as long as we are clothed with flesh, and the law pronounces death and judgment against all who have not yielded a perfect righteousness, there will always be grounds to accuse and convict us unless the mercy of God intervenes and again and again absolves us by the constant forgiveness of sins. Therefore the statement with which we began is always true: if we are measured by our own worthiness, then in everything that we think or devise, with all our efforts and endeavors, we deserve death and destruction.

11¶

We must strongly insist on these two things: that no believer has ever performed one work which, if tested by the strict judgment of God, could escape condemnation; and further, that even if this were granted to be possible (though it is not), yet since the act is vitiated and polluted by the sins of which its author is certainly guilty, it is stripped of its merit. This is the cardinal point of the present discussion. There is no controversy between us and the sounder Schoolmen as to the beginning of justification. The following sentence is added in the French: "Il est bien vray que le poure monde a esté seduit jusques la, de penser que l'homme se preparast de soy-mesme pour estre justifié de Dieu: et que ce blaspheme a regné communement tant en predications qu'aux escoles; comme encore aujourdhui il est soustenue de ceux qui veulent maintenir toutes les abominations de la Papauté." It is very true that the poor world has until now been led astray into thinking that man could of himself prepare to be justified by God, and that this blasphemy has commonly reigned both in sermons and in the schools, as it is still asserted at the present day by those who would maintain all the abominations of the Papacy.

12¶

The evasions by which the Schoolmen try to escape here will not set them free. They say that good works are not of such intrinsic worth as to be sufficient to procure justification, but that they have this effect because of accepting grace. Then, because they are forced to confess that the righteousness of works is always imperfect here, they grant that as long as we are in this life we need the forgiveness of sins to make up for the deficiency of our works, but they say that the faults committed are compensated by works of supererogation. I answer that the grace they call accepting is nothing else than the free goodness with which the Father embraces us in Christ, when he clothes us with the innocence of Christ and accepts it as ours, so that in view of it he regards us as holy, pure, and innocent. For the righteousness of Christ (since it alone is perfect, so it alone can stand the scrutiny of God) must be presented for us, and, as a surety, must represent us in judgment. Furnished with this righteousness, we continually obtain the forgiveness of sins through faith. Our imperfection and impurity, covered with this purity, are not imputed, but are, as it were, buried, so as not to come into judgment until the hour arrives when, the old man being destroyed and plainly extinguished in us, the divine goodness will receive us into blessed peace with the new Adam, there to await the day of the Lord, on which, being clothed with incorruptible bodies, we shall be carried up to the glory of the heavenly kingdom.

13¶

If these things are so, it is certain that our works cannot in themselves make us agreeable and acceptable to God, and cannot even please God, except insofar as we please him and obtain forgiveness of sins by being covered with the righteousness of Christ. God has not promised life as the reward of certain works, but only declares, "which if a man do, he shall live in them" (Lev. 18:5), while pronouncing the well-known curse against all who do not continue in all things written in the book of the law to do them. In this way the fiction of a partial righteousness is completely refuted, since the only righteousness acknowledged in heaven is the perfect keeping of the law. Their dogma of compensation by means of works of supererogation is no more solid. For must they not always return to the proposition already disproved, namely, that he who keeps the law in part is to that extent justified by works? This, which no man of sound judgment will grant them, they are not ashamed to take for granted. Since the Lord has so often declared that he recognizes no justification by works unless they are works by which the law is perfectly fulfilled, how perverse it is, while we lack such works, to try to secure for ourselves some ground of glorying, that is, not to yield it entirely to God, by boasting of some kind of fragments of works and trying to make up the deficiency by other satisfactions! Satisfactions have already been so completely disposed of that we ought never again even to dream of them. Here all I say is that those who trifle with sin in this way do not at all consider how detestable it is in the sight of God; if they did, they would certainly understand that all the righteousness of men gathered into one heap would not be enough to compensate for a single sin. For we see that by one sin man was so cast off and forsaken by God that he lost at the same time all power of recovering salvation. He was therefore deprived of the power of making satisfaction. Those who flatter themselves with this idea will never satisfy God, who cannot possibly accept or be pleased with anything that comes from his enemies. But all to whom he imputes sin are enemies, and therefore our sins must be covered and forgiven before the Lord has regard to any of our works. From this it follows that the forgiveness of sins is free, and this forgiveness is wickedly insulted by those who bring in the idea of satisfaction. Let us, therefore, following the example of the apostle, "forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before," "press toward the mark for the prize of the high calling of God in Jesus Christ" (Phil. 3:13, 14).

14¶

How can boasting in works of supererogation agree with the command given to us: "When ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do" (Luke 17:10)? To say or speak in the presence of God is not to pretend or lie, but to declare what we hold as certain. Our Lord, therefore, commands us sincerely to feel and consider within ourselves that we do not perform free and voluntary duties, but pay him service that is owed. And rightly so. For the obligations of service under which we lie are so numerous that we could not discharge them even if all our thoughts and members were devoted to keeping the law; and therefore, when he says, "When ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you," it is just as if he had said that all the righteousness of men would not amount to a single one of these things. Seeing, then, that everyone is very far from that goal, how can we presume to boast of having accumulated more than is due? It cannot be objected that a person, though falling short in some measure of what is necessary, may yet in intention go beyond what is necessary. For it must always be held that in whatever pertains to the worship of God or to love, nothing can ever be thought of that is not included under the law. But if it is part of the law, let us not boast of voluntary generosity in matters of necessary obligation.

15¶

On this subject they constantly appeal to the boast of Paul that among the Corinthians he voluntarily gave up a right which he might have exercised if he had chosen otherwise (1 Cor. 9:15); thus he not only paid what he owed them in duty, but freely bestowed on them more than duty required. They ought to have noticed the reason expressed there, that his aim was to avoid giving offense to the weak. For wicked and deceitful workers used this pretense of kindness to win favor for their pernicious dogmas and to stir up hatred against the gospel, so that Paul had either to endanger the doctrine of Christ or to thwart their schemes. Now, if it is a matter of indifference to a Christian man whether or not he causes offense when it is in his power to avoid it, then I admit that the apostle performed a work of supererogation for his Master; but if what he did was rightly required of a prudent minister of the gospel, then I say he did what he was bound to do. In short, even where no such reason appears, the saying of Chrysostom is always true, that everything we have is held on the same terms as the private property of slaves: it is always owed to our Master. Christ does not conceal this in the parable, for he asks concerning the master who, on returning from his labor, requires his servant to gird himself and serve him, "Does he thank that servant because he did the things that were commanded him? I trow not" (Luke 17:9). But perhaps the servant was more industrious than the master would have ventured to demand. Suppose so: still he did nothing to which his condition as a servant did not bind him, because his utmost ability belongs to his master. I say nothing of the kind of supererogations on which these men would pride themselves before God. They are trifles which he never commanded, which he does not approve, and which he will not accept when they come to give their account. The only sense in which we admit works of supererogation is that expressed by the prophet when he says, "Who has required this at your hand?" (Isaiah 1:12). But let them remember what is said of them elsewhere: "Wherefore do ye spend money for that which is not bread? and your labour for that which satisfieth not?" (Isaiah 55:2). It is indeed an easy matter for these idle Rabbis to carry on such discussions sitting in their soft chairs in the shade, but when the Supreme Judge sits on his tribunal, all these blustering dogmas will have to disappear. French, "Tout ce qu'ils auront determiné ne profitera gueres, ains s'evanouisra comme fumee" — all their decisions will scarcely avail them, but will vanish like smoke.

16¶

In this matter the minds of men must be especially guarded against two pestilent dogmas, namely, against putting any confidence in the righteousness of works and against ascribing any glory to them. The Scriptures consistently turn us away from all such confidence when they declare that our righteousness is offensive in the sight of God unless it takes a sweet fragrance from the purity of Christ, and that it can have no other effect than to provoke divine vengeance unless it is upheld by his forbearing mercy. Accordingly, the only thing they leave us is to plead with our Judge in that confession of David: "Enter not into judgment with thy servant: for in thy sight shall no living be justified" (Psalm 143:2). And when Job says, "If I be wicked, woe unto me: and if I be righteous, yet will I not lift up my head" (Job 10:15), although he refers to that spotless righteousness of God before which even angels are not clean, he nevertheless shows that when mortals are brought to the bar of God, all they can do is stand speechless. He does not merely mean that he would rather give way of his own accord than risk a contest with the divine severity, but that he was not aware of possessing any righteousness that would not fall the very first moment it was brought into the presence of God. When confidence is banished, all glorying must necessarily cease. For who can ascribe any merit of righteousness to works which, instead of giving confidence, only make us tremble in the presence of God? We must therefore come to what Isaiah invites us to: "In the Lord shall all the seed of Israel be justified, and shall glory" (Isaiah 45:25); for it is most true, as he says elsewhere, that we are "the planting of the Lord, that he might be glorified" (Isaiah 61:3). Our soul, therefore, will never be rightly purified until it ceases to have any confidence or to feel any exultation in works. Foolish men are puffed up to this false and lying confidence by the mistaken idea that the cause of their salvation lies in works.

17¶

But if we attend to the four kinds of causes which philosophers set before us in regard to effects, we will find that not one of them applies to works as a cause of salvation. Scripture consistently proclaims that the efficient cause of our eternal salvation is the mercy and free love of the heavenly Father toward us; that the material cause is Christ, with the obedience by which he purchased righteousness for us; and what can the formal or instrumental cause be but faith? John includes the three in one sentence when he says, "God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life" (John 3:16). The apostle, moreover, declares that the final cause is the demonstration of the divine righteousness and the praise of his goodness. In the same place he distinctly mentions the other three causes as well, for he speaks to the Romans in this way: "All have sinned, and come short of the glory of God, being justified freely by his grace" (Rom. 3:23, 24). Here you have the head and first source: God has embraced us with free mercy. The next words are, "through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus"; this is, as it were, the material cause by which righteousness is procured for us. "Whom God has set forth to be a propitiation through faith." Faith is thus the instrumental cause by which righteousness is applied to us. Lastly, he adds the final cause when he says, "To declare at this time his righteousness; that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus." And to show in passing that this righteousness consists in reconciliation, he says that Christ was "set forth to be a propitiation." So also, in the Epistle to the Ephesians, he tells us that we are received into the favor of God by mere mercy; that this is done through the intervention of Christ; that it is laid hold of by faith; and that the end of all is that the glory of the divine goodness may be fully displayed. When we see that all the parts of our salvation thus lie outside ourselves, what ground can we have for glorying or trusting in our works? Neither as to the efficient cause nor as to the final cause can the most sworn enemies of divine grace raise any controversy with us, unless they would renounce the whole of Scripture. As to the material or formal cause, they put a gloss on it, as if they held that our works share the merit with faith and the righteousness of Christ. But here too Scripture protests, simply affirming that Christ is both righteousness and life, and that the blessing of justification is possessed by faith alone.

18¶

When the saints repeatedly strengthen and console themselves by remembering their innocence and integrity, and sometimes do not even refrain from proclaiming them, this is done in two ways: either because, comparing their good cause with the bad cause of the ungodly, they feel sure of victory, not so much from any commendation of their own righteousness as from the just and deserved condemnation of their adversaries; or because, examining themselves before God even without any comparison with others, the purity of their conscience gives them some comfort and security. The former reason will be considered later (chapter 17, section 14, and chapter 20, section 10); let us now briefly show, in regard to the latter, how it agrees with what we said above, that we can have no confidence in works before the bar of God and cannot glory in any opinion of their worth. The agreement lies here: when the question concerns the constitution and foundation of salvation, believers, paying no regard to works, fix their eyes on the goodness of God alone. Nor do they turn to it only at the outset, as to the beginning of blessedness, but they rest in it as its completion. The conscience, being thus founded, built up, and established, is further established by the consideration of works, inasmuch as they are proofs that God dwells and reigns in us. Since, then, this confidence in works has no place unless you have first fixed your whole confidence on the mercy of God, it should not seem contrary to that on which it depends. Therefore, when we exclude confidence in works, we mean only that the Christian mind must not turn back to the merit of works as an aid to salvation, but must rest entirely on the free promise of justification. But we forbid no believer to confirm and support this faith by the signs of the divine favor toward him. For if, when we call to mind the gifts which God has bestowed on us, they are like rays of the divine countenance, by which we are enabled to behold the highest light of his goodness, much more is this true of the gift of good works, which shows that we have received the Spirit of adoption.

19¶

When believers, therefore, feel their faith strengthened by a consciousness of integrity and are moved to rejoice, it is simply because the fruits of their calling convince them that the Lord has admitted them to a place among his children. Accordingly, when Solomon says, "In the fear of the Lord is strong confidence" (Prov. 14:26), and when the saints sometimes beseech the Lord to hear them because they walked before his face in simplicity and integrity (Gen. 24:10; 2 Kings 20:3), these expressions do not apply to laying the foundation of a firm conscience, but have force only when taken a posteriori. Latin, "a posteriori"; French, "comme enseigne de la vocation de Dieu" — as a sign of the calling of God.

20¶

We now see that believers have no such confidence in works as to ascribe any merit to them (since they regard them only as divine gifts, in which they recognize his goodness, and as signs of calling, in which they discern their election); nor such confidence as to detract in any respect from the free righteousness of Christ, since it depends on this and cannot stand without it. Augustine expresses the same thing briefly but elegantly when he says, "I do not say to the Lord, Despise not the works of my hands; I have sought the Lord with my hands, and have not been deceived. But I do not commend the works of my hands, for I fear that when you examine them you will find more faults than merits. This only I say, this I ask, this I desire: Despise not the works of your hands. See in me your work, not mine. If you see mine, you condemn; if you see your own, you crown. Whatever good works I have are from you" (Augustine, on Psalm 137). He gives two reasons for not venturing to boast of his works before God: first, that if he has any good works, he sees nothing of his own in them; and second, that these works are overwhelmed by a multitude of sins. Hence the conscience draws from them more fear and alarm than security. Therefore the only way in which he wishes God to look on any work he may have done rightly is that God may see in it the grace of his calling and may perfect the work he has begun.

21¶

Moreover, when Scripture indicates that the good works of believers are causes why the Lord does them good, we must still understand this so as to hold unshaken what has been said before, namely, that the efficient cause of our salvation is placed in the love of God the Father; the material cause in the obedience of the Son; the instrumental cause in the illumination of the Spirit, that is, in faith; and the final cause in the praise of the divine goodness. In this, however, nothing prevents the Lord from embracing works as inferior causes. But how so? In this way: those whom in mercy he has destined for the inheritance of eternal life, he brings into possession of it, in his ordinary administration, by means of good works. What comes first in the order of administration is called the cause of what follows. For this reason he sometimes makes eternal life a consequence of works; not because it is to be ascribed to them, but because those whom he has elected he justifies, that he may at length glorify them (Rom. 8:30); he makes the prior grace a kind of cause, because it is a kind of step to what follows. But whenever the true cause is to be assigned, he commands us not to take refuge in works, but to keep our thoughts entirely fixed on the mercy of God: "The wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life" (Rom. 6:23). Why, when he contrasts life with death, does he not also contrast righteousness with sin? Why, when he sets down sin as the cause of death, does he not also set down righteousness as the cause of life? The antithesis, which would otherwise be complete, is somewhat marred by this variation; but the apostle used the comparison to express the fact that death is due to the deserts of men, but that life was laid up solely in the mercy of God. In short, these expressions mark the order rather than the cause. French, "Brief, en toutes ces facons de parler, ou il est fait mention de bonnes œuvres, il n'est pas question de la cause pourquoy Dieu fait bien aux siens, mais seulement de l'ordre qu'il y tient" — in short, in all those forms of expression in which good works are mentioned, there is no question of the cause why God does good to his people, but only of the order which he observes in it.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Even the regenerate, Calvin insists, produce no work that, weighed in God's strict balance, escapes condemnation; the believer's best deed carries some taint of the flesh, and one sin is enough to efface a lifetime of supposed righteousness. So our works, real fruit of the indwelling Spirit and dear to the Father, are never the cause of our salvation but only the order in which mercy leads us to glory — signs of our calling, never the ground of our standing. Lay hold of this in the home, where it is easy to mistake diligence for desert. Disciple your children to do good works heartily and to glory in none of them, to see in their obedience the grace of God's calling rather than a claim upon him. In a generation that keeps score and demands its reward, let the believer's settled posture be that of the unprofitable servant who, having done all, knows it was God who worked it — and rests his whole hope outside himself, in Christ alone.
