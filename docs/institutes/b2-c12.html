Christ, to Perform the Office of Mediator, Behoved to Become Man

The two divisions of this chapter are, I. The reasons why our Mediator behoved to be very God, and to become man, sec. 1–3. II. Disposal of various objections by some fanatics, and especially by Osiander, to the orthodox doctrine concerning the Mediator, sec. 4–7.

Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. Necessary, not absolutely, but by divine decree, that the Mediator should be God, and become man. Neither man nor angel, though pure, could have sufficed. The Son of God behoved to come down. Man in innocence could not penetrate to God without a Mediator, much less could he after the fall.
  2. 2. A second reason why the Mediator behoved to be God and man—viz. that he had to convert those who were heirs of hell into children of God.
  3. 3. Third reason, that in our flesh he might yield a perfect obedience, satisfy the divine justice, and pay the penalty of sin. Fourth reason, regarding the consolation and confirmation of the whole Church.
  4. 4. First objection against the orthodox doctrine: Answer to it. Conformation from the sacrifices of the Law, the testimony of the Prophets, Apostles, Evangelists, and even Christ himself.
  5. 5. Second objection: Answer: Answer confirmed. Third objection: Answer. Fourth objection by Osiander: Answer.
  6. 6. Fifth objection, forming the basis of Osiander’s errors on this subject: Answer. Nature of the divine image in Adam. Christ the head of angels and men.
  7. 7. Sixth objection: Answer. Seventh objection: Answer. Eighth objection: Answer. Ninth objection: Answer. Tenth objection: Answer. Eleventh objection: Answer. Twelfth objection: Answer. The sum of the doctrine.
1¶

It was of the deepest concern to us that the one who was to be our Mediator should be true God and true man. If we ask about the necessity of this, it was not what is commonly called a simple or absolute necessity, but one that flowed from the divine decree on which the salvation of man depended. Our most merciful Father determined what was best for us. Since our iniquities, like a cloud coming between him and us, had utterly cut us off from the kingdom of heaven, no one but a person who could reach to him could be the means of restoring peace. But who could reach to him in this way? Could any of the sons of Adam? All of them, together with their parents, shuddered at the sight of God. Could any of the angels? They themselves needed a head, by connection with whom they might cleave to their God entirely and inseparably. What then? The case was certainly hopeless if the Godhead itself did not come down to us, since it was impossible for us to go up. So the Son of God had to become our Emmanuel, God with us; and in such a way that by a mutual union his divinity and our nature might be joined together. Otherwise the nearness would not have been close enough, nor the kinship strong enough, to give us hope that God would dwell with us, so great was the opposition between our pollution and the spotless purity of God. Even if man had remained free from every taint, his condition was too lowly for him to come through to God without a Mediator. What, then, must it have been when, by a fatal ruin, he was plunged into death and hell, defiled by so many stains, made loathsome by corruption, and, in short, overwhelmed with every curse? It is not without reason, therefore, that Paul, when he would set forth Christ as the Mediator, expressly declares him to be man. There is, he says, "one Mediator between God and man, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5). He might have called him God; or at least, if he left out calling him God, he might also have left out calling him man. But because the Spirit, speaking by his mouth, knew our weakness, he fittingly provides for it by the most suitable remedy, setting the Son of God before us in a familiar way as one of ourselves. So that no one may be perplexed about where to seek the Mediator, or by what means to reach him, the Spirit, by calling him man, reminds us that he is near, indeed right next to us, since he is our flesh. And indeed he indicates the same thing in another place, where he explains at greater length that he is not a high priest who "cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities; but was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15).

2¶

This will become still clearer if we consider that the work the Mediator had to perform was of no ordinary kind: he was to restore us to the divine favor, so as to make us sons of God instead of sons of men, and heirs of a heavenly kingdom instead of heirs of hell. Who could do this unless the Son of God should also become the Son of man, and so take what is ours as to transfer to us what is his, making what is his by nature become ours by grace? Relying on this pledge, we trust that we are the sons of God, because the natural Son of God took to himself a body of our body, flesh of our flesh, bones of our bones, so that he might be one with us. He did not refuse to take what was proper to us, so that he might in turn extend to us what was properly his own, and so might be, in common with us, both Son of God and Son of man. Hence that holy brotherhood which he commends with his own lips when he says, "I ascend to my Father, and your Father, to my God, and your God" (John 20:17). In this way we have a sure inheritance in the heavenly kingdom, because the only Son of God, to whom it wholly belonged, has adopted us as his brothers; and if brothers, then partners with him in the inheritance (Romans 8:17). Moreover, it was especially necessary for this reason also that the one who was to be our Redeemer should be true God and man. It was his task to swallow up death: who could do that but Life itself? It was his task to conquer sin: who could do that but Righteousness itself? It was his task to put to flight the powers of the air and of the world: who could do that but a mighty power superior to both? But who possesses life and righteousness, and the dominion and government of heaven, but God alone? Therefore God, in his infinite mercy, having determined to redeem us, became himself our Redeemer in the person of his only begotten Son.

3¶

Another principal part of our reconciliation with God was this: that man, who had lost himself by his disobedience, should by way of remedy set obedience against it, satisfy the justice of God, and pay the penalty of sin. Therefore our Lord came forth as true man, took on the person of Adam, and assumed his name, so that he might obey the Father in Adam's place; so that he might present our flesh as the price of satisfaction to the just judgment of God, and in the same flesh pay the penalty we had incurred. Finally, since as God alone he could not suffer, and as man alone he could not overcome death, he united the human nature with the divine, so that he might subject the weakness of the one to death as an expiation for sin, and by the power of the other, carrying on a struggle with death, might win the victory for us. Those, therefore, who rob Christ of either his divinity or his humanity either detract from his majesty and glory or obscure his goodness. On the other hand, they do no less injury to men, undermining and overthrowing their faith, which cannot stand unless it rests on this foundation. Moreover, the expected Redeemer was that son of Abraham and David whom God had promised in the Law and in the Prophets. Here believers have a further advantage. Tracing his origin in an orderly line back to David and Abraham, they recognize him more distinctly as the Messiah celebrated by so many oracles. But special attention must be paid to what I have just explained: namely, that a common nature is the pledge of our union with the Son of God; that, clothed with our flesh, he waged war to the death against sin so that he might be our triumphant conqueror; and that the flesh he received from us he offered in sacrifice, so that by making expiation he might wipe away our guilt and appease the just anger of his Father.

4¶

Whoever considers these things with due attention will easily set aside idle speculations, which attract flighty minds and lovers of novelty. One speculation of this kind is that Christ would still have become man even if there had been no need for his intervention to redeem the human race. I grant that in the first ordering of creation, while the state of nature was whole, he was appointed head of angels and men; for which reason Paul calls him "the first-born of every creature" (Colossians 1:15). But since the whole of Scripture proclaims that he was clothed with flesh in order to become a Redeemer, it is presumptuous to imagine any other cause or end. We know well why Christ was first promised: namely, that he might renew a fallen world and come to the aid of lost man. Hence under the Law he was foreshadowed by sacrifices, to inspire believers with the hope that God would be favorable to them once he had been reconciled by the expiation of their sins. Since from the earliest age, even before the Law was published, there was never any promise of a Mediator without blood, we rightly infer that he was appointed in the eternal counsel of God to cleanse the pollution of man, the shedding of blood being the sign of expiation. So too the prophets, in speaking of him, foretold that he would be the Mediator between God and man. It is enough to point to the very remarkable prophecy of Isaiah (Isaiah 53:4, 5), in which he foretells that he was "smitten for our iniquities"; that "the chastisement of our peace was upon him"; that as a priest "he was made an offering for sin"; "that by his stripes we are healed"; that since all "like lost sheep have gone astray," "it pleased the Lord to bruise him, and put him to grief," so that he might "bear our iniquities." Having heard that Christ was divinely appointed to bring relief to miserable sinners, whoever leaps over these limits gives too much rein to a foolish curiosity.

Then, when he actually appeared, he declared the cause of his coming to be that by appeasing God he might bring us from death to life. The testimony of the Apostles concerning him was to the same effect (John 1:9; 10:14). So John, before teaching that the Word was made flesh, tells of the fall of man. But above all, let us listen to our Savior himself when he speaks of his office: "God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." Again, "The hour is coming, and now is, when the dead shall hear the voice of the Son of God: and they that hear shall live." "I am the resurrection and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live." "The Son of man is come to save that which was lost." Again, "They that be whole need not a physician." John 3:16; 5:25; Matthew 18:11; 9:12.

Luke 24:46; John 10:17; 3:14; 12:27, 28.

5¶

Should anyone object that nothing in this prevents the same Christ who redeemed us when we were condemned from also showing his love to us, had we remained safe, by taking on our nature, the brief answer is this: when the Spirit declares that by the eternal decree of God the two things were joined together, namely, that Christ should be our Redeemer and at the same time a partaker of our nature, it is not lawful to inquire further. Whoever is itching to know something more, not content with the unchangeable ordinance of God, shows also that he is not content even with that Christ who has been given to us as the price of redemption. And indeed Paul not only declares for what end he was sent, but, rising to the sublime mystery of predestination, timely restrains all the wantonness and itching curiosity of the human mind: "He has chosen us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and without blame before him in love: having predestinated us unto the adoption of children by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will, to the praise of the glory of his grace, wherein he has made us accepted in the Beloved: In whom we have redemption through his blood" (Ephesians 1:4–7). Here, certainly, the fall of Adam is not presupposed as earlier in point of time; rather, our attention is directed to what God determined beforehand, before all ages, when he was pleased to provide a cure for the misery of the human race. If, again, it is objected that this counsel of God depended on the fall of man, which he foresaw, it is enough and more for me to reply that those who set out to inquire into, or desire to know, more of Christ than God predestined by his secret decree are presuming with impious audacity to invent a new Christ. Paul, when he speaks of the proper office of Christ, rightly prays for the Ephesians that God would strengthen them "by his Spirit in the inner man," that they might "be able to comprehend with all saints what is the breadth and length, and depth and height; and to know the love of Christ which passeth knowledge" (Ephesians 3:16, 18). It is as if he deliberately meant to set barriers around our minds and keep them from turning aside one iota from the gift of reconciliation whenever Christ is mentioned. Therefore, since it is, as Paul declares, "a faithful saying, and worthy of all acceptation, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners" (1 Timothy 1:15), I gladly rest in it. And since the same Apostle elsewhere declares that the grace now made manifest by the Gospel "was given us in Christ Jesus before the world began" (2 Timothy 1:9), I am resolved to hold to it firmly even to the end. This restraint is unjustly reviled by Osiander, who in our own day has unhappily stirred up again this question, which a few had raised in former times. He charges with overconfidence those who deny that the Son of God would have appeared in the flesh if Adam had not fallen, on the ground that this notion is not rejected by any passage of Scripture. As if Paul did not put a curb on perverse curiosity when, after speaking of the redemption obtained by Christ, he bids us "avoid foolish questions" (Titus 3:9). Some have gone to such madness, in their perverse eagerness to appear clever, that they have raised the question whether the Son of God might not have taken on the nature of an ass. This blasphemy, at which all godly minds rightly shudder with loathing, Osiander excuses on the pretext that it is nowhere expressly refuted in Scripture. As if Paul, when he counted nothing valuable or worth knowing "save Jesus Christ and him crucified" (1 Corinthians 2:2), were admitting that the author of salvation is an ass! He who elsewhere declares that Christ was appointed by the eternal counsel of the Father "head over all things to the church" would never have acknowledged another to whom no office of redemption had been assigned.

6¶

The principle on which Osiander builds is altogether frivolous. He insists that man was created in the image of God because he was formed on the pattern of the future Messiah, in order to resemble him whom the Father had already determined to clothe with flesh. From this he infers that even if Adam had never fallen from his first and pure origin, Christ would still have been man. All men of sound judgment see at once how silly and distorted this view is. Yet he thinks he was the first to see what the image of God was: namely, that not only did the divine glory shine forth in the excellent gifts with which Adam was adorned, but God dwelt in him essentially. But while I grant that Adam bore the image of God insofar as he was united to God (this being the true and highest perfection of dignity), I still maintain that the likeness of God is to be sought only in those marks of superiority by which God distinguished Adam above the other animals. And I likewise acknowledge, with common consent, that Christ was even then the image of God, and that accordingly whatever excellence was engraved on Adam had its origin in this, that by means of the only begotten Son he drew near to the glory of his Maker. Man, therefore, was created in the image of God (Genesis 1:27), and in him the Creator was pleased to behold his own glory as in a mirror. To this degree of honor he was raised by the kindness of the only begotten Son. But I add that, as the Son was the common head of both men and angels, so the dignity conferred on man belonged to the angels also. For when we hear them called the sons of God (Psalm 82:6), it would be absurd to deny that they were endowed with some quality in which they resembled the Father. But if he was pleased to have his glory represented in men and angels, and made manifest in both natures, it is ignorant trifling on Osiander's part to say that angels were ranked below men because they did not bear the image of Christ. They could not continually enjoy the immediate presence of God if they were not like him; nor does Paul teach (Colossians 3:10) that men are renewed in the image of God in any other way than by being joined with the angels, so that they may be united together under one head. In short, if we believe Christ, our happiness will be made complete when we have been received into heaven and made like the angels. But if Osiander is entitled to infer that the primary pattern of the image of God was in the man Christ, then on the same ground anyone might maintain that Christ had to partake of the angelic nature, since angels also possess the image of God.

7¶

Osiander has no reason to fear that God would be found a liar if the decree to make the Son incarnate had not been fixed unchangeably in his mind beforehand. Even if Adam had not lost his integrity, he would have been like God together with the angels; and yet it would not therefore have been necessary for the Son of God to become either a man or an angel. In vain does he entertain the absurd fear that, unless it had been determined by the unchangeable counsel of God before man was created that Christ should be born, not as the Redeemer but as the first man, Christ might lose his precedence, since he would then have been born only because of an accidental circumstance, namely, to restore the lost race of man, and in this way would have been created in the image of Adam. For why should he be alarmed at what Scripture plainly teaches, that "he was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15)? Hence Luke also does not hesitate to count him in his genealogy as a son of Adam (Luke 3:38). I should like to know why Paul calls Christ the second Adam (1 Corinthians 15:47), unless it is because a human condition was decreed for him for the purpose of raising up the ruined descendants of Adam. For if, in point of order, that condition came before creation, he ought to have been called the first Adam. Osiander confidently asserts that because Christ was foreknown as man in the purpose of God, men were formed after him as their model. But Paul, by calling him the second Adam, places that revolt which made it necessary to restore nature to its original condition between its first formation and the restoration we obtain through Christ. From this it follows that it was this restoration which caused the Son of God to be born and so to become man. Moreover, Osiander argues badly and absurdly that as long as Adam kept his integrity he would have been the image of himself and not of Christ. I maintain, on the contrary, that even if the Son of God had never become incarnate, the image of God would nevertheless have been plainly visible in Adam, both in his body and in his soul; and in the rays of this image it always appeared that Christ was truly the head and had the preeminence in all things. In this way we dispose of the empty sophism Osiander puts forward, that the angels would have been without this head had God not purposed to clothe his Son with flesh even apart from the sin of Adam. He rashly assumes what no reasonable person will grant: that Christ could have had no supremacy over the angels, such that they might enjoy him as their prince, except insofar as he was man. But it is easy to infer from the words of Paul (Colossians 1:15) that, insofar as he is the eternal Word of God, he is the first-born of every creature, not because he is created or is to be counted among the creatures, but because the whole structure of the world, such as it was from the beginning when it was adorned with exquisite beauty, had no other beginning; and then, insofar as he was made man, he is the first-born from the dead. For in one short passage (Colossians 1:16–18) the Apostle draws our attention to both points: that by the Son all things were created, so that he has dominion over angels; and that he became man in order that he might begin to be a Redeemer. Out of the same ignorance Osiander says that men would not have had Christ as their king unless he had been a man. As if the kingdom of God could not have been established by his eternal Son, though not clothed with human flesh, holding the supremacy while angels and men were gathered together to share in his heavenly life and glory! But he is always deceived, or deceives himself, by this false principle: that the church would have been akefalon, without a head, had Christ not appeared in the flesh. Just as the angels enjoyed him as their head, could he not by his divine power preside over men as well, and by the secret power of his Spirit give them life and cherish them as his body, until they were gathered into heaven to enjoy the same life with the angels? Osiander regards the absurdities I have been refuting as infallible oracles. Taking an intoxicating delight in his own speculations, he is in the habit of drawing ridiculous schemes out of nothing. He then says that he has a much stronger passage to bring forward, namely, the prophecy of Adam, who, when the woman was brought to him, said, "This is now bone of my bone, and flesh of my flesh" (Genesis 2:23). But how does he prove it to be a prophecy? Because in Matthew Christ attributes the same words to God! As if everything God has spoken through man contained a prophecy. On the same principle, since the law came from God, let Osiander find a prophecy in every precept. Add to this that our Savior's interpretation would have been harsh and earthbound had he confined himself to the literal meaning. He was not referring to the mystical union with which he has honored the Church, but only to faithfulness in marriage, and he states that the reason God declared man and wife to be one flesh was to keep anyone from breaking that indissoluble bond by divorce. If this simple meaning is too lowly for Osiander, let him find fault with Christ for not leading his disciples to the hidden sense by interpreting his Father's words with more subtlety. Paul gives no support to Osiander's dream when, after saying that "we are members of his body, of his flesh, and of his bones," he immediately adds, "This is a great mystery" (Ephesians 5:30–32). For he did not mean to refer to the sense in which Adam used the words, but sets forth, under the figure and likeness of marriage, the sacred union that makes us one with Christ. His words have this meaning: reminding us that he is speaking of Christ and the Church, he distinguishes, by way of correction, between the marriage bond and the spiritual union of Christ with his Church. And so this subtlety vanishes at once. I consider it unnecessary to discuss similar absurdities, for from this very brief refutation the emptiness of them all will be exposed. Abundantly sufficient for the solid nourishment of the children of God is this sober truth: that "when the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth his Son, made of a woman, made under the law, to redeem them who were under the law" (Galatians 4:4, 5).

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin sets out why our Mediator must be both very God and true man — that he might bridge the gulf sin had opened, render in our flesh the perfect obedience we owed, bear in our nature the wrath we had earned, and join God and man in one person reconciled. This is no abstract puzzle; it is the hinge of all our comfort. In 2026, when so much religion shrinks Christ to a moral teacher or inflates him into a symbol, the church must hold fast to the wonder Calvin guards — God himself stooped into our weakness, took bone of our bone, and obeyed where we rebelled. For the believer, this means your salvation does not rest on the fragile thread of your own striving but on a Mediator strong enough, because divine, and near enough, because human, to save completely. Teach your children this incarnate Lord, neither half a man nor a costumed deity but the God-man who could die for sinners and rise for them. Steadfastness has a foundation: the One who became what we are so that he might bring us where he is.
