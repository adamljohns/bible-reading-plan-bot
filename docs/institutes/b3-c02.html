1¶

All these things will be easily understood once we have given a clearer definition of faith, so as to enable readers to grasp its nature and power. Here it is important to recall what was taught earlier. First, since God by his Law prescribes what we ought to do, failure in any one respect subjects us to the dreadful judgment of eternal death which it pronounces. Secondly, because it is not only difficult but altogether beyond our strength and ability to fulfill the demands of the Law, if we look only to ourselves and consider what is due to our merits, no ground of hope remains, but we lie forsaken by God under eternal death. Thirdly, there is only one way of deliverance which can rescue us from this miserable calamity, namely, when Christ the Redeemer appears, by whose hand our heavenly Father, out of his infinite goodness and mercy, has been pleased to help us, if we embrace this mercy with true faith and rest in it with firm hope. It is now fitting to consider the nature of this faith, by means of which those who are adopted into the family of God obtain possession of the heavenly kingdom. For the accomplishment of so great an end, it is obvious that no mere opinion or persuasion is adequate. And all the more care and diligence is needed in discussing the true nature of faith because of the pernicious delusions under which many in the present day labor with regard to it. Great numbers, on hearing the term, think that nothing more is meant than a certain common assent to the Gospel history. Indeed, when the subject of faith is discussed in the Schools, by simply representing God as its object, they by empty speculation, as we have said elsewhere (Book 2, chapter 6, section 4), hurry wretched souls away from the right mark instead of directing them to it. For seeing that God dwells in light that is inaccessible, Christ must intervene. Hence he calls himself "the light of the world," and in another passage, "the way, the truth, and the life." No one comes to the Father (who is the fountain of life) except by him; for "no man knows who the Father is but the Son, and he to whom the Son will reveal him." For this reason Paul declares, "I count all things as loss for the excellency of the knowledge of Christ Jesus my Lord." In the twentieth chapter of Acts he states that he preached "faith toward our Lord Jesus Christ"; and in another passage he introduces Christ as addressing him thus: "I have appeared unto you for this purpose, to make you a minister and a witness," "delivering you from the people, and from the Gentiles, unto whom now I send you," "that they may receive forgiveness of sins, and inheritance among them which are sanctified by faith that is in me." Paul further declares that in the person of Christ the glory of God is visibly manifested to us, or, what is the same thing, that we have "the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." 1 Timothy 6:16; John 8:12; 14:6; Luke 10:22; 1 Corinthians 2:2; Acts 20:21; 26:17, 18; 2 Corinthians 4:6.

3¶

I do not deny, indeed (so wrapped are we in ignorance), that very many things are now, and will continue to be, completely obscure to us until we lay aside this weight of flesh and come nearer to the presence of God. In such cases the fittest course is to suspend our judgment and resolve to maintain unity with the Church. But under this pretext to honor ignorance tempered with humility with the name of faith is most absurd. Faith consists in the knowledge of God and Christ (John 17:3), not in reverence for the Church. And we see what a labyrinth they have built out of this implicit faith: everything, sometimes even the most monstrous errors, is received by the ignorant as an oracle without any discernment, provided it is prescribed to them under the name of the Church. This thoughtless credulity, though it is the surest precipice to destruction, is nevertheless excused on the ground that it believes nothing definitely, but only with the added condition, "if such is the faith of the Church." Thus they pretend to find truth in error, light in darkness, true knowledge in ignorance. Not to dwell any longer on refuting these views, we simply advise the reader to compare them with ours. The clearness of the truth will itself furnish a sufficient refutation. For the question they raise is not whether there may be an implicit faith with many remnants of ignorance; rather, they maintain that persons living in, and even indulging, a stupid ignorance duly believe, provided that in regard to things unknown they assent to the authority and judgment of the Church: as if Scripture did not uniformly teach that understanding is joined with faith.

4¶

We grant, indeed, that so long as we are pilgrims in the world faith is implicit, not only because many things are as yet hidden from us, but because, wrapped in the mists of error, we do not attain to all things. The highest wisdom, even of the one who has attained the greatest perfection, is to go forward and endeavor, in a calm and teachable spirit, to make further progress. Hence Paul exhorts believers to wait for further illumination in any matter in which they differ from one another (Philippians 3:15). See Augustine, Letter 102: "Si propter eos solos Christus mortuus est, qui certa intelligentia possunt ista discernera, pæne frustra in ecclesia laboramus," etc. — If Christ died only for those who are able to discern these things with true understanding, our labor in the Church is almost in vain.

5¶

We may also call their faith implicit in the sense that it is properly nothing else than a preparation for faith. The Evangelists describe many as having believed, although they were only roused to wonder by the miracles and went no further than believing that Christ was the promised Messiah, without being imbued at all with Evangelical doctrine. The reverence which subdued them and made them willingly submit to Christ is honored with the name of faith, though it was nothing but its beginning. Thus the nobleman who believed in the promised cure of his son is said by the Evangelist, on returning home, to have believed again (John 4:53). That is, he had first received the words which fell from the lips of Christ as an oracular answer, and thereafter submitted to his authority and received his doctrine. Although it is to be observed that he was teachable and disposed to learn, yet the word "believed" in the former passage denotes a particular faith, and in the latter gives him a place among those disciples who had devoted themselves to Christ. Not unlike this is the example which John gives of the Samaritans, who believed the woman and eagerly hurried to Christ, but after they had heard him expressed themselves thus: "Now we believe, not because of your saying, for we have heard him ourselves, and know that this is indeed the Christ, the Savior of the world" (John 4:42). From these passages it is obvious that even those who are not yet imbued with the first principles, provided they are disposed to obey, are called believers: not properly, indeed, but inasmuch as God in his kindness is pleased to honor their pious feeling so highly. But this teachableness, joined with a desire for further progress, is very different from the gross ignorance in which those sluggishly indulge themselves who are content with the implicit faith of the Papists. If Paul severely condemns those who are "ever learning, and never able to come to the knowledge of the truth," how much more sharply ought those to be rebuked who openly make a show of knowing nothing?

6¶

The true knowledge of Christ consists in receiving him as he is offered by the Father, namely, as clothed with his Gospel. For as he is appointed to be the end of our faith, so we cannot move directly toward him except under the guidance of the Gospel. There, certainly, the treasures of grace are unfolded to us. If these remained shut, Christ would profit us little. Hence Paul makes faith the inseparable companion of doctrine in these words: "You have not so learned Christ; if so be that you have heard him, and have been taught by him, as the truth is in Jesus" (Ephesians 4:20, 21). Still, I do not confine faith to the Gospel in such a sense as to deny that enough was delivered to Moses and the Prophets to form a foundation of faith; but since the Gospel presents a fuller manifestation of Christ, Paul justly calls it the doctrine of faith (1 Timothy 4:6). For this reason also he says elsewhere that by the coming of faith the Law was abolished (Romans 10:4), including under that expression a new and unaccustomed mode of teaching, by which Christ, from the time of his appearing as the great Teacher, gave a fuller illustration of the Father's mercy and testified more surely of our salvation. But an easier and more fitting method will be to descend from the general to the particular. First, we must remember that there is an inseparable relation between faith and the word, and that these can no more be disconnected from each other than rays of light from the sun. Hence in Isaiah the Lord exclaims, "Hear, and your soul shall live" (Isaiah 4:3). And John points to this same fountain of faith in the following words: "These are written that you might believe" (John 20:31). The Psalmist also, exhorting the people to faith, says, "Today, if you will hear his voice" (Psalm 95:7), to hear being uniformly taken for to believe. In short, in Isaiah the Lord distinguishes the members of the Church from strangers by this mark: "All your children shall be taught of the Lord" (Isaiah 54:13); for if the benefit were given without distinction, why should he address his words only to a few? In keeping with this, the Evangelists uniformly use the terms believers and disciples as synonyms. This is done especially by Luke in several passages of Acts. He even applies the term disciple to a woman (Acts 9:36). Therefore, if faith turns aside in the least degree from the mark at which it ought to aim, it does not retain its nature, but becomes uncertain credulity and a vague wandering of the mind. The same word is the basis on which it rests and by which it is sustained. Turning aside from it, it falls. Take away the word, therefore, and no faith will remain. We are not discussing here whether the ministry of man is necessary for spreading the word of God by which faith is engendered (this will be considered elsewhere); but we say that the word itself, in whatever way it is conveyed to us, is a kind of mirror in which faith beholds God. In this, therefore, whether God uses the agency of man or works immediately by his own power, it is always by his word that he manifests himself to those whom he intends to draw to himself. Hence Paul describes faith as the obedience which is given to the Gospel (Romans 1:5); and writing to the Philippians, he commends them for the obedience of faith (Philippians 2:17). For faith includes not merely the knowledge that God is, but also, indeed chiefly, a perception of his will toward us. It concerns us to know not only what he is in himself, but also in what character he is pleased to manifest himself to us. We now see, therefore, that faith is the knowledge of the divine will toward us, as learned from his word. And its foundation is a prior persuasion of the truth of God. So long as your mind entertains any misgivings about the certainty of the word, its authority will be weak and doubtful, or rather it will have no authority at all. Nor is it enough to believe that God is true, and cannot lie or deceive, unless you feel firmly persuaded that every word which him is sacred, inviolable truth.

7¶

But since the heart of man is not brought to faith by every word of God, we must still consider what it is in the word that faith properly looks to. God's declaration to Adam was, "You shall surely die" (Genesis 2:17); and to Cain, "The voice of your brother's blood cries unto me from the ground" (Genesis 4:10); but these, so far from being fitted to establish faith, tend only to shake it. At the same time, we do not deny that it is the office of faith to assent to the truth of God whenever, whatever, and in whatever way he speaks: we are only asking what faith can find in the word of God to lean and rest upon. When conscience sees only wrath and indignation, how can it do anything but tremble and be afraid? And how can it avoid fleeing from the God whom it so dreads? But faith ought to seek God, not flee from him. It is evident, therefore, that we have not yet obtained a full definition of faith, since it is impossible to give that name to every kind of knowledge of the divine will. Shall we, then, in place of "will," which is often the messenger of bad news and the herald of terror, substitute the benevolence or mercy of God? In this way, no doubt, we come nearer to the nature of faith. For we are drawn to seek God when we are told that our safety is stored up in him; and we are confirmed in this when he declares that he cares for and takes an interest in our welfare. Hence there is need of the gracious promise, in which he testifies that he is a propitious Father; since there is no other way in which we can approach him, the promise being the only thing on which the heart of man can recline. For this reason the two things, mercy and truth, are uniformly joined together in the Psalms as being mutually connected. For it would be of no use to us to know that God is true, if he did not in mercy draw us to himself; nor could we of ourselves embrace his mercy if he did not expressly offer it. "I have declared your faithfulness and your salvation: I have not concealed your loving-kindness and your truth. Withhold not your tender mercies from me, O Lord: let your loving-kindness and your truth continually preserve me" (Psalm 40:10, 11). "Your mercy, O Lord, is in the heavens; and your faithfulness reaches unto the clouds" (Psalm 36:5). "All the paths of the Lord are mercy and truth unto such as keep his covenant and his testimonies" (Psalm 25:10). "His merciful kindness is great toward us: and the truth of the Lord endures for ever" (Psalm 117:2). "I will praise your name for your loving-kindness and your truth" (Psalm 138:2). I need not quote what is said in the Prophets to the effect that God is merciful and faithful in his promises. It would be presumptuous for us to hold that God is propitious to us, if we did not have his own testimony, and if he did not go before us with his invitation, which leaves no doubt or uncertainty about his will. It has already been seen that Christ is the only pledge of love, for without him all things, both above and below, speak of hatred and wrath. We have also seen that, since the knowledge of the divine goodness cannot be of much importance unless it leads us to trust in it, we must exclude a knowledge mingled with doubt, a knowledge which, so far from being firm, is continually wavering. But the human mind, when blinded and darkened, is very far from being able to rise to a proper knowledge of the divine will; nor can the heart, tossed by perpetual doubt, rest secure in such knowledge. Hence, in order that the word of God may gain full credit, the mind must be enlightened and the heart confirmed from some other source. We shall now have a full definition of faith. This definition is explained in sections 14, 15, 28, 29, 32, 33, 31 of this chapter.

8¶

But before I proceed further, it will be necessary to make some preliminary observations in order to remove difficulties which might otherwise obstruct the reader. And first, I must refute the empty distinction of the Schoolmen between formed and unformed faith. See Lombard, Book 3, Distinction 23. See the refutation in the middle of sections 41, 42, 43, where it is shown that faith produces, and is inseparable from, hope and love.

9¶

In their attempt to mar faith by stripping it of love, they are accustomed to insist on the words of Paul: "Though I have all faith, so that I could remove mountains, and have not charity, I am nothing" (1 Corinthians 13:2). But they do not consider what faith it is of which the Apostle there speaks. Having in the previous chapter discoursed on the various gifts of the Spirit (1 Corinthians 12:10), including diversity of tongues, miracles, and prophecy, and having exhorted the Corinthians to follow the better gifts, in other words, those from which the whole body of the Church would derive greater benefit, he adds, "Yet show I unto you a more excellent way" (1 Corinthians 12:30). All other gifts, however excellent they may be in themselves, are of no value unless they serve charity. They were given for the edification of the Church, and they fail of their purpose if they are not so applied. To prove this he adopts a division, repeating the same gifts which he had mentioned before, but under different names. Miracles and faith are used to denote the same thing, namely, the power of working miracles. Seeing, then, that this miraculous power or faith is a particular gift of God, which a wicked man may possess and abuse, like the gift of tongues, prophecy, or other gifts, it is not strange that he separates it from charity. Their whole error lies in this: while the term faith has a variety of meanings, they overlook this variety and argue as if its meaning were always one and the same. The passage of James by which they try to defend their error will be discussed elsewhere (below, chapter 17, section 11). Although, in discussing faith, we admit that it has a variety of forms, yet when our object is to show what knowledge of God the wicked possess, we hold and maintain, in accordance with Scripture, that the godly alone have faith. Multitudes undoubtedly believe that God is, and admit the truth of the Gospel history and of the other parts of Scripture, in the same way in which they believe the records of past events, or events which they have actually witnessed. Some go even further: they regard the Word of God as an infallible oracle; they do not altogether disregard its precepts, but are moved to some degree by its threatenings and promises. To such people the testimony of faith is attributed, but by catachresis, because they do not with open impiety attack, reject, or condemn the Word of God, but rather display some semblance of obedience.

10¶

But as this shadow or image of faith is of no account, so it is unworthy of the name. How far it differs from true faith will shortly be explained at length. Here, however, we may simply point it out in passing. Simon Magus is said to have believed, though soon afterward he gave proof of his unbelief (Acts 8:13-18). As to the faith attributed to him, we do not understand, with some, that he merely pretended a belief which had no existence in his heart. We rather think that, overcome by the majesty of the Gospel, he yielded some kind of assent, and so far acknowledged Christ to be the author of life and salvation as to take his name willingly upon himself. In like manner, in the Gospel of Luke, those in whom the seed of the word is choked before it brings forth fruit, or in whom, having no depth of earth, it soon withers away, are said to believe for a time. Such people, we do not doubt, eagerly receive the word with a kind of relish and have some feeling of its divine power, so as not only to deceive men by a false semblance of faith, but even to deceive themselves. They imagine that the reverence which they give to the word is genuine piety, because they have no idea of any impiety except that which consists in open and avowed contempt. But whatever that assent may be, it by no means penetrates to the heart so as to have a fixed seat there. Although it sometimes seems to have put down roots, these have no life in them. The human heart has so many recesses for vanity, so many hiding places for falsehood, and is so shrouded by deceit and hypocrisy, that it often deceives itself. Let those who glory in such semblances of faith know that in this respect they are not one bit better than the devils. The one class, indeed, is inferior to them, inasmuch as they are able to hear and understand without emotion things the knowledge of which makes devils tremble (James 2:19). The other class equals them in this, that whatever impression is made upon them, its only result is terror and dismay.

12¶

Although faith is a knowledge of the divine favor toward us and a full persuasion of its truth, it is not strange that the sense of the divine love, which, though akin to faith, differs greatly from it, vanishes in those who are only temporarily impressed. The will of God, I admit, is unchangeable, and his truth is always consistent with itself; but I deny that the reprobate ever advance so far as to penetrate to that secret revelation which Scripture reserves for the elect alone. I therefore deny that they either understand his will as unchangeable or steadily embrace his truth, since they rest content with a fleeting impression. They are like a tree not planted deep enough, which may take root but will in the course of time wither away, though for several years it may not only put forth leaves and flowers but also produce fruit. In short, just as by the revolt of the first man the image of God could be effaced from his mind and soul, so there is nothing strange in his shedding some rays of grace on the reprobate and afterward allowing these to be extinguished. There is nothing to prevent him from giving some a slight knowledge of his Gospel and imbuing others with it thoroughly. Meanwhile, we must remember that, however feeble and slender the faith of the elect may be, yet since the Spirit of God is to them a sure earnest and seal of their adoption, the impression once engraved can never be effaced from their hearts, whereas the light which glimmers in the reprobate is afterward quenched. See section 13, where it is said that this impression, which sometimes exists in the reprobate, is called faith, but improperly.

14¶

Let us now go over the parts of the definition again one by one: I should think that, after a careful examination of them, no doubt will remain. By knowledge we do not mean comprehension, such as the comprehension we have of things that fall under human sense. For this knowledge is so far superior that the human mind must far surpass and go beyond itself in order to reach it. Nor, even when it has reached it, does it comprehend what it feels; but, persuaded of what it does not comprehend, it understands more from the mere certainty of persuasion than it could discern of any human matter by its own capacity. Hence Paul elegantly describes it as the ability "to comprehend with all saints what is the breadth, and length, and depth, and height; and to know the love of Christ, which passes knowledge" (Ephesians 3:18, 19). His purpose was to indicate that what our mind embraces by faith is in every way infinite, and that this kind of knowledge far surpasses all understanding. But because the "mystery which has been hid from ages and from generations" is now "made manifest to the saints" (Colossians 1:26), faith is with good reason sometimes called understanding in Scripture (Colossians 2:2); and it is called knowledge, as by John (1 John 3:2), when he declares that believers know themselves to be the sons of God. And certainly they do know, but rather as those confirmed by a belief in the truthfulness of God than as those taught by any demonstration of reason. Paul also indicates this when he says that "while we are at home in the body, we are absent from the Lord: (For we walk by faith, not by sight)" (2 Corinthians 5:6, 7), thus showing that what we understand by faith is still distant from us and escapes our view. Hence we conclude that the knowledge of faith consists more in certainty than in discernment.

15¶

We add that it is sure and firm, in order to express more fully the strength and constancy of the persuasion. For as faith is not content with a doubtful and fickle opinion, so neither is it content with an obscure and ill-defined conception. The certainty which it requires must be full and decisive, as is usual in matters that have been ascertained and proved. So deeply rooted in our hearts is unbelief, and so prone are we to it, that while all confess with their lips that God is faithful, no one ever believes it without a hard struggle. Especially when brought to the test, Latin, "Præsentim ubi ad rem ventum est." French, "Principalament quand les tentations nous pressent" — especially when temptations press us.

16¶

The principal hinge on which faith turns is this: we must not suppose that the promises of mercy which the Lord offers are true only outside us, and not at all in us; we should rather make them ours by embracing them inwardly. Only in this way is that confidence engendered which he elsewhere calls peace (Romans 5:1), though perhaps he rather means to make peace follow from it. This is the security which quiets and calms the conscience in view of the judgment of God, and without which the conscience is necessarily troubled and almost torn apart by tumultuous dread, except when it happens to slumber for a moment, forgetful both of God and of itself. And truly it is only for a moment. It never enjoys that miserable forgetfulness for long, for the memory of the divine judgment, recurring again and again, stings it to the quick. In a word, he alone is a true believer who, firmly persuaded that God is reconciled and is a kind Father to him, hopes for everything from his kindness; who, trusting in the promises of the divine favor, anticipates salvation with undoubting confidence. So the Apostle shows in these words: "We are made partakers of Christ, if we hold the beginning of our confidence steadfast unto the end" (Hebrews 3:14). He thus holds that none hope well in the Lord except those who confidently glory in being heirs of the heavenly kingdom. No one, I say, is a believer except the one who, trusting in the security of his salvation, confidently triumphs over the devil and death, as we are taught by the noble exclamation of Paul: "I am persuaded, that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:38). In like manner, the same Apostle does not consider the eyes of our understanding to be enlightened unless we know what is the hope of the eternal inheritance to which we are called (Ephesians 1:18). Thus throughout his writings he consistently indicates that the goodness of God is not properly understood when security does not follow as its fruit.

17¶

But it will be said that this differs widely from the experience of believers, who, in recognizing the grace of God toward them, not only feel disquiet (this often happens), but sometimes tremble, overcome with terror, As to the imperfection, strengthening, and increase of faith, see Book 4, chapter 4, sections 7, 8.

18¶

To make this intelligible, we must return to the distinction between flesh and spirit, which we have already mentioned, and which here becomes most apparent. The believer finds two principles within himself: the one filling him with delight as he recognizes the divine goodness, the other filling him with bitterness under a sense of his fallen state; the one leading him to rest on the promise of the Gospel, the other alarming him with the conviction of his iniquity; the one making him exult in the expectation of life, the other making him tremble with the fear of death. This diversity is due to the imperfection of faith, since in the course of the present life we are never so well off as to be entirely cured of the disease of distrust and completely filled and possessed by faith. Hence those conflicts, in which the distrust that clings to the remains of the flesh rises up to assail the faith enlisted in our hearts. But if in the believer's mind certainty is mingled with doubt, must we not always be carried back to the conclusion that faith consists not of a sure and clear, but only of an obscure and confused, understanding of the divine will toward us? By no means. Though we are distracted by various thoughts, it does not follow that we are immediately stripped of faith. Though we are agitated and carried to and fro by distrust, we are not immediately plunged into the abyss; though we are shaken, we are not therefore driven from our place. The invariable outcome of the contest is that faith in the long run overcomes the difficulties by which it was beset and in which it seemed to be endangered.

19¶

The whole matter, then, comes to this: as soon as the smallest particle of faith is instilled into our minds, we begin to behold the face of God, calm, serene, and propitious; far off, indeed, but still so distinctly as to assure us that there is no delusion in it. In proportion to the progress we afterward make (and the progress ought to be uninterrupted), we obtain a nearer and surer view, the very continuance making it more familiar to us. Thus we see that a mind enlightened with the knowledge of God is at first wrapped in much ignorance, an ignorance, however, which is gradually removed. Still, this partial ignorance or obscure discernment does not prevent that clear knowledge of the divine favor which holds the first and principal place in faith. For one who is shut up in a prison, where through a narrow opening he receives the rays of the sun indirectly and, as it were, divided, though he is deprived of a full view of the sun, has no doubt about the source from which the light comes, and he benefits from it. So believers, while bound with the fetters of an earthly body, though surrounded on all sides with much obscurity, are so far enlightened by any slender light which shines on them and displays the divine mercy as to feel secure.

20¶

The Apostle elegantly refers to both in different passages. When he says, "We know in part, and we prophesy in part," and "Now we see through a glass darkly" (1 Corinthians 13:9, 12), he indicates how very small a portion of divine wisdom is given to us in the present life. For although those expressions do not simply indicate that faith is imperfect so long as we groan under a height of flesh, but that the necessity of being constantly engaged in learning is due to our imperfection, he at the same time reminds us that a subject of boundless extent cannot be comprehended by our feeble and narrow capacities. Paul affirms this of the whole Church, each individual being held back and hindered by his own ignorance from coming as near as would be desirable. But that the foretaste which we obtain from any small portion of faith is certain, and by no means deceptive, he shows elsewhere when he affirms that "We all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord" (2 Corinthians 3:18). In such degrees of ignorance much doubt and trembling is necessarily involved, especially since our heart is by its own natural bent prone to unbelief. To this we must add the temptations which, various in kind and infinite in number, are again and again violently assailing us. In particular, conscience itself, weighed down by the load of sins lying upon it, at one time complains and groans, at another accuses itself; at one time murmurs in secret, at another openly rebels. Therefore, whether adverse circumstances seem to signal the wrath of God, or conscience finds the subject and material within itself, unbelief draws from this its weapons and engines to put faith to flight. The aim of all its efforts is to make us think that God is opposed and hostile to us, and so, instead of hoping for any help from him, to make us dread him as a deadly foe.

21¶

To withstand these assaults, faith arms and fortifies itself with the word of God. When the temptation suggested is that God is an enemy because he afflicts, faith replies that while he afflicts he is merciful, his chastening proceeding more from love than from anger. To the thought that God is the avenger of wickedness, it opposes the pardon that stands ready to be bestowed for all offenses whenever the sinner takes refuge again in the divine mercy. Thus the godly mind, however much it may be agitated and torn, at length rises above all difficulties and does not allow its confidence in the divine mercy to be destroyed. Indeed, the disputes which exercise and disturb it rather tend to establish this confidence. A proof of this is that the saints, when the hand of God lies heaviest upon them, still bring their complaints to him and continue to call upon him, when to all appearance he is least disposed to hear. But what use would it be to lament before him if they had no hope of comfort? They would never call upon him if they did not believe that he is ready to help them. Thus the disciples, while they were rebuked by their Master for the weakness of their faith in crying out that they were perishing, still implored his aid (Matthew 8:25). And he, in rebuking them for their lack of faith, does not disown them or class them with unbelievers, but urges them to shake off the fault. Therefore, as we have already said, we again maintain that faith, once fixed in the believer's breast, can never be rooted out of it. However much it may seem shaken and bent in this direction or that, its flame is never so completely quenched as not at least to lurk under the embers. In this way it appears that the word, which is an incorruptible seed, produces fruit like itself. Its germ never withers away utterly and perishes. The saints cannot have a stronger ground for despair than to feel that, according to present appearances, the hand of God is armed for their destruction; and yet Job declares the strength of his confidence thus: "Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him." The truth is that unbelief does not reign in the hearts of believers, but only assails them from without; it does not wound them mortally with its darts, but troubles them, or at most gives them a wound which can be healed. Faith, as Paul declares (Ephesians 6:16), is our shield, which, receiving these darts, either wards them off entirely or at least breaks their force and keeps them from reaching the vital parts. Hence, when faith is shaken, it is just as when a soldier standing firm is forced by the violent blow of a javelin to step back and give way a little; and again, when faith is wounded, it is as if the shield were pierced but not run through by the blow. The godly mind will always rise and be able to say with David, "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for you are with me" (Psalm 23:4). Doubtless it is a terrifying thing to walk in the darkness of death, and it is impossible for believers, however great their strength may be, not to shudder at it; but since the prevailing thought is that God is present and providing for their safety, the feeling of security overcomes that of fear. As Augustine says, whatever engines the devil raises against us, since he cannot gain the heart where faith dwells, he is cast out. Thus, if we may judge by the outcome, not only do believers come off safe from every contest, so as to be ready after a short rest to go down again into the arena, but the saying of John in his Epistle is fulfilled: "This is the victory that overcomes the world, even our faith" (1 John 5:4). It is not said that it will be victorious in a single fight, or a few, or in some one assault, but that it will be victorious over the whole world, though it should be assailed a thousand times.

22¶

There is another kind of fear and trembling which, so far from impairing the security of faith, rather tends to establish it. This is when believers, reflecting that the examples of divine vengeance on the ungodly are a kind of beacon warning them not to provoke the wrath of God by similar wickedness, keep anxious watch; or when, taking a view of their own inherent wretchedness, they learn their entire dependence on God, without whom they feel themselves to be as fleeting and passing as the wind. For when the Apostle sets before the Corinthians the scourges which the Lord inflicted in ancient times on the people of Israel, so that they might be afraid of exposing themselves to similar calamities, he does not in any degree destroy the ground of their confidence; he only shakes off their carnal sluggishness, which suppresses faith but does not strengthen it. Nor, when he takes occasion from the case of the Israelites to exhort, "Let him that thinks he stands take heed lest he fall" (1 Corinthians 10:12), does he bid us waver, as if we had no security for our steadfastness. He only removes arrogance and rash confidence in our own strength, telling the Gentiles not to presume because the Jews had been cast off and they had been admitted to their place (Romans 11:20). In that passage, indeed, he is not addressing believers only, but also includes hypocrites, who gloried merely in outward appearance. Nor is he addressing individuals; rather, contrasting the Jews and the Gentiles, he first shows that the rejection of the former was a just punishment of their ingratitude and unbelief, and then exhorts the latter to beware lest pride and presumption deprive them of the grace of adoption which had lately been transferred to them. For as in that rejection of the Jews there still remained some who were not excluded from the covenant of adoption, so there might be some among the Gentiles who, possessing no true faith, were only puffed up with vain carnal confidence, and so abused the goodness of God to their own destruction. But even if you hold that the words were addressed to elect believers, no inconsistency will follow. It is one thing, in order to keep believers from indulging in vain confidence, to restrain the rashness which, from the remains of the flesh, sometimes gains upon them; and it is another thing to strike terror into their consciences and prevent them from feeling secure in the mercy of God.

23¶

Then, when he bids us work out our salvation with fear and trembling, all he requires is that we accustom ourselves to think very humbly of our own strength and to trust in the strength of the Lord. For nothing stirs us so strongly to place all our confidence and assurance in the Lord as distrust of ourselves, and the anxiety produced by an awareness of our calamitous condition. In this sense we are to understand the words of the Psalmist: "I will come into your house in the multitude of your mercy: and in your fear will I worship toward your holy temple" (Psalm 5:7). Here he fittingly unites confident faith, leaning on the divine mercy, with religious fear, which we must necessarily feel whenever, coming into the presence of the divine majesty, we are made aware by its splendor of the extent of our own impurity. Solomon also truly declares: "Happy is the man that fears always: but he that hardens his heart shall fall into mischief" (Proverbs 28:14). The fear he speaks of is the fear which makes us more cautious, not the fear which produces despondency: the fear which is felt when the mind, confounded in itself, regains its composure in God; downcast in itself, takes courage in God; distrusting itself, breathes confidence in God. Hence there is nothing inconsistent in believers being afraid and at the same time possessing secure consolation, as they alternately behold their own vanity and direct their thoughts to the truth of God. How, it will be asked, can fear and faith dwell in the same mind? In just the same way as sluggishness and anxiety can dwell there. The ungodly court a state of lethargy so that the fear of God may not trouble them; and yet the judgment of God so presses upon them that they cannot obtain their desire. In the same way God can train his people to humility and curb them with the bridle of modesty, while they are still fighting bravely. And it is plain from the context that this was the Apostle's meaning, since he states as the ground of fear and trembling that it is God who works in us both to will and to do of his good pleasure. In the same sense we must understand the words of the Prophet: "The children of Israel" "shall fear the Lord and his goodness in the latter days" (Hosea 3:5). For not only does piety beget reverence toward God, but the sweet attraction of grace inspires a man, though despairing of himself, with fear and admiration at once, making him feel his dependence on God and submit humbly to his power.

24¶

Here, however, we give no support to that most pestilential philosophy which some semi-papists are at present beginning to spread in corners. Unable to defend the gross doubt taught by the Schoolmen, they resort to another fiction, so that they may compound a mixture of faith and unbelief. They admit that whenever we look to Christ we are furnished with full ground for hope; but since we are always unworthy of all the blessings which are offered to us in Christ, they would have us waver and hesitate in view of our unworthiness. In short, they give conscience a position between hope and fear, making it turn by successive turns from the one to the other. Hope and fear, again, they set in complete opposition: the one falling as the other rises, and rising as the other falls. Thus Satan, finding the devices by which he used to destroy the certainty of faith too obvious to be of any use now, is trying by indirect methods to undermine it. The French is, "Voila comme Satan, quand il voit que par mensonge clair et ouvert il ne peust plus destruire la certitude de la foy, s'efforce en cachette et comme par dessous terre la ruiner" — Behold how Satan, when he sees that by plain and open falsehood he can no longer destroy the certainty of faith, is striving in secret, and as it were underground, to ruin it.

25¶

The same doctrine is taught by Bernard when he treats this subject directly in his Fifth Homily on the Dedication of the Temple: "By the blessing of God, sometimes when meditating on the soul, I seem to find in it, as it were, two contraries. When I look at it as it is in itself and of itself, the truest thing I can say of it is that it has been reduced to nothing. What need is there to list each of its miseries? How burdened with sin, darkened with obscurity, ensnared by allurements, teeming with lusts, ruled by passion, filled with delusions, ever prone to evil, inclined to every vice; lastly, full of disgrace and confusion. If all its righteousnesses, when examined by the light of truth, are but as filthy rags (Isaiah 64:6), what must we suppose its unrighteousness to be? 'If, therefore, the light that is in you be darkness, how great is that darkness?' (Matthew 6:23). What then? Man doubtless has been made subject to vanity; man has been reduced to nothing; man is nothing. And yet how is he whom God exalts utterly nothing? How is he nothing to whom a divine heart has been given? Let us breathe again, brethren. Although we are nothing in our own hearts, perhaps something of us may lie hidden in the heart of God. O Father of mercies! O Father of the miserable! How do you plant your heart in us? Where your heart is, there is your treasure also. But how are we your treasure if we are nothing? All nations before you are as nothing. Observe: before you, not within you. Such they are in the judgment of your truth, but not such in regard to your affection. You call the things which are not as though they were; and they are not, because you call them 'things that are not'; and yet they are, because you call them. For though they are not as to themselves, yet they are with you, according to the declaration of Paul: 'Not of works, but of him that calls'" (Romans 9:11). He then goes on to say that the connection is wonderful from both points of view. Certainly things which are connected together do not destroy each other. He explains this more clearly in his conclusion in the following terms: "If in both views we diligently consider what we are, in the one view our nothingness, in the other our greatness, I suppose our glorying will seem restrained; but perhaps it is rather increased and confirmed, because we glory not in ourselves but in the Lord. Our thought is, if he has determined to save us, we shall be delivered; and here we begin to breathe again. But, rising to a loftier height, let us seek the city of God, let us seek the temple, let us seek our home, let us seek our spouse. I have not forgotten myself when, with fear and reverence, I say: We are; we are in the heart of God. We are, by his dignifying, not by our own dignity."

26¶

Moreover, the fear of the Lord, which is uniformly attributed to all the saints, and which in one passage is called "the beginning of wisdom" and in another wisdom itself, although it is one, proceeds from a twofold cause. God is entitled to the reverence due to a Father and a Lord. Hence whoever desires to worship him rightly will strive to act the part both of an obedient son and of a faithful servant. The obedience paid to God as a Father he calls, by his prophet, honor; the service rendered to him as a master he calls fear. "A son honors his father, and a servant his master. If then I be a father, where is my honor? and if I be a master, where is my fear?" Psalm 111:10; Proverbs 1:7, 9:10, 15:24; Job 28:28; Malachi 1:6.

27¶

There is nothing contrary to this in the statement of John: "There is no fear in love; but perfect love casts out fear: because fear has torment" (1 John 4:18). For he is speaking of the fear of unbelief, between which and the fear of believers there is a wide difference. The wicked do not fear God out of any unwillingness to offend him, if only they could do so with impunity; but knowing that he is armed with power for vengeance, they tremble in dismay when they hear of his anger. And they dread his anger in this way because they think it is hanging over them, and they expect every moment that it will fall upon their heads. But believers, as has been said, dread the offense even more than the punishment. They are not alarmed by the fear of punishment, as if it were hanging over them, Latin, "acsi cervicibus suis impenderet." French, "comme si l'enfer leur etoit desia present pour les englouter" — as if hell were already present to engulf them.

28¶

The divine favor to which faith is said to look, we understand to include the possession of salvation and eternal life. For if no good thing can be lacking to us when God is propitious, we have ample security for our salvation when we are assured of his love. "Turn us again, O God, and cause your face to shine," says the Prophet, "and we shall be saved" (Psalm 80:3). Hence the Scriptures make the sum of our salvation consist in the removal of all enmity and our admission into favor, thus indicating that when God is reconciled all danger is past, and every good thing will come to us. Therefore faith, laying hold of the love of God, has the promise both of the present and of the future life, and ample security for all blessings (Ephesians 2:14). The nature of this must be learned from the word. Faith does not promise us length of days, riches, and honors (the Lord not having been pleased to appoint any of these for us); but it is content with the assurance that, however poor we may be in present comforts, God will never fail us. The chief security lies in the expectation of the future life, which is placed beyond doubt by the word of God. Whatever miseries and calamities await the children of God in this world, they cannot make his favor cease to be complete happiness. Hence, when we wished to express the sum of blessedness, we described it as the favor of God, from which, as from their source, all kinds of blessings flow. And we may observe throughout the Scriptures that they direct us to the love of God, not only when they treat of our eternal salvation, but in regard to any blessing whatever. For this reason David sings that the loving-kindness of God, experienced by the godly heart, is sweeter and more to be desired than life itself (Psalm 63:3). In short, if we have every earthly comfort we could wish for, but are uncertain whether we have the love or the hatred of God, our happiness will be cursed, and therefore miserable. But if God lifts upon us the light of his fatherly countenance, our very miseries will be blessed, since they will become helps to our salvation. Thus Paul, after gathering together all kinds of adversity, boasts that they cannot separate us from the love of God; and in his prayers he uniformly begins with the grace of God as the source of all prosperity. In like manner, against all the terrors which assail us, David sets simply the favor of God: "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for you are with me" (Psalm 23:4). And we feel that our minds always waver until, content with the grace of God, we seek peace in it and feel thoroughly persuaded of what is said in the psalm: "Blessed is the nation whose God is the Lord, and the people whom he has chosen for his own inheritance" (Psalm 33:12).

29¶

We make the free promise the foundation of faith, because in it faith properly consists. For though faith holds that God is always true, whether in commanding or forbidding, promising or threatening; though it obediently receives his commands, observes his prohibitions, and gives heed to his threatenings; yet it properly begins with the promise, continues with it, and ends with it. It seeks life in God, a life which is not found in commands or in the pronouncements of punishment, but in the promise of mercy. And this promise must be free; for a conditional promise, which throws us back upon our works, promises life only so far as we find it existing in ourselves. Therefore, if we would not have faith waver and tremble, we must support it with the promise of salvation, which is offered by the Lord spontaneously and freely, out of regard for our misery rather than our worth. Hence the Apostle bears this testimony to the Gospel, that it is the word of faith (Romans 10:8). This he grants neither to the precepts nor to the promises of the Law, since there is nothing which can establish our faith except that free embassy by which God reconciles the world to himself. Hence he often uses faith and the Gospel as correlative terms, as when he says that the ministry of the Gospel was committed to him for "obedience to the faith"; that "it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believes"; that "therein is the righteousness of God revealed from faith to faith" (Romans 1:5, 16, 17). No wonder; for seeing that the Gospel is "the ministry of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:18), there is no other sufficient evidence of the divine favor, such as faith needs to know. Therefore, when we say that faith must rest on a free promise, we do not deny that believers accept and embrace the word of God in all its parts; but we point to the promise of mercy as its special object. Believers, indeed, ought to recognize God as the judge and avenger of wickedness; and yet mercy is the object to which they properly look, since he is set before their contemplation as "good and ready to forgive," "plenteous in mercy," "slow to anger," "good to all," and shedding "his tender mercies over all his works." Psalm 86:5; 103:8; 145:8, 9.

30¶

I do not stop to consider the rabid objections of Pighius and others like him, who attack this restriction as tearing faith apart and laying hold of only one of its fragments. I admit, as I have already said, that the general object of faith (as they express it) is the truth of God, whether he threatens or gives hope of his favor. Accordingly, the Apostle attributes it to faith in Noah that he feared the destruction of the world when it was not yet seen (Hebrews 11:17). If fear of impending punishment was a work of faith, threatening ought not to be excluded in defining it. This is indeed true; but we are unjustly and slanderously charged with denying that faith looks to the whole word of God. We only mean to maintain these two points: that faith is never settled until it attains to a free promise; and that the only way in which faith reconciles us to God is by uniting us with Christ. Both deserve attention. We are inquiring after a faith which separates the children of God from the reprobate, believers from unbelievers. Shall every man, then, who believes that God is just in what he commands and true in what he threatens be classed on that account with believers? Very far from it. Faith, then, has no firm footing until it stands in the mercy of God. Then what end do we have in view in discussing faith? Is it not that we may understand the way of salvation? But how can faith be saving except in so far as it grafts us into the body of Christ? There is no absurdity, therefore, when in defining it we press its special object in this way, and, by way of distinction, add to the general character the particular mark which distinguishes the believer from the unbeliever. In short, the malicious have nothing to find fault with in this doctrine, unless they mean to bring the same censure against the Apostle Paul, who specially calls the Gospel "the word of faith" (Romans 10:8).

31¶

Hence again we infer, as has already been explained, that faith has no less need of the word than the fruit of a tree has of a living root; because, as David testifies, none can hope in God except those who know his name (Psalm 9:10). This knowledge, however, is not left to every man's imagination, but depends on the testimony which God himself gives to his goodness. The same Psalmist confirms this in another passage: "Your salvation, according to your word" (Psalm 119:41). Again, "Save me," "I hoped in your word" (Psalm 119:146, 147). Here we must note the relation of faith to the word, and to salvation as its consequence. Still, we do not exclude the power of God. If faith cannot support itself by looking to this power, it will never give him the honor which is his due. Paul seems to relate a trivial or very ordinary matter with regard to Abraham when he says that he believed that God, who had given him the promise of a blessed seed, was able also to perform it (Romans 4:21). And likewise in another passage he says of himself, "I know whom I have believed, and am persuaded that he is able to keep that which I have committed unto him against that day" (2 Timothy 1:12). But let anyone consider within himself how he is again and again assailed with doubts about the power of God, and he will readily perceive that those who rightly magnify it have made no small progress in faith. We all acknowledge that God can do whatever he pleases; but while every temptation, even the most trivial, fills us with fear and dread, it is plain that we detract from the power of God by attaching less importance to his promises than to Satan's threatenings against them. The French adds, "Combien que nous ayons les promesses de Dieu pour nous munir à l'encontre" — although we have the promise of God to strengthen us for the encounter.

This is the reason why Isaiah, when he wishes to impress the certainty of faith on the hearts of the people, speaks so magnificently of the boundless power of God. He often seems, after beginning to speak of the hope of pardon and reconciliation, to digress and needlessly take a long, roundabout course, describing how wonderfully God rules the fabric of heaven and earth, with the whole course of nature; and yet he introduces nothing which is not suited to the occasion. For unless the power of God, to which all things are possible, is set before our eyes, our ears perversely refuse admission to the word, or set no just value upon it. We may add that an effectual power is meant here; for piety, as has been seen elsewhere, always makes a practical application of the power of God, and in particular keeps in view those works in which he has declared himself to be a Father. Hence the frequent mention of redemption in Scripture, from which the Israelites might learn that he who had once been the author of salvation would be its perpetual guardian. By his own example David also reminds us that the benefits which God has bestowed privately on any individual tend to confirm his faith for the time to come. Indeed, when God seems to have forsaken us, we ought to extend our view further and take courage from his former favors, as is said in another psalm: "I remember the days of old; I meditate on all your works" (Psalm 143:5). Again, "I will remember the works of the Lord: surely I will remember your wonders of old" (Psalm 77:11). But because all our conceptions of the power and works of God are fleeting without the word, we are not rash in maintaining that there is no faith until God presents us with clear evidence of his grace.

Here, however, a question might be raised about the view to be taken of Sarah and Rebekah, both of whom, driven as it would seem by zeal for the faith, went beyond the limits of the word. Sarah, in her eager desire for the promised seed, gave her maid to her husband. That she sinned in many respects is not to be denied; but the only fault to which I now refer is that she was carried away by zeal and did not confine herself within the limits prescribed by the word. It is certain, however, that her desire proceeded from faith. Rebekah, again, divinely informed of the election of her son Jacob, procures the blessing for him by a wicked stratagem: she deceives her husband, who was a witness and minister of divine grace; she forces her son to lie; by various frauds and deceptions she corrupts divine truth; in short, by exposing his promise to scorn, she does all in her power to make it of no effect. And yet this conduct, however wicked and blameworthy, was not devoid of faith. She must have overcome many obstacles before she came to so strong a desire for something which, without any hope of earthly advantage, was full of difficulty and danger. In the same way, we cannot say that the holy patriarch Isaac was altogether devoid of faith because, after he had been similarly informed of the honor transferred to the younger son, he still continued his preference for his firstborn, Esau. These examples certainly show that error is often mingled with faith, and yet that when faith is real, it always gains the upper hand. For as the particular error of Rebekah did not render the blessing of no effect, neither did it nullify the faith which generally ruled in her mind and was the principle and cause of that action. In this, nevertheless, Rebekah showed how prone the human mind is to turn aside whenever it allows itself the least indulgence. But though defect and weakness obscure faith, they do not extinguish it. Still, they warn us how carefully we ought to cling to the word of God, and at the same time they confirm what we have taught, namely, that faith gives way when it is not supported by the word, just as the minds of Sarah, Isaac, and Rebekah would have lost themselves in wandering paths had not the secret restraint of Providence kept them obedient to the word.

32¶

On the other hand, we have good ground for including all the promises in Christ, since the Apostle includes the whole Gospel under the knowledge of Christ, and declares that all the promises of God are in him yea, and in him amen. Romans 1:3; 1 Corinthians 2:2; 2 Corinthians 1:20.

33¶

A simple outward presentation of the word ought to be amply sufficient to produce faith, if our blindness and perversity did not prevent it. But such is the proneness of our mind to vanity that it can never hold fast to the truth of God, and such is its dullness that it is always blind even in his light. Hence without the illumination of the Spirit the word has no effect; and hence also it is obvious that faith is something higher than human understanding. Nor would it be enough for the mind to be enlightened by the Spirit of God, unless the heart were also strengthened and supported by his power. Here the Schoolmen go completely astray, dwelling entirely, in their treatment of faith, on the bare, simple assent of the understanding, and altogether overlooking the confidence and security of the heart. Faith is the special gift of God in both ways: in purifying the mind so as to give it a relish for divine truth, and afterward in establishing it in that truth. For the Spirit does not merely originate faith, but gradually increases it, until by means of it he leads us into the heavenly kingdom. "That good thing which was committed unto you," says Paul, "keep by the Holy Ghost which dwells in us" (2 Timothy 1:14). In what sense Paul says (Galatians 3:2) that the Spirit is given by the hearing of faith may be easily explained. If there were only a single gift of the Spirit, he who is the author and cause of faith could not without absurdity be said to be its effect; but since he is celebrating the gifts with which God adorns his church, and by successive additions of faith leads it to perfection, there is nothing strange in his ascribing to faith the very gifts which faith prepares us to receive. It seems paradoxical to some when it is said that none can believe in Christ except those to whom it is given; but this is partly because they do not observe how hidden and sublime heavenly wisdom is, or how dull the mind of man is in discerning divine mysteries, and partly because they pay no regard to that firm and stable constancy of heart which is the chief part of faith.

35¶

Having shown more fully elsewhere, when treating of the corruption of our nature, how little able men are to believe (Book 2, chapters 2, 3), I will not weary the reader by repeating it again. Let it suffice to observe that the spirit of faith is used by Paul as synonymous with the very faith which we receive from the Spirit, but which we do not have by nature (2 Corinthians 4:13). Accordingly, he prays for the Thessalonians "that our God would count you worthy of this calling, and fulfill all the good pleasure of his goodness, and the work of faith with power" (2 Thessalonians 1:2). Here, by calling faith the work of God, and distinguishing it by way of epithet, fittingly calling it his good pleasure, he declares that it is not of man's own nature; and not content with this, he adds that it is a display of divine power. When he tells the Corinthians that faith stands not "in the wisdom of men, but in the power of God" (1 Corinthians 2:4), he is no doubt speaking of outward miracles; but since the reprobate are blinded when they behold them, he also includes that inward seal of which he makes mention elsewhere. And to display his generosity the better in this most excellent gift, God does not bestow it on all indiscriminately, but by special privilege imparts it to whom he will. To this effect we have already quoted passages of Scripture, concerning which Augustine, their faithful expositor, exclaims (On the Words of the Apostle, Sermon 2): "Our Savior, to teach that faith in him is a gift, not a merit, says, 'No man can come to me, except the Father which has sent me draw him' (John 6:44). It is strange: when two persons hear, the one despises, the other ascends. Let him who despises impute it to himself; let him who ascends not claim it for himself." In another passage he asks, "Why is it given to the one and not to the other? I am not ashamed to say, This is one of the deep things of the cross. From some unknown depth of the judgments of God, which we cannot search out, all our ability proceeds. I see that I am able; but how I am able I do not see. This far only I see, that it is of God. But why the one and not the other? This is too great for me: it is an abyss, a depth of the cross. I can cry out in wonder; I cannot discuss and demonstrate." The whole comes to this: Christ, when he produces faith in us by the agency of his Spirit, at the same time grafts us into his body, so that we may become partakers of all blessings.

36¶

The next thing necessary is that what the mind has taken in be transferred to the heart. The word is not received in faith when it merely flutters about in the brain, but when it has taken deep root in the heart and become an invincible bulwark to withstand and repel all the assaults of temptation. But if the illumination of the Spirit is the true source of understanding in the intellect, his agency is much more evident in the confirmation of the heart, since there is more distrust in the heart than blindness in the mind, and it is more difficult to inspire the soul with security than to imbue it with knowledge. Hence the Spirit performs the part of a seal, sealing upon our hearts the very promises whose certainty was previously impressed on our minds. He also serves as an earnest in establishing and confirming these promises. Thus the Apostle says, "In whom also, after that you believed, you were sealed with that holy Spirit of promise, which is the earnest of our inheritance" (Ephesians 1:13, 14). You see how he teaches that the hearts of believers are stamped with the Spirit as with a seal, and calls him the Spirit of promise because he ratifies the gospel to us. In like manner he says to the Corinthians, "God has also sealed us, and given the earnest of the Spirit in our hearts" (2 Corinthians 1:22). And again, when speaking of a full and confident hope, he grounds it on the "earnest of the Spirit" (2 Corinthians 5:5).

39¶

But, they say, it is rash and presumptuous to claim an undoubted knowledge of the divine will. I would grant this if we held that we were able to subject the incomprehensible counsel of God to our feeble intellect. But when we simply say with Paul, "We have received, not the spirit of the world, but the Spirit which is of God; that we might know the things that are freely given to us of God" (1 Corinthians 2:12), what can they set against this without insulting the Spirit of God? And if it is sacrilege to charge the revelation which he has given us with falsehood, or uncertainty, or ambiguity, how can we be wrong in maintaining its certainty? But they still cry out that there is great rashness in our presuming to glory in possessing the Spirit of God. The French adds, "En quoy ils demonstrent grandement leur betisc" — In this they give a great demonstration of their stupidity.

40¶

So that they may not try to undermine the certainty of faith in one direction only, they attack it in another, namely, that although the believer may lawfully form a judgment about the favor of God from his present state of righteousness, the knowledge of final perseverance still remains in suspense. An admirable security is left to us indeed, if for the present moment only we can judge by moral conjecture that we are in grace, but do not know how we shall be tomorrow! Very different is the language of the Apostle: "I am persuaded that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:38). They try to evade the force of this by frivolously pretending that the Apostle had this assurance by special revelation. But they are caught too firmly to escape in this way; for in that passage he is treating not of his individual experience, but of the blessings which all believers in common derive from faith. But then, they say, Paul in another passage alarms us by mentioning our weakness and inconstancy: "Let him that thinks he stands take heed lest he fall" (1 Corinthians 10:12). True; but he says this not to fill us with terror, but so that we may learn to humble ourselves under the mighty hand of God, as Peter explains (1 Peter 5:6). Then how absurd it is to limit the certainty of faith to a point of time, seeing that it is the property of faith to pass beyond the whole course of this life and to stretch forward to a future immortality! Therefore, since believers owe it to the favor of God that, enlightened by his Spirit, they enjoy through faith the prospect of heavenly life, there is so little approach to arrogance in each one's glorying in this, that anyone who is ashamed to confess it, instead of showing modesty or submission, rather betrays extreme ingratitude by maliciously suppressing the divine goodness.

41¶

Since the nature of faith could not be shown better or more clearly than by the substance of the promise on which it leans as its proper foundation, and without which it immediately falls, or rather vanishes away, we have derived our definition from it. It is a definition, however, not at all at variance with that definition, or rather description, which the Apostle adapts to his discourse when he says that faith is "the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen" (Hebrews 11:1). For by the term substance (ὑπόστασις) he means a kind of prop on which the godly mind rests and leans. It is as if he had said that faith is a kind of certain and secure possession of those things which are promised to us by God; unless we prefer to take ὑπόστασις for confidence. I have no objection to this, though I am more inclined to adopt the other interpretation, which is more generally received. Again, he wishes to indicate that until the last day, when the books will be opened (Daniel 7:10; Revelation 20:12), the things pertaining to our salvation are too lofty to be perceived by our senses, seen by our eyes, or handled by our hands; and that in the meantime there is no possible way in which we can possess them, unless we can go beyond the reach of our own intellect and raise our eyes above all worldly objects; in short, surpass ourselves. So he adds that this certainty of possession relates to things which are only hoped for, and therefore not seen. For as Paul says (Romans 8:24), "A hope that is seen is not hope," and we "hope for that we see not." When he calls it the evidence or proof, or, as Augustine repeatedly renders it (see Homilies on John 79 and 95), the conviction of things not present, the Greek term being ἔλενγχος, it is the same as if he had called it the appearance of things not apparent, the sight of things not seen, the clearness of things obscure, the presence of things absent, the manifestation of things hidden. For the mysteries of God (and to this class belong the things which pertain to our salvation) cannot be discerned in themselves, or, as it is put, in their own nature; but we behold them only in his word, of whose truth we ought to be as firmly persuaded as if we held that everything which it says were already done and completed. But how can the mind rise to such a perception and foretaste of the divine goodness without being at the same time wholly inflamed with love for God? The abundance of joy which God has stored up for those who fear him cannot be truly known without making a most powerful impression. Whoever is once affected in this way is raised up and carried entirely toward him. Hence it is not strange that no wicked and perverse heart ever experiences this feeling, by which, transported to heaven itself, we are admitted to the most hidden treasures of God and the holiest recesses of his kingdom, which must not be profaned by the entrance of an impure heart. For what the Schoolmen say about the priority of love to faith and hope is a mere dream (see Sentences, Book 3, Distinction 25, etc.), since it is faith alone that first engenders love. How much better is Bernard: "The testimony of conscience, which Paul calls 'the rejoicing' of believers, I believe to consist in three things. It is necessary, first of all, to believe that you cannot have remission of sins except by the indulgence of God; secondly, that you cannot have any good work at all unless he also gives it; lastly, that you cannot merit eternal life by any works unless it also is freely given" (Bernard, Sermon 1 on the Annunciation). Shortly afterward he adds, "These things are not sufficient, but are a kind of beginning of faith; for while believing that your sins can be forgiven only by God, you must also hold that they are not forgiven until you are persuaded by the testimony of the Holy Spirit that salvation is stored up for us; that as God pardons sins, and gives merits, and after merits rewards, you cannot stop at that beginning." But these and other topics will be considered in their own place; let it suffice at present to understand what faith is.

42¶

Wherever this living faith exists, it must have the hope of eternal life as its inseparable companion, or rather must of itself beget and display it; where hope is lacking, however clearly and elegantly we may discourse about faith, it is certain that we do not have it. For if faith is (as has been said) a firm persuasion of the truth of God, a persuasion that it can never be false, never deceive, never be in vain, then those who have received this assurance must at the same time expect that God will perform his promises, which in their conviction are absolutely true. So, in a word, hope is nothing more than the expectation of those things which faith has already believed to have been truly promised by God. Thus faith believes that God is true; hope expects that in due season he will manifest his truth. Faith believes that he is our Father; hope expects that he will always act the part of a Father toward us. Faith believes that eternal life has been given to us; hope expects that it will one day be revealed. Faith is the foundation on which hope rests; hope nourishes and sustains faith. For as no one can expect anything from God without first believing his promises, so, on the other hand, the weakness of our faith, which might grow weary and fall away, must be supported and cherished by patient hope and expectation. For this reason Paul rightly says, "We are saved by hope" (Romans 8:24). For while hope silently waits for the Lord, it restrains faith from rushing ahead too hastily; it confirms faith when it might waver concerning the promises of God or begin to doubt their truth; it refreshes faith when it might grow tired; it extends its view to the final goal, so as not to let it give up in the middle of the course, or at the very outset. In short, by constantly renewing and reviving faith, it is again and again supplying fresh vigor for perseverance. On the whole, how necessary the reinforcements of hope are to establish faith will appear better if we reflect on the many forms of temptation by which those who have embraced the word of God are assailed and shaken. First, the Lord often keeps us in suspense by delaying the fulfillment of his promises much longer than we could wish. Here the office of hope is to do what the prophet commands: "Though it tarry, wait for it" (Habakkuk 2:3). Sometimes he not only allows faith to grow faint, but even openly shows his displeasure. Here there is still greater need for the aid of hope, so that we may be able to say with another prophet, "I will wait upon the Lord, that hides his face from the house of Jacob, and I will look for him" (Isaiah 8:17). Scoffers also rise up, as Peter tells us, and ask, "Where is the promise of his coming? for since the fathers fell asleep, all things continue as they were from the beginning of the creation" (2 Peter 3:4). Indeed, the world and the flesh suggest the same thing. Here faith must be supported by the patience of hope and, fixed on the contemplation of eternity, must consider that "one day is with the Lord as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" (2 Peter 3:8; Psalm 90:4).

43¶

Because of this connection and affinity, Scripture sometimes mingles the two terms faith and hope. For when Peter says that we are "kept by the power of God through faith unto salvation, ready to be revealed in the last time" (1 Peter 1:5), he attributes to faith what more properly belongs to hope. And not without reason, since we have already shown that hope is nothing else than the food and strength of faith. Sometimes the two are joined together, as in the same Epistle: "That your faith and hope might be in God" (1 Peter 1:21). Paul, again, in the Epistle to the Philippians, derives expectation from hope (Philippians 1:20), because in hoping patiently we hold our wishes in suspense until God shows his own time. The whole of this subject may be better understood from the tenth chapter of the Epistle to the Hebrews, to which I have already referred. Paul, in another passage, though not in strictly proper speech, expresses the same thing in these words: "For we through the Spirit wait for the hope of righteousness by faith" (Galatians 5:5); that is, after embracing the testimony of the Gospel concerning free love, we wait until God openly manifests what is now only an object of hope. It is now obvious how absurdly Peter Lombard lays down a double foundation of hope, namely, the grace of God and the merit of works (Sentences, Book 3, Distinction 26). Hope cannot have any other object than faith has. But we have already shown clearly that the only object of faith is the mercy of God, to which, to use the common expression, it must look with both eyes. But it is worthwhile to listen to the strange reason which he puts forward. If you presume, he says, to hope for anything without merit, it should be called not hope but presumption. Who, dear reader, does not detest the gross stupidity Latin, "Quis non merito, amice lector, tales bestias execretur?" French, "Je vous prie, mes amis, qui se tiendra de maudire telles bestes?" — I pray you, my friends, who can refrain from cursing such beasts?

Faith, Calvin teaches, is no bare assent to the Gospel as history, but a firm and certain knowledge of God's benevolence toward us, founded on the free promise in Christ, sealed on the heart by the Spirit — a knowledge that grows even as the believer trembles, for faith and fear dwell together in the same breast and faith comes off victorious in the long war. This is steadying for any saint in 2026 who measures his faith by his feelings and finds himself shaken. The doubt that assails you from without does not unseat the faith planted within; the soldier forced to step back has not been driven from the field. Teach your children that assurance rests not on the strength of their grip but on the faithfulness of him they grip — that to walk through the valley fearing no evil is not the absence of trembling but the presence of God. In a faithless and fretful age, hold the beginning of your confidence steadfast unto the end.
