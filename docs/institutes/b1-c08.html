The Credibility of Scripture Sufficiently Proved in So Far as Natural Reason Admits

This chapter consists of four parts. The first contains certain general proofs which may be easily gathered out of the writings both of the Old and New Testament—viz. the arrangement of the sacred volume, its dignity, truth, simplicity, efficacy, and majesty, sec. 1, 2. The second part contains special proofs taken from the Old Testament—viz. the antiquity of the books of Moses, their authority, his miracles and prophecies, sec. 3-7; also, the predictions of the other prophets and their wondrous harmony, sec. 8. There is subjoined a refutation of two objections to the books of Moses and the Prophets, sec. 9, 10. The third part exhibits proofs gathered out of the New Testament, e.g. , the harmony of the Evangelists in their account of heavenly mysteries, the majesty of the writings of John, Peter, and Paul, the remarkable calling of the Apostles and conversion of Paul, sec. 11. The last part exhibits the proofs drawn from ecclesiastical history, the perpetual consent of the Church in receiving and preserving divine truth, the invincible force of the truth in defending itself, the agreement of the godly (though otherwise differing so much from one another), the pious profession of the same doctrine by many illustrious men; in fine, the more than human constancy of the martyrs, sec. 12, 13. This is followed by a conclusion of the particular topic discussed.

1¶

It would be useless to fortify the authority of Scripture by argument, or to prop it up with the agreement of the Church, or to confirm it by any other helps, if it were not accompanied by an assurance higher and stronger than human judgment can give. Until this better foundation has been laid, the authority of Scripture hangs in suspense. On the other hand, once we recognize that Scripture is exempt from the common rule, and receive it reverently and according to its dignity, then those proofs which were not strong enough on their own to produce and rivet a full conviction in our minds become most fitting helps. For it is remarkable how much we are strengthened in our belief when we consider more attentively how admirably the system of divine wisdom contained in it is arranged — how perfectly free its doctrine is from everything that smacks of earth, how beautifully it harmonizes in all its parts, and how rich it is in all the other qualities that lend an air of majesty to a piece of writing. Our hearts are assured still more firmly when we reflect that our admiration is drawn out more by the dignity of the subject matter than by the graces of style. For it was not without an admirable arrangement of Providence that the lofty mysteries of the kingdom of heaven have for the most part been delivered in words of contemptible plainness. Had they been dressed in a more splendid eloquence, the wicked might have quibbled and claimed that this was the whole of their force. But as it is, when an unpolished simplicity, almost bordering on crudeness, makes a deeper impression than the loftiest flights of oratory, what does that show, if not that the Holy Scriptures are too mighty in the power of truth to need the rhetorician's art?

So the Apostle had good ground for declaring that the faith of the Corinthians rested not on "the wisdom of men," but on "the power of God" (1 Cor. 2:5), since his speech and preaching among them had been "not with enticing words of man's wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power" (1 Cor. 2:5). For truth is vindicated against every doubt when, needing no outside aid, it finds its whole sufficiency in itself. How uniquely this property belongs to Scripture appears from the fact that no human writings, however skillfully composed, are able to affect us in anything like the same way. Read Demosthenes or Cicero; read Plato, Aristotle, or any other of that class. You will, I grant, feel wonderfully drawn in, pleased, moved, enchanted. But turn from them to the reading of the Sacred Volume, and whether you want it to or not, it will so affect you, so pierce your heart, so work its way into your very marrow, that compared with the impression it leaves, the impression of orators and philosophers will all but disappear — making it plain that in the Sacred Volume there is a truth divine, something that makes it immeasurably superior to all the gifts and graces that human beings can attain.

2¶

I confess, however, that in elegance and beauty — indeed, in splendor — the style of some of the prophets is not surpassed by the eloquence of pagan writers. By examples of this kind the Holy Spirit was pleased to show that where he used a rough and homely style elsewhere, it was not for want of eloquence. But whether you read David, Isaiah, and others of that same class, whose speech flows sweet and pleasant, or Amos the herdsman, Jeremiah, and Zechariah, whose rougher idiom has the flavor of the countryside, that majesty of the Spirit which I mentioned stands out plainly in them all. I am not unaware that, just as Satan often apes God in order to work his way the more easily into the minds of the simple by a deceptive resemblance, so he craftily spread abroad the ungodly errors with which he deceived wretched people in an uncouth and half-barbarous style, and often used obsolete forms of expression in order to cloak his impostures. Anyone with even a moderate share of sense needs no telling how empty and vile such affectation is. But as for the Holy Scriptures, however insolently people may try to carp at them, they are filled with thoughts which plainly no human being could ever have conceived. Let each of the prophets be examined, and not one will be found who does not rise far above human reach. Those who find their writings dull must be utterly devoid of taste.

3¶

Since this subject has been treated at length by others, it will be enough here simply to touch on its leading points. Besides the qualities already mentioned, great weight is due to the antiquity of Scripture (Euseb. Prepar. Evang. lib. 2 c. 1). Whatever fables Greek writers may tell about the Egyptian theology, no monument of any religion exists that is not far later than the age of Moses. But Moses does not introduce a new deity. He only sets out that teaching about the eternal God which the Israelites had received by tradition from their fathers, and which had been handed down, as it were, from hand to hand through a long series of ages. For what else is he doing than leading them back to the covenant that had been made with Abraham? Had he appealed to matters they had never heard of, he could never have succeeded; but their deliverance from the bondage in which they were held must have been a fact familiar and universally known, the mere mention of which would immediately have aroused everyone's attention. It is likely, moreover, that they were intimately acquainted with the whole span of those four hundred years. Now, if Moses — who comes so much earlier than all other writers — traces the tradition of his teaching from so remote a period, it is obvious how far the Holy Scriptures must surpass all other writings in point of antiquity.

4¶

Some, perhaps, may prefer to credit the Egyptians when they carry their own antiquity back six thousand years before the world was created. But their chatter, which even some profane authors have held up to ridicule, can hardly need refuting by me. Josephus, however, in his work against Appion, produces important passages from very ancient writers which imply that the doctrine delivered in the law was renowned among all nations from the remotest ages, even though it was neither read nor accurately known. And then, so that the malicious might have no ground for suspicion and the ungodly no handle for quibbling, God has guarded against both dangers in the most effective way possible. When Moses reports the words that Jacob, under divine inspiration, spoke about his descendants almost three hundred years before, how does he honor his own tribe? He brands it with everlasting infamy in the person of Levi. "Simon and Levi," says he, "are brethren; instruments of cruelty are in their habitations. O my soul, come not thou into their secret; unto their assembly mine honour be not thou united" (Gen. 49:5, 6). He certainly might have passed over this stigma in silence, not only to spare his own ancestor, but also to save himself and his whole family from a share of the disgrace. How can any suspicion attach to a man who, by willingly proclaiming that the first founder of his family had been declared detestable by a divine oracle, neither consults his own private interest nor shrinks from incurring reproach among his own tribe, who must have been offended by his statement of the fact? Again, when he reports the wicked murmuring of his brother Aaron and his sister Miriam (Numb. 12:1), shall we say that he spoke his own natural feelings, or that he obeyed the command of the Holy Spirit? Moreover, when he was invested with supreme authority, why does he not bestow the office of High Priest on his sons, instead of consigning them to the lowest place? I touch on only a few points out of many; but the Law itself contains numerous proofs throughout, which fully vindicate the credibility of Moses and place it beyond dispute that he was in truth a messenger sent forth from God.

6¶

For it is also worth remarking that the miracles he relates are bound up with disagreeable circumstances, which would have provoked opposition from the whole body of the people if there had been the smallest ground for it. Hence it is obvious that they were induced to assent merely because they had already been convinced by their own experience. But because the fact was too clear to leave it free for heathen writers to deny that Moses did perform miracles, the father of lies suggested a calumny and ascribed them to magic (Exod. 9:11). Yet with what plausibility is a charge of magic brought against a man who held it in such abhorrence that he ordered everyone who should consult soothsayers and magicians to be stoned? (Lev. 20:27). Assuredly, no impostor deals in tricks without studying to raise his own reputation by amazing the common people. But what does Moses do? By crying out that he and Aaron his brother are nothing (Exod. 16:7), that they merely execute what God has commanded, he clears himself from every approach to suspicion. Again, if the facts are considered in themselves, what kind of incantation could cause manna to rain down from heaven every day, and in quantity sufficient to maintain a whole people, while anyone who gathered more than the appointed measure saw his incredulity divinely punished as it turned to worms? To this we may add that God then allowed his servant to be subjected to so many serious trials that the ungodly cannot now gain anything by their clamor. When the people, as often happened, rose up against him proudly and petulantly, when individuals conspired and attempted to overthrow him, how could any impostures have enabled him to elude their rage? The event plainly shows that by these means his doctrine was attested to all succeeding ages.

7¶

Moreover, it is impossible to deny that he was guided by a prophetic spirit when, in the person of Jacob, he assigned the first place to the tribe of Judah — especially if we take into view the fact itself, as explained by the event. Suppose that Moses was the inventor of the prophecy; still, after he committed it to writing, four hundred years pass away during which no mention is made of a sceptre in the tribe of Judah. After Saul is anointed, the kingly office seems fixed in the tribe of Benjamin (1 Sam. 11:15 ; 16:13). When David is anointed by Samuel, what apparent ground is there for the transfer? Who could have looked for a king out of the plebeian family of a herdsman? And out of seven brothers, who could have thought that the honor was destined for the youngest? And then by what means did he afterward come within reach of the throne? Who would dare say that his anointing was regulated by human art, or skill, or prudence, and was not rather the fulfillment of a divine prophecy? In the same way, do not the predictions of the admission of the Gentiles into the divine covenant — obscure as they are, and not fulfilled until almost two thousand years later — make it palpable that Moses spoke under divine inspiration? I omit other predictions which so plainly betoken divine revelation that all people of sound mind must see they were spoken by God. In short, his Song itself (Deut. 32) is a bright mirror in which God is manifestly seen.

8¶

In the case of the other prophets the evidence is even clearer. I will select only a few examples, for it would be far too tedious to enumerate them all. Isaiah, in his own day, when the kingdom of Judah was at peace and had even some ground to confide in the protection of the Chaldeans, spoke of the destruction of the city and the captivity of the people (Isaiah 55:1). And supposing it were not sufficient evidence of divine inspiration to foretell, many years beforehand, events which at the time seemed fabulous but which ultimately turned out to be true — whence shall it be said that the prophecies he uttered concerning their return proceeded, if it was not from God? He names Cyrus, by whom the Chaldeans were to be subdued and the people restored to freedom. After the prophet thus spoke, more than a hundred years elapsed before Cyrus was born, that being nearly the interval between the death of the one and the birth of the other. It was impossible at that time to guess that some Cyrus would arise to make war on the Babylonians and, after subduing their powerful monarchy, put an end to the captivity of the children of Israel. Does not this simple, unadorned narrative plainly demonstrate that what Isaiah spoke was not the conjecture of man, but the undoubted oracle of God? Again, when Jeremiah, a considerable time before the people were led away, assigned seventy years as the period of the captivity and fixed the date of their liberation and return, must not his tongue have been guided by the Spirit of God? What effrontery it would be to deny that by these evidences the authority of the prophets is established, when the very thing to which they appeal in support of their credibility has been fulfilled! "Behold, the former things are come to pass, and new things do I declare; before they spring forth I tell you of them" (Isaiah 42:9). I say nothing of the agreement between Jeremiah and Ezekiel, who, living so far apart and yet prophesying at the same time, harmonize as completely in all they say as if they had dictated the words to one another. What shall I say of Daniel? Did he not deliver prophecies embracing a future period of almost six hundred years, as if he had been writing of past events generally known? (Dan. 9 , &c). If the pious will duly meditate on these things, they will be sufficiently instructed to silence the cavils of the ungodly. The demonstration is too clear to be gainsaid.

9¶

I am aware of what is muttered in corners by certain miscreants when they would display their acuteness in assailing divine truth. They ask: how do we know that Moses and the prophets wrote the books which now bear their names? Nay, they even dare to question whether there ever was a Moses at all. Were anyone to question whether there ever was a Plato, or an Aristotle, or a Cicero, would not the rod or the whip be deemed the fit chastisement of such folly? The law of Moses has been wonderfully preserved, more by divine providence than by human care; and though, owing to the negligence of the priests, it lay buried for a short time, from the time when it was found by good King Josiah (2 Kings 22:8 ; 2 Chron. 34:15) it has continued in the hands of men, transmitted in unbroken succession from generation to generation. Nor, indeed, when Josiah brought it forth, was it a book unknown or new; it was one that had always been a matter of notoriety and was then in full remembrance. The original writing had been deposited in the temple, and a copy taken from it had been deposited in the royal archives (Deut. 17:18, 19). The only thing that had occurred was that the priests had ceased to publish the law itself in due form, and the people also had neglected their customary reading of it. I may add that scarcely an age passed during which its authority was not confirmed and renewed. Were the books of Moses unknown to those who had the Psalms of David in their hands? To sum the whole thing up in one word, it is certain beyond dispute that these writings passed down, if I may so express it, from hand to hand, transmitted in an unbroken series from the fathers, who either heard them spoken with their own ears or learned them from those who had, while the remembrance of them was still fresh.

10¶

An objection taken from the history of the Maccabees (1 Macc. 1:57, 58) to impugn the credibility of Scripture is, on the contrary, fitted in the best possible way to confirm it. First, however, let us clear away the gloss which is put upon it; having done so, we shall turn the engine they erect against us back upon themselves. Since Antiochus ordered all the books of Scripture to be burnt, it is asked where the copies we now have came from. I ask in my turn: in what workshop could they have been so quickly fabricated? It is certain that they were in existence the moment the persecution ceased, and that they were acknowledged without dispute by all the pious who had been educated in their doctrine and were familiarly acquainted with them. Nay, while all the wicked insulted the Jews so wantonly, as if they had leagued together for the purpose, not one ever dared to charge them with having introduced spurious books. Whatever, in their opinion, the Jewish religion might be, they acknowledged that Moses was the founder of it. What, then, do those babblers do but betray their snarling petulance in falsely alleging the spuriousness of books whose sacred antiquity is proved by the consent of all history? But rather than spend labor in vain refuting these vile calumnies, let us attend instead to the care which the Lord took to preserve his Word, when against all hope he rescued it from the truculence of a most cruel tyrant as from the very midst of the flames — inspiring pious priests and others with such constancy that they did not hesitate, though it had to be purchased at the expense of their lives, to transmit this treasure to posterity, and so defeating the keenest search of the prefects and their satellites.

Who does not recognize it as a signal and miraculous work of God that those sacred monuments, which the ungodly had persuaded themselves had utterly perished, immediately returned to resume their former rights, and indeed in greater honor? For the Greek translation appeared, to disseminate them over the whole world. Nor does it seem so wonderful that God rescued the tables of his covenant from the sanguinary edicts of Antiochus as that they remained safe and entire amid the manifold disasters by which the Jewish nation was from time to time crushed, devastated, and almost exterminated. The Hebrew language was held in no estimation and was almost unknown; and assuredly, had God not provided for religion, it must have utterly perished. For it is obvious from the prophetical writings of that age how much the Jews, after their return from the captivity, had lost the genuine use of their native tongue. It is of importance to attend to this, because the comparison more clearly establishes the antiquity of the Law and the Prophets. And whom did God employ to preserve the doctrine of salvation contained in the Law and the Prophets, that Christ might manifest it in its own time? The Jews, the bitterest enemies of Christ; and hence Augustine justly calls them the librarians of the Christian Church, because they supplied us with books of which they themselves had not the use.

11¶

When we proceed to the New Testament, how solid are the pillars by which its truth is supported! Three evangelists give their narrative in a mean and humble style. The proud often eye this simplicity with disdain, because they attend not to the principal heads of doctrine; for from these they might easily infer that these evangelists treat of heavenly mysteries beyond the capacity of man. Those who have the least particle of candor must be ashamed of their own fastidiousness when they read the first chapter of Luke. Even our Savior's discourses, of which a summary is given by these three evangelists, ought to prevent anyone from treating their writings with contempt. John, again, thundering in majesty, strikes down the petulance of those who refuse to submit to the obedience of faith more powerfully than any thunderbolt. Let all those acute censors come forward, whose highest pleasure it is to banish a reverential regard for Scripture from their own hearts and from other men's; let them read the Gospel of John, and, willing or unwilling, they will find a thousand sentences which will at the very least arouse them from their sloth — nay, which will burn into their consciences as with a hot iron and check their derision. The same thing may be said of Peter and Paul, whose writings, though the greater part of readers go through them blindfold, exhibit a heavenly majesty which in a manner binds and rivets every reader. But one circumstance, sufficient of itself to exalt their doctrine above the world, is this: that Matthew, who had formerly been fixed down to his money table, and Peter and John, who were employed with their little boats, being all rude and illiterate, had never learned in any human school what they delivered to others. Paul, moreover, who had not only been an avowed but a cruel and bloody foe, was changed into a new man, and shows by that sudden and unhoped-for change that a heavenly power had compelled him to preach the doctrine which once he destroyed. Let those dogs deny that the Holy Spirit descended upon the apostles; or, if not, let them refuse credit to the history — still the very circumstances proclaim that the Holy Spirit must have been the teacher of those who, formerly contemptible among the people, all of a sudden began to discourse so magnificently of heavenly mysteries.

12¶

Add, moreover, that for the best of reasons the consent of the Church is not without its weight. For it is not to be accounted of no consequence that, from the first publication of Scripture, so many ages have uniformly concurred in yielding obedience to it, and that, notwithstanding the many extraordinary attempts which Satan and the whole world have made to oppress and overthrow it, or to efface it completely from the memory of men, it has flourished like the palm tree and continued invincible. Though in old times there was scarcely a sophist or orator of any note who did not exert his powers against it, their efforts proved unavailing. The powers of the earth armed themselves for its destruction, but all their attempts vanished into smoke. When thus powerfully assailed on every side, how could it have resisted if it had trusted only to human aid? Nay, its divine origin is more completely established by the fact that when all human wishes were against it, it advanced by its own energy. Add that it was not a single city or a single nation that concurred in receiving and embracing it. Its authority was recognized as far and as wide as the world extends — various nations who had nothing else in common entering for this purpose into a holy league. Moreover, while we ought to attach the greatest weight to the agreement of minds so diversified, and in all other things so much at variance with each other — an agreement which a divine Providence alone could have produced — it adds no small weight to the whole when we attend to the piety of those who thus agree: not of all of them indeed, but of those in whom, as lights, God was pleased that his Church should shine.

13¶

Again, with what confidence does it become us to subscribe to a doctrine attested and confirmed by the blood of so many saints? Once they had embraced it, they did not hesitate boldly and intrepidly, and even with great alacrity, to meet death in its defense. Transmitted to us with such an earnest, who of us shall not receive it with firm and unshaken conviction? It is therefore no small proof of the authority of Scripture that it was sealed with the blood of so many witnesses — especially when it is considered that in bearing testimony to the faith they met death not with fanatical enthusiasm, as erring spirits are sometimes wont to do, but with a firm and constant, yet sober and godly, zeal. There are other reasons, neither few nor feeble, by which the dignity and majesty of the Scriptures may not only be proved to the pious but also completely vindicated against the cavils of slanderers. These, however, cannot of themselves produce a firm faith in Scripture until our heavenly Father manifests his presence in it, and thereby secures an implicit reverence for it. Then only, therefore, does Scripture suffice to give a saving knowledge of God: when its certainty is founded on the inward persuasion of the Holy Spirit. Still, the human testimonies which go to confirm it will not be without effect, if they are used in subordination to that chief and highest proof, as secondary helps to our weakness. But it is foolish to attempt to prove to infidels that the Scripture is the Word of God. This it cannot be known to be, except by faith. Justly, therefore, does Augustine remind us that every man who would have any understanding in such high matters must previously possess piety and mental peace.

Calvin grants that reason can pile up evidence for Scripture's truth — its antiquity, its fulfilled prophecy, its unadorned majesty that pierces marrow as no orator can, its preservation through tyrants' flames, the blood of martyrs who died for it sober and unshaken — yet he is careful to put each proof in its place: such helps confirm the faith the Spirit has already planted, but they cannot manufacture it in an unbelieving heart. This order matters for us. In an age that demands every conviction be argued into existence, we should not be ashamed of the evidences; let our young people see how the Word stands where Demosthenes and Cicero fade, how its witnesses met death with steady joy. But teach them, too, that no argument births faith. Use the proofs as secondary helps to our weakness, never as the foundation. The same Word that survived Antiochus and Rome will outlast every modern scoffer — read it expecting God himself to manifest his presence in it.
