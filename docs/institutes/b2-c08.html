Exposition of the Moral Law

Three things always to be attended to in ascertaining and expounding the meaning of the Moral Law, sec. 6–12. III. Exposition of the Moral Law, or the Ten Commandments, sec. 13–15. IV. The end for which the whole Law is intended—viz. to teach not only elementary principles, but perfection, sec. 51, to the end of the chapter.

1¶

I think it will not be out of place here to introduce the Ten Commandments of the Law and to give a brief exposition of them. In this way it will be made clearer that the worship which God originally prescribed is still in force (a point I have already touched on); and then a second point will be confirmed, namely, that the Jews not only learned from the law what true piety consisted in, but, from feeling their inability to keep it, were overawed by the fear of judgment and so drawn, even against their will, toward the Mediator. In giving a summary of what constitutes the true knowledge of God, This chapter is connected with Book 1, chapters 1 and 2, and with Book 2, chapters 1–6. See also Book 2, chapter 2, section 22.

2¶

It is now easy to understand the teaching of the law, namely, that God, as our Creator, has the right to be regarded by us as a Father and Master, and ought therefore to receive from us fear, love, reverence, and glory; indeed, that we are not our own, to follow whatever course passion dictates, but are bound to obey him without reserve and to rest entirely in his good pleasure. Again, the Law teaches that justice and uprightness are a delight to him and injustice an abomination, and therefore, if we would not revolt from our Maker in wicked ingratitude, our whole life must be spent in the pursuit of righteousness. For if we show fitting reverence only when we prefer his will to our own, it follows that the only legitimate service we can render him is the practice of justice, purity, and holiness. Nor can we plead as an excuse that we lack the power and, like debtors whose means are exhausted, are unable to pay. We cannot be allowed to measure the glory of God by our own ability. Whatever we may be, he always remains like himself, the friend of righteousness and the enemy of unrighteousness; and whatever his demands on us may be, since he can require only what is right, we are necessarily under a natural obligation to obey. Our inability to do so is our own fault. If lust, in which sin holds its dominion, so enslaves us that we are not free to obey our Father, there is no ground for pleading necessity as a defense, since this evil necessity lies within us and must be charged to ourselves.

3¶

When we have advanced this far under the guidance of the Law, we must, under the same guidance, go on to descend into ourselves. In this way we at length arrive at two results. First, comparing our conduct with the righteousness of the Law, we see how very far it is from agreeing with the will of God, and therefore how unworthy we are to hold our place among his creatures, much less to be counted his sons. Second, taking a survey of our powers, we see that they are not only unequal to fulfilling the Law, but are altogether nothing. The necessary result must be to produce distrust of our own ability, and also anxiety and trembling of mind. Conscience cannot feel the burden of its guilt without immediately turning to the judgment of God, and the sight of this judgment cannot fail to stir up a dread of death. In the same way, the proofs of our utter powerlessness must at once give birth to despair of our own strength. Both feelings produce humility and abasement, and so the sinner, terrified at the prospect of eternal death (which he sees justly hanging over him for his iniquities), turns to the mercy of God as the only haven of safety. Feeling his utter inability to pay what he owes to the Law, and so despairing of himself, he bethinks himself to apply and look elsewhere for help.

4¶

But the Lord does not count it enough to inspire reverence for his justice. To fill our hearts with love for himself and, at the same time, with hatred of iniquity, he has added promises and threatenings. Because the eye of our mind is too dim to be drawn by the mere beauty of goodness, our most merciful Father has been pleased, in his great indulgence, to entice us to love and long for it by the hope of reward. He therefore declares that rewards for virtue are stored up with him, and that none who obey his commands will labor in vain. On the other hand, he proclaims not only that iniquity is hateful in his sight, but that it will not go unpunished, because he will be the avenger of his insulted majesty. To encourage us in every way, he promises present blessings as well as eternal happiness to the obedience of those who have kept his commands, while he threatens transgressors with present suffering as well as the punishment of eternal death. The promise, "Ye shall therefore keep my statutes, and my judgments; which if a man do, he shall live in them" (Leviticus 18:5), and, corresponding to it, the threatening, "The soul that sinneth, it shall die" (Ezekiel 18:4, 20), without doubt point to a future life and death, both without end. But though in every passage where the favor or anger of God is mentioned the former includes eternity of life and the latter eternal destruction, the Law at the same time lists a long catalogue of present blessings and curses (Leviticus 26:4; Deuteronomy 28:1). The threatenings bear witness to the spotless purity of God, which cannot bear iniquity, while the promises bear witness both to his infinite love of righteousness (which he cannot leave unrewarded) and to his wonderful kindness. Since we are bound to do him homage with all that we have, he is fully entitled to demand everything he requires of us as a debt; and a debt, when paid, deserves no reward. He therefore gives up his right when he holds out a reward for services which are not offered freely, as though they were not owed. What these services amount to in themselves has been partly described and will appear more clearly in its own place. For the present it is enough to remember that the promises of the Law are no small commendation of righteousness, since they show how greatly God is pleased with its observance, while the threatenings pronounced are meant to produce a greater horror of unrighteousness, so that the sinner may not indulge in the allurements of vice and forget the judgment which the divine Lawgiver has prepared for him.

5¶

The Lord, in delivering a perfect rule of righteousness, has reduced it in all its parts to his mere will, and in this way has shown that nothing is more acceptable to him than obedience. There is all the more need to attend to this because the human mind, in its willfulness, is forever inventing different modes of worship as a means of gaining his favor. This irreligious affectation of religion, being innate in the human mind, has shown itself in every age and still does so, men always longing to devise some method of obtaining righteousness without any warrant from the Word of God. 6¶

Once we have expounded the Divine Law, what was said earlier about its office and use will be understood more easily and with greater profit. But before we take up each commandment separately, it will be proper to survey the whole in general. At the outset it was proved that in the Law human life is instructed not merely in outward decency but in inward, spiritual righteousness. Though no one can deny this, very few pay proper attention to it, because they do not consider the Lawgiver, by whose character the character of the Law must also be judged. If a king were to issue an edict forbidding murder, adultery, and theft, the penalty, I admit, would not fall on the man who has only felt a longing in his mind for these vices but has not actually committed them. The reason is that a human lawgiver does not extend his care beyond outward order, and so his commands are not broken without outward acts. But God, whose eye nothing escapes, and who regards not so much the outward appearance as purity of heart, includes under the prohibition of murder, adultery, and theft also wrath, hatred, lust, covetousness, and everything else of the same kind. Being a spiritual Lawgiver, he speaks to the soul no less than to the body. The murder which the soul commits is wrath and hatred; its theft is covetousness and avarice; and its adultery is lust. It may be objected that human laws have regard to intentions and wishes, and not to chance events. I admit this, but then these must show themselves outwardly. Such laws consider the animus with which the act was done, but they do not search out the secret thoughts. Accordingly, their demand is satisfied when the hand merely refrains from transgression. By contrast, since the law of heaven is enacted for our minds, the first thing necessary to a proper keeping of the Law is to put them under restraint. But most men, even while they are most anxious to hide their disregard of the Law, only train their hands and feet and other parts of their body to some kind of observance, while all the time they keep the heart utterly estranged from anything like obedience. They think it enough to have carefully hidden from men what they are doing in the sight of God. Hearing the commandments, "Thou shalt not kill," "Thou shalt not commit adultery," "Thou shalt not steal," they do not unsheathe their sword for slaughter, nor defile their bodies with harlots, nor lay their hands on other men's goods. So far, so good; but with their whole soul they breathe out slaughter, boil with lust, cast a greedy eye on their neighbor's property, and devour it in their wishes. Here the chief thing the Law requires is missing. Where, then, does this gross stupidity come from, if not from the fact that they lose sight of the Lawgiver and form an idea of righteousness to suit their own disposition? Against this Paul strongly protests when he declares that the "law is spiritual" (Romans 7:14), meaning that it not only demands the homage of the soul, and mind, and will, but requires an angelic purity which, cleansed from all filthiness of the flesh, savors only of the Spirit.

7¶

In saying that this is the meaning of the Law, we are not introducing a new interpretation of our own; we are following Christ, the best interpreter of the Law (Matthew 5:22, 28, 44). Since the Pharisees had instilled into the people the false idea that the Law was fulfilled by anyone who did nothing against the Law in outward act, he pronounces this a most dangerous delusion, and declares that an immodest look is adultery and that hatred of a brother is murder. "Whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause, shall be in danger of the judgment"; whoever by whispering or murmuring shows that he has taken offense "shall be in danger of the council"; whoever gives way to open anger in reproaches and evil-speaking "shall be in danger of hell-fire." Those who have not grasped this have imagined that Christ was only a second Moses, the giver of an evangelical law to make up the deficiency of the Mosaic Law. Hence the common axiom about the perfection of the Evangelical Law and its great superiority to that of Moses. This idea is in many ways most harmful. For it will appear from Moses himself, when we come to summarize his precepts, that great dishonor is thereby done to the Divine Law. It certainly suggests that the holiness of the fathers under the Law was little more than hypocrisy, and it leads us away from that one unchanging rule of righteousness. It is very easy, however, to refute this error, which rests on the supposition that Christ added to the Law, whereas he only restored it to its integrity, maintaining and purifying it when it had been obscured by the falsehood and defiled by the leaven of the Pharisees.

8¶

The next observation we would make is that there is always more in the commands and prohibitions of the Law than is expressed in words. This, however, must be understood in such a way as not to turn it into a kind of Lesbian code; "Ne sit nobis Lesbiæ regulæ," omitted in the French.

9¶

What is touched on here somewhat obscurely will become perfectly clear as we go on and grow familiar with the exposition of the Commandments. It is enough to have mentioned the subject in this way; but perhaps our last statement needs to be briefly confirmed, since otherwise it might not be understood, or, even if understood, might at first seem unsound. There is no need to prove that when good is commanded, the evil opposed to it is forbidden. Everyone admits this. It will also be granted without much difficulty that when evil is forbidden, its opposite is commanded. Indeed, it is a common saying that the censure of vice is the commendation of virtue. We, however, ask for somewhat more than is commonly understood by these expressions. When the particular virtue opposed to a particular vice is spoken of, all that is usually meant is abstaining from that vice. We maintain that it goes further and means the opposite duties and positive acts. So most men will take the commandment, "Thou shalt not kill," merely as a command to abstain from all injury and from every wish to inflict injury. I hold that it means, besides this, that we are to help our neighbor's life by every means in our power. And so as not to assert this without giving my reasons, I prove it as follows. God forbids us to injure or hurt a brother because he wants his life to be dear and precious to us; and therefore, when he forbids this, he at the same time demands all the offices of love which can contribute to his preservation.

10¶

But why did God deliver his commandments in this way, by halves as it were, using condensed expressions that carry a larger meaning than what is actually expressed? Other reasons are given, but the following seems to me the best. Since the flesh is always on the alert to lessen the heinousness of sin (unless it is made, as it were, perceptible to the touch) and to cover it with plausible pretexts, the Lord sets forth by way of example whatever is foulest and most wicked in each kind of transgression, so that the very statement of it might make the hearer shudder and impress on his mind a deeper horror of sin. In weighing sins we are often deceived by imagining that the more hidden they are, the less heinous they are. The Lord dispels this delusion by training us to refer the whole multitude of sins to particular heads, which show admirably how great a degree of heinousness there is in each. For example, wrath and hatred do not seem so very bad when they are called by their own names; but when they are forbidden under the name of murder, we understand better how abominable they are in the sight of God, who places them in the same class with that horrible crime. Guided by his judgment, we accustom ourselves to judge more accurately of the heinousness of offenses which before seemed trivial.

11¶

It will now be proper to consider what is meant by the division of the divine Law into Two Tables. All men of sense will judge, from the formal way in which these are sometimes mentioned, that this was not done at random or without reason. Indeed, the reason is so obvious that it does not allow us to remain in doubt about it. God divided his Law into two parts, which together contain a complete rule of righteousness, so that he might assign the first place to the duties of religion, which relate especially to his worship, and the second to the duties of love, which concern man. The first foundation of righteousness is without doubt the worship of God. When it is overthrown, all the other parts of righteousness, like a building torn apart and in ruins, are wrenched and scattered. What kind of righteousness do you call it not to commit theft and robbery, if in the meantime you rob God of his glory with wicked sacrilege? Or not to defile your body with fornication, if you profane his holy name with blasphemy? Or not to take a man's life, if you strive to cut off and destroy the remembrance of God? It is vain, therefore, to talk of righteousness apart from religion. Such righteousness has no more beauty than the trunk of a body deprived of its head. The French is "Tout ainsi comme si quelcun vouloit faire une belle monstre d'un corps sans teste"; just as if one were to try to make a beautiful monster of a body without a head.

12¶

But although the whole Law is contained under two heads, the Lord, in order to remove every pretext for excuse, has been pleased to set out more fully and explicitly in Ten Commandments everything relating to his own honor, fear, and love, as well as everything relating to the love which, for his sake, he commands us to have toward our fellow men. Nor is it an unprofitable study to consider the division of the commandments, provided we remember that it is one of those matters on which every man should have full freedom of judgment, and over which a difference of opinion should not lead to strife. We are, in fact, obliged to make this observation, so that the division we are about to adopt may not arouse the surprise or ridicule of the reader as something novel or recently invented.

There is no room for controversy over the fact that the Law is divided into ten heads, since this is repeatedly confirmed by divine authority. The question, therefore, is not about the number of the parts, but about the method of dividing them. Those who adopt a division that gives three commandments to the First Table and throws the remaining seven into the Second strike the commandment about images out of the list, or at least hide it under the first, though there cannot be any doubt that the Lord set it down distinctly as a separate commandment; while the tenth, which forbids coveting what belongs to our neighbor, they absurdly break into two. Moreover, it will soon appear that this method of dividing was unknown in a purer age. Others count four commandments in the First Table, as we do, but for the first they set down the introductory promise without adding the precept. But because I must hold, unless I am convinced by clear evidence to the contrary, that the "ten words" mentioned by Moses are Ten Commandments, and because I see that number arranged in a most admirable order, I must, while leaving them to hold their own opinion, follow what seems to me better established, namely, that what they make the first commandment is in the nature of a preface to the whole Law, and that after it follow four commandments in the First Table and six in the Second, in the order in which they will be reviewed here. Origen adopts this division without discussion, as though it had been everywhere received in his day. 13¶

Whether you take the first sentence as part of the commandment or read it separately is to me a matter of indifference, provided you grant that it is a kind of preface to the whole Law. In making laws, the first thing to guard against is that they be immediately set aside through contempt. The Lord therefore takes care, in the first place, that this shall not happen to the Law he is about to deliver, by introducing it with a threefold sanction. He claims for himself the power and authority to command, in order to impress on the chosen people the necessity of obedience; he holds out a promise of favor as a means of drawing them to the pursuit of holiness; and he reminds them of his kindness, in order to convict them of ingratitude if they fail to make a fitting return. The name Lord denotes power and lawful dominion. If all things are from him and hold together by him, they ought in justice to be referred to him, as Paul says (Romans 11:36). This name, therefore, is in itself enough to bring us under the authority of the divine majesty, for it would be monstrous for us to want to withdraw from the dominion of him apart from whom we cannot even exist.

14¶

After showing that he has the right to command and to be obeyed, he next, so as not to seem to drag men by sheer necessity but rather to draw them, graciously declares that he is the God of the Church. For the form of expression implies that a mutual relation is contained in the promise, "I will be their God, and they shall be my people" (Jeremiah 31:33). Hence Christ infers the immortality of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob from the fact that God had declared himself to be their God (Matthew 22:32). It is therefore the same as if he had said: I have chosen you for myself, as a people to whom I will not only do good in the present life, but will also grant happiness in the life to come. The end in view here is mentioned in the Law in various passages. For when the Lord stoops in mercy to honor us so far as to admit us into partnership with his chosen people, he chooses us, as Moses says, "to be a holy people," "a peculiar people unto himself," to "keep all his commandments" (Deuteronomy 7:6; 14:2; 26:18). Hence the exhortation, "Ye shall be holy; for I the Lord your God am holy" (Leviticus 19:2). 17¶

As in the first commandment the Lord declares that he is one, and that besides him no gods must be either worshiped or imagined, so here he declares more plainly what his nature is and what kind of worship he is to be honored with, so that we may not presume to form any carnal idea of him. The point of the commandment, therefore, is that he will not have his lawful worship profaned by superstitious rites. Accordingly, in general he calls us entirely away from the carnal, frivolous observances which our dull minds are accustomed to devise after forming some gross idea of the divine nature, while at the same time he instructs us in the worship which is lawful, namely, spiritual worship of his own appointment. The grossest vice forbidden here is outward idolatry. This commandment has two parts. The first restrains the reckless daring which would subject the incomprehensible God to our senses or represent him under any visible shape. The second forbids the worship of images on any religious ground. There is, moreover, a brief list of all the forms in which the Deity was usually represented by heathen and superstitious nations. By "any thing that is in heaven above" is meant the sun, the moon, and the stars, perhaps also birds, since in Deuteronomy, where the meaning is explained, there is mention of birds as well as stars (Deuteronomy 4:15). I would not have made this observation had I not seen that some absurdly apply it to the angels. The other particulars I pass over, as they need no explanation. We have already shown clearly enough (Book 1, chapters 11, 12) that every visible shape of Deity which man devises is directly opposed to the divine nature, and therefore that the moment idols appear, true religion is corrupted and adulterated.

18¶

The threatening added here ought to have no small effect in shaking off our sluggishness. It reads as follows:

The meaning here is the same as if he had said that our duty is to cleave to him alone. To lead us to this, he proclaims his authority, which he will not allow to be impaired or despised with impunity. It is true that the word used is El, which means God; but since it is derived from a word meaning strength, I have not hesitated, in order to express the sense more fully, to render it as inserted in the margin. Second, he calls himself jealous, because he cannot bear a partner. Third, he declares that he will vindicate his majesty and glory if any transfer it either to creatures or to graven images, and that not by a simple punishment of brief duration, but by one extending to the third and fourth generation of those who imitate the wickedness of their forefathers. In the same way, he declares his constant mercy and kindness to the remote posterity of those who love him and keep his Law. 19¶

In the threatening we must attend to what is meant when God declares that he will visit the iniquity of the fathers upon the children to the third and fourth generation. It seems inconsistent with the fairness of God's dealings to punish the innocent for another's fault; and the Lord himself declares that "the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father" (Ezekiel 18:20). Yet more than once we meet with a declaration that the punishment of the sins of fathers is deferred to future generations. Thus Moses repeatedly addresses the Lord as "visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation" (Numbers 14:18). In the same way Jeremiah says, "Thou showest loving-kindness unto thousands, and recompenses the iniquity of the fathers into the bosom of their children after them" (Jeremiah 32:18). Some, sorely perplexed about how to solve this difficulty, think it is to be understood of temporal punishments only, which, they say, sons may properly bear for the sins of their parents, because such punishments are often inflicted for their own good. This is indeed true; for Isaiah declared to Hezekiah that his children would be stripped of the kingdom and carried away into captivity for a sin which he had committed (Isaiah 39:7); and the households of Pharaoh and Abimelech were made to suffer for a wrong done to Abraham (Genesis 12:17; 20:3–18). But to try to solve the question in this way is an evasion rather than a true interpretation. For the punishment threatened here and in similar passages is too great to be confined within the limits of the present life. We must therefore understand it to mean that a curse from the Lord righteously falls not only on the head of the guilty individual but also on his whole line. When it has fallen, what can be expected but that the father, deprived of the Spirit of God, will live most shamefully; that the son, likewise forsaken by the Lord because of his father's iniquity, will follow the same road to destruction; and that he in turn will be followed by succeeding generations, forming a seed of evildoers?

20¶

First, let us examine whether such punishment is inconsistent with divine justice. If human nature is universally condemned, those to whom the Lord does not impart his grace must be doomed to destruction; yet they perish by their own iniquity, not by any unjust hatred on God's part. There is no room to complain and ask why the grace of God does not advance their salvation as it does that of others. Therefore, when God punishes the wicked and shameless for their crimes by depriving their families of his grace for many generations, who will dare to bring a charge against him for this most righteous vengeance? But, it will be said, the Lord on the contrary declares that the son shall not suffer for the father's sin (Ezekiel 18:20). Observe the drift of that passage. The Israelites, after enduring a long period of unbroken calamities, had begun to say as a proverb that their fathers had eaten the sour grape and so set the children's teeth on edge; meaning that they, though in themselves righteous and innocent, were paying the penalty for sins committed by their parents, and this more from the implacable anger of God than from his duly tempered severity. The prophet declares that it was not so: that they were punished for their own wickedness; that it did not agree with the justice of God that a righteous son should suffer for the iniquity of a wicked father; and that nothing of the kind was shown in what they suffered. For if the visitation of which we now speak is carried out when God withdraws from the children of the wicked the light of his truth and the other helps to salvation, the only way in which they are cursed for their fathers' wickedness is in being blinded and abandoned by God, and so left to walk in their parents' footsteps. The misery they suffer in time, and the destruction to which they are finally doomed, are thus punishments inflicted by divine justice, not for the sins of others, but for their own iniquity.

21¶

On the other hand, there is a promise of mercy to thousands, a promise frequently mentioned in Scripture and forming an article of the solemn covenant made with the Church: I will be "a God unto thee, and to thy seed after thee" (Genesis 17:7). With reference to this Solomon says, "The just man walketh in his integrity: his children are blessed after him" (Proverbs 20:7); not only as a result of a religious upbringing (though this is certainly by no means unimportant), but as a result of the blessing promised in the covenant, namely, that the divine favor will dwell forever in the families of the righteous. Here is excellent comfort for believers and great ground of terror for the wicked; for if, after death, the mere remembrance of righteousness and iniquity has such an influence on God's dealings that his blessing rests on the posterity of the righteous and his curse on the posterity of the wicked, much more must it rest on the heads of the individuals themselves. Notwithstanding this, however, the offspring of the wicked sometimes turn out better, while those of believers degenerate; because the Almighty has not laid down here an inflexible rule which might detract from his free election. For the comfort of the righteous and the dismay of the sinner, it is enough that the threatening itself is not vain or empty, even though it does not always take effect. For just as the temporal punishments inflicted on a few of the wicked are proofs of the divine wrath against sin and of the future judgment that will finally overtake all sinners, though many escape unpunished even to the end of their lives, so, when the Lord gives one example of blessing a son for his father's sake by visiting him in mercy and kindness, it is a proof of constant and unfailing favor toward his worshipers. On the other hand, when in any single instance he visits the iniquity of the father on the son, he gives notice of the judgment which awaits all the reprobate for their own iniquities. The certainty of this is the chief thing taught here. Moreover, the Lord, as it were in passing, commends the riches of his mercy by extending it to thousands, while he limits his vengeance to four generations.

THOU SHALT NOT TAKE THE NAME OF THE LORD THY GOD IN VAIN.

22¶

The point of this Commandment is that the majesty of the name of God is to be held sacred. In sum, therefore, it means that we must not profane it by using it irreverently or contemptuously. This prohibition implies a corresponding precept, namely, that we make it our study and care to treat his name with religious veneration. It is therefore fitting for us to govern our minds and our tongues so as never to think or speak of God and his mysteries without reverence and great soberness, and never, in weighing his works, to have any feeling toward him but one of deep veneration. We must, I say, steadily observe the following three things. First, whatever our mind conceives of him and whatever our tongue utters must reflect his excellence and correspond to the loftiness of his sacred name; in short, it must be fitted to extol its greatness. Second, we must not rashly and perversely twist his sacred word and adorable mysteries to serve ambition, or greed, or amusement, but, since they bear the stamp of his dignity, we must always keep them in due honor and esteem. Last, we must not disparage or cast reproach on his works, as wretched men are accustomed to do insultingly, but must praise every action we attribute to him as wise, and just, and good. This is to sanctify the name of God. When we act otherwise, his name is profaned by vain and wicked abuse, because it is applied to a purpose foreign to the one to which it is consecrated. Even if there were nothing worse, in being stripped of its dignity it is gradually brought into contempt. But if there is so much evil in the rash and untimely use of the divine name, there is still more evil in its being used for wicked purposes, as is done by those who use it in necromancy, cursing, unlawful exorcisms, and other impious incantations. But the Commandment refers especially to oaths, in which a perverse use of the divine name is particularly detestable; and it does this to deter us all the more effectively from every kind of profanation. That what is commanded here concerns the worship of God and the reverence due to his name, and not the fairness which men are to practice toward one another, is evident from this: afterwards, in the Second Table, there is a condemnation of the perjury and false testimony by which human society is harmed, and the repetition would be superfluous if this Commandment dealt with the duty of love. Moreover, this is necessary even for the sake of distinction, because, as was observed, God has for good reason divided his Law into two tables. The inference, then, is that God here vindicates his own right and defends his sacred name, but does not teach the duties which men owe to men.

23¶

In the first place, we must consider what an oath is. An oath, then, is calling God to witness that what we say is true. Curses, being plainly insulting to God, do not deserve to be classed among oaths. That an oath, when properly taken, is a kind of divine worship appears from many passages of Scripture. So when Isaiah prophesies of the admission of the Assyrians and Egyptians to a share in the covenant, he says, "In that day shall five cities in the land of Egypt speak the language of Canaan, and swear to the Lord of hosts" (Isaiah 19:18). Swearing by the name of the Lord here means that they will make a profession of religion. In the same way, speaking of the extension of the Redeemer's kingdom, it is said, "He who blesseth himself in the earth shall bless himself in the God of truth: and he that sweareth in the earth shall swear by the God of truth" (Isaiah 65:16). In Jeremiah it is said, "If they will diligently learn the ways of my people, to swear by my name, The Lord liveth; as they taught my people to swear by Baal; then shall they be built in the midst of my people" (Jeremiah 12:16). By appealing to the name of the Lord and calling him to witness, we are rightly said to declare our own religious veneration of him. For in this way we acknowledge that he is eternal and unchangeable truth, since we call upon him not only in preference to others as a fit witness to the truth, but as its only defender, able to bring hidden things to light, a discerner of hearts. When human testimony fails, we appeal to God as witness, especially when the matter to be proved lies hidden in the conscience. For this reason the Lord is grievously offended with those who swear by strange gods, and treats such swearing as a proof of open revolt: "Thy children have forsaken me, and sworn by them that are no gods" (Jeremiah 5:7). The heinousness of the offense is shown by the punishment pronounced against it: "I will cut off them that swear by the Lord, and that swear by Malcham" (Zephaniah 1:4, 5).

24¶

Since we understand that the Lord wants our oaths to be a kind of divine worship, we must be all the more careful that, instead of worship, they do not contain insult, or contempt, and disparagement. It is no slight insult to swear by him falsely; hence in the Law this is called profanation (Leviticus 19:12). For if God is robbed of his truth, what is left? Without truth he could not be God. But he is certainly robbed of his truth when he is made the approver and witness of what is false. Hence, when Joshua is trying to make Achan confess the truth, he says, "My son, give, I pray thee, glory to the Lord God of Israel" (Joshua 7:19), indicating that grievous dishonor is done to God when men swear falsely by him. And no wonder; for, as far as lies in them, his sacred name is in a manner branded with falsehood. That this form of expression was common among the Jews whenever anyone was called on to take an oath is evident from a similar solemn charge used by the Pharisees, as given in John (John 9:24). Scripture reminds us of the caution we ought to use by employing such expressions as the following: "As the Lord liveth"; "God do so and more also"; "I call God for a record upon my soul." 1 Samuel 14:44; 2 Kings 5:31; 2 Corinthians 1:23.

25¶

The name of God is cheapened and disparaged when it is used in oaths which, though true, are superfluous. This, too, is to take his name in vain. It is not enough, therefore, to abstain from perjury, unless we also remember that an oath is not appointed or allowed for passion or pleasure, but for necessity; and that he who uses it on any but necessary occasions therefore makes a licentious use of it. Moreover, no case of necessity can be claimed except where some purpose of religion or love is to be served. In this matter great sin is committed in the present day, a sin all the more intolerable in that its frequency has made it cease to be regarded as a fault, though it is certainly not counted trivial before the judgment seat of God. The name of God is everywhere profaned by being thrown indiscriminately into frivolous talk; and the evil is disregarded because it has long and brazenly been persisted in with impunity. The commandment of the Lord, however, stands; the penalty also stands, and will one day take effect. Special vengeance will be executed on those who have taken the name of God in vain. 26¶

The Anabaptists, not content with this moderate use of oaths, condemn all oaths without exception, on the ground of our Savior's general prohibition, "I say unto you, Swear not at all"; "Let your speech be Yea, yea; Nay, nay: for whatsoever is more than these cometh of evil" (Matthew 5:34; James 5:12). In this way they thoughtlessly make Christ a stumbling stone, setting him against the Father, as if he had come down into the world to annul his decrees. In the Law the Almighty not only permits an oath as a lawful thing (which would be quite enough), but in a case of necessity actually commands it (Exodus 22:11). Christ, again, declares that he and his Father are one; that he delivers only what his Father commanded; that his doctrine is not his own, but his that sent him (John 10:18, 30; 7:16). What then? Will they make God contradict himself by approving and commanding at one time what he afterwards forbids and condemns? But since there is some difficulty in what our Savior says on the subject of swearing, it may be proper to consider it a little. Here, however, we shall never arrive at the true meaning unless we attend to Christ's purpose and to the subject he is treating. His purpose was neither to relax nor to cut short the Law, but to restore its true and genuine meaning, which had been greatly corrupted by the false glosses of the Scribes and Pharisees. If we attend to this, we shall not suppose that Christ condemned all oaths, but only those which broke the rule of the Law. It is evident from the oaths themselves that the people were accustomed to think it enough if they avoided perjury, whereas the Law forbids not perjury only, but also vain and superfluous oaths. Therefore our Lord, who is the best interpreter of the Law, reminds them that there is sin not only in perjury but in swearing. How in swearing? Namely, in swearing vainly. Those oaths, however, which are authorized by the Law he leaves safe and free. Those who condemn oaths think their argument unanswerable when they seize on the expression, not at all. But the expression applies not to the word swear, but to the forms of oaths added after it. For part of the error lay in their supposing that when they swore by heaven and earth they did not touch the name of God. The Lord, therefore, after cutting off the chief source of evasion, deprives them of every subterfuge, warning them not to suppose that they escape guilt by suppressing the name of God and appealing to heaven and earth. For it should be observed here in passing that, although the name of God is not expressed, men still swear by him when they use indirect forms, as when they swear by the light of life, by the bread they eat, by their baptism, or by any other pledges of God's generosity toward them. Some wrongly suppose that our Savior in that passage rebukes superstition by forbidding men to swear by heaven and earth and Jerusalem. Rather, he refutes the sophistical subtlety of those who thought it nothing to utter indirect oaths vainly, imagining that in this way they spared the holy name of God, whereas that name is inscribed on every one of his mercies. The case is different when any mortal, living or dead, or an angel is put in the place of God, as in the vile form devised by flattery among heathen nations, "By the life or genius of the king"; for in this case the false deification obscures and impairs the glory of the one God. But when nothing else is intended than to confirm what is said by an appeal to the holy name of God, then, even though it is done indirectly, his majesty is insulted by all frivolous oaths. Christ strips this abuse of every vain pretext when he says, "Swear not at all." To the same effect is the passage in which James uses the words of our Savior quoted above (James 5:12). For this rash swearing has always prevailed in the world, even though it is a profanation of the name of God. If you refer the words "not at all" to the act itself, as though every oath without exception were unlawful, what will be the use of the explanation which immediately follows, "Neither by heaven, neither by the earth," and so on? These words make it clear that the aim was to answer the quibbles by which the Jews thought they could lessen their fault.

27¶

Everyone of sound judgment must now see that in that passage our Lord merely condemned those oaths which were forbidden by the Law. For he who in his life displayed a model of the perfection he taught did not object to oaths whenever the occasion required them; and the disciples, who no doubt obeyed their Master in all things, followed the same rule. Who will dare to say that Paul would have sworn (Romans 1:9; 2 Corinthians 1:23) if an oath had been altogether forbidden? But when the occasion calls for it, he adjures without any scruple, and sometimes even calls down a curse. The question, however, is not yet settled. For some think that the only oaths exempted from the prohibition are public oaths, such as those administered to us by the magistrate, or those independent states use in ratifying treaties, or those the people take when they swear allegiance to their sovereign, or the soldier's military oath, and others of the same kind. To this class they refer (and rightly) those solemn declarations in the writings of Paul which assert the dignity of the Gospel, since the Apostles, in carrying out their office, were not private individuals but the public servants of God. I certainly do not deny that such oaths are the safest, because they are most strongly supported by passages of Scripture. The magistrate is commanded, in a doubtful matter, to put the witness on oath, and the witness in turn to answer on oath; and an Apostle says that in this way there is an end of all strife (Hebrews 6:16). In this commandment both parties are fully approved. Indeed, we may observe that among the ancient heathen a public and solemn oath was held in great reverence, while the common oaths used indiscriminately were held in little or no regard, as though they thought that the Deity did not intervene in them. To condemn private oaths used soberly, sacredly, and reverently on necessary occasions would be dangerous, supported as they are by reason and example. For if private individuals are allowed, in a grave and serious matter, to appeal to God as a judge, much more may they appeal to him as a witness. Your brother charges you with treachery. You, as bound by the duties of love, labor to clear yourself of the charge. He will on no account be satisfied. If, through his obstinate malice, your good name is put in jeopardy, you may appeal without offense to the judgment of God, that he may in time make your innocence plain. If the terms are weighed, it will be found that to call on him as witness is a lesser thing; and so I do not see how it can be called unlawful to do so. Nor is there any lack of examples. If it is claimed that the oath which Abraham and Isaac made with Abimelech was of a public nature, the one by which Jacob and Laban bound themselves in a mutual league was private. Boaz, though a private man, confirmed his promise of marriage to Ruth in the same way. Obadiah, too, a just man who feared God, though a private individual, affirms with an oath when he seeks to persuade Elijah. Genesis 21:24; 26:31; 31:53; Ruth 3:13; 1 Kings 18:10.

REMEMBER THE SABBATH DAY TO KEEP IT HOLY. SIX DAYS SHALT THOU LABOUR AND DO ALL THY WORK: BUT THE SEVENTH DAY IS THE SABBATH OF THE LORD THY GOD. IN IT THOU SHALT NOT DO ANY WORK, &C.

28¶

The point of the commandment is that, being dead to our own desires and works, we should meditate on the kingdom of God, and, for the sake of such meditation, make use of the means which he has appointed. But since this commandment stands in peculiar circumstances, apart from the others, the method of exposition must be somewhat different. Early Christian writers commonly call it typical, as containing the outward observance of a day which was abolished with the other types at the coming of Christ. This is indeed true, but it leaves half the matter untouched. We must therefore look deeper for our exposition and attend to three points in which, it seems to me, the keeping of this commandment consists. First, under the rest of the seventh day the divine Lawgiver meant to give the people of Israel a type of the spiritual rest by which believers were to cease from their own works and allow God to work in them. Second, he meant that there should be a fixed day on which they would assemble to hear the Law and perform religious rites, or which, at least, they would especially devote to meditating on his works, and so be trained in piety. Third, he meant that servants, and those who lived under the authority of others, should be granted a day of rest, and so have some relief from labor.

29¶

We are taught in many passages Numbers 13:22; Ezekiel 20:12; 22:8; 23:38; Jeremiah 27:21, 22, 27; Isaiah 55:2; Nehemiah 9:14.

30¶

This complete cessation was represented to the Jews by the observance of one day in seven, which the Lord commended by his own example so that it might be kept more devoutly. For it is no small spur to man's zeal to know that he is engaged in imitating his Creator. If anyone looks for some secret meaning in the number seven, since in Scripture this is the number of perfection, it may have been chosen, not without cause, to denote perpetuity. In keeping with this, Moses ends his description of the succession of day and night on the same day on which he relates that the Lord rested from his works. Another probable reason for the number may be that the Lord intended that the Sabbath should never be completed before the arrival of the last day. Here we begin our blessed rest in him and make new progress in it daily; but because we must still wage unceasing warfare with the flesh, it will not be brought to completion until the fulfillment of the prophecy of Isaiah: "From one new moon to another, and from one sabbath to another, shall all flesh come to worship before me, saith the Lord" (Isaiah 66:23); in other words, when God shall be "all in all" (1 Corinthians 15:28). It may seem, therefore, that by the seventh day the Lord portrayed to his people the future perfection of his sabbath on the last day, so that by continual meditation on the sabbath they might aspire to this perfection throughout their whole lives.

31¶

If these remarks on the number seem somewhat far-fetched to anyone, I have no objection to his taking it more simply: that the Lord appointed a certain day on which his people might be trained, under the tutelage of the Law, to meditate constantly on the spiritual rest, and fixed on the seventh, either because he foresaw that it would be sufficient, or so that his own example might act as a stronger spur, or at least to remind men that the Sabbath was appointed for no other purpose than to make them conform to their Creator. It matters little which of these is adopted, provided we do not lose sight of the chief thing portrayed, namely, the mystery of perpetual rest from our works. To the contemplation of this the Jews were again and again called by the prophets, so that they would not think a carnal cessation from labor sufficient. Besides the passages already quoted, there is the following: "If thou turn away thy foot from the sabbath, from doing thy pleasure on my holy day; and call the sabbath a delight, the holy of the Lord, honourable; and shalt honour him, not doing thine own ways, nor finding thine own pleasure, nor speaking thine own words: then shalt thou delight thyself in the Lord" (Isaiah 58:13, 14). Still, there can be no doubt that at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ the ceremonial part of the commandment was abolished. He is the truth, at whose presence all the emblems vanish; the body, at the sight of which the shadows disappear. He, I say, is the true fulfillment of the sabbath: "We are buried with him by baptism unto death: that like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we should walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:4). Hence, as the Apostle says elsewhere, "Let no man therefore judge you in meat, or in drink, or in respect of an holiday, or of the new moon, or of the sabbath days; which are a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Christ" (Colossians 2:16, 17); meaning by body the whole substance of the truth, as is well explained in that passage. This is not content with one day, but requires the whole course of our lives, until, being completely dead to ourselves, we are filled with the life of God. Christians, therefore, should have nothing to do with a superstitious observance of days.

32¶

The two other points ought not to be classed with the ancient shadows, but are suited to every age. Though the sabbath has been abrogated, there is still room among us, first, to assemble on fixed days for hearing the Word, breaking the mystical bread, and public prayer; and second, to give our servants and laborers relief from labor. There can be no doubt that the Lord provided for both in the commandment of the Sabbath. The first is abundantly shown by the mere practice of the Jews. The second Moses has expressed in Deuteronomy in the following terms: "The seventh day is the sabbath of the Lord thy God: in it thou shalt not do any work, thou, nor thy son, nor thy daughter, nor thy man-servant, nor thy maid-servant; that thy man-servant and thy maid-servant may rest as well as thou" (Deuteronomy 5:14). Likewise in Exodus: "That thine ox and thine ass may rest, and the son of thy handmaid, and the stranger, may be refreshed" (Exodus 23:12). Who can deny that both apply to us as much as to the Jews? Religious meetings are commanded us by the word of God, and experience itself sufficiently proves their necessity. But unless these meetings are regular and have fixed days assigned to them, how can they be held? We must, as the apostle puts it, do all things decently and in order (1 Corinthians 14:40). Yet so impossible would it be to preserve decency and order without this orderly arrangement that its dissolution would immediately lead to the disturbance and ruin of the Church. But if the reason for which the Lord appointed a sabbath for the Jews applies equally to us, no one can claim that it is a matter with which we have nothing to do. Our most provident and indulgent Parent has been pleased to provide for our needs no less than for the needs of the Jews. Why, it may be asked, do we not hold daily meetings, and so do away with the distinction of days? Would that we had that privilege! Spiritual wisdom certainly deserves to have some portion of every day devoted to it. But if, because of the weakness of many, daily meetings cannot be held, and love will not allow us to demand more of them, why should we not adopt the rule which the will of God has plainly laid upon us?

33¶

I am obliged to dwell a little longer on this because some restless spirits are now raising an outcry about the observance of the Lord's day. They complain that Christian people are being trained in Judaism because some observance of days is retained. My reply is that we observe those days without Judaism, because in this matter we differ widely from the Jews. We do not celebrate the day with the most minute formality, as a ceremony by which we imagine a spiritual mystery is typified, but we adopt it as a necessary remedy for preserving order in the Church. Paul tells us that Christians are not to be judged in respect of its observance, because it is a shadow of something to come (Colossians 2:16); and accordingly he expresses a fear that his labor among the Galatians may prove to be in vain, because they still observed days (Galatians 4:10, 11). And he tells the Romans that it is superstitious to make one day differ from another (Romans 14:5). But who, except those restless men, does not see what observance the Apostle is referring to? Those persons had no regard for that civil and ecclesiastical arrangement, "Finem istum politicum et ecclesiasticum ordinem." French, "la police et ordre en l'Eglise"; policy and order in the Church.

34¶

It was not, however, without reason that the early Christians substituted what we call the Lord's day for the Sabbath. Since the resurrection of our Lord is the end and fulfillment of that true rest which the ancient sabbath typified, this day, by which the types were abolished, serves to warn Christians against clinging to a shadowy ceremony. I do not hold so tightly to the number seven as to bring the Church into bondage to it, nor do I condemn churches for holding their meetings on other solemn days, provided they guard against superstition. This they will do if they use those days merely for the keeping of discipline and good order. The whole may be summed up as follows. As the truth was delivered to the Jews in types, so it is given to us without figure: first, that throughout our whole lives we may aim at a constant rest from our own works, so that the Lord may work in us by his Spirit; second, that every individual, as he has opportunity, may diligently exercise himself in private in pious meditation on the works of God, and at the same time that all may observe the lawful order appointed by the Church for hearing the word, administering the sacraments, and public prayer; and third, that we may avoid oppressing those who are under us. HONOUR THY FATHER AND THY MOTHER: THAT THY DAYS MAY BE LONG UPON THE LAND WHICH THE LORD THY GOD GIVETH THEE.

35¶

The end of this commandment is that, since the Lord takes pleasure in the preservation of his own ordinance, the degrees of dignity he has appointed must be held inviolable. The sum of the commandment, therefore, will be that we are to look up to those whom the Lord has set over us, rendering them honor, gratitude, and obedience. From this it follows that everything in the way of contempt, ingratitude, or disobedience is forbidden. For the term honor has this breadth of meaning in Scripture. Thus when the Apostle says, "Let the elders that rule well be counted worthy of double honour" (1 Timothy 5:17), he refers not only to the reverence due to them, but to the reward to which their services are entitled. But since this command to submit is very repugnant to the perversity of the human mind (which, puffed up with ambitious longings, will scarcely allow itself to be subject), that form of superiority which is most attractive and least resented is set forth as an example suited to soften and bend our minds to habits of submission. By means of the subjection most easily endured, the Lord gradually accustoms us to every kind of lawful subjection, since the same principle governs all. For to those whom he raises to high positions he imparts his authority, so far as is necessary to maintain their station. The titles Father, God, and Lord all meet in him alone, and so whenever any one of them is mentioned, our mind should be impressed with the same feeling of reverence. Those, therefore, on whom he bestows such titles he marks out with some small spark of his splendor, so as to entitle them to honor, each in his own place. In this way we must consider that our earthly father has something divine in him, since there is some reason for his bearing a divine title, and that he who is our prince and ruler is admitted to some share of honor with God.

36¶

We ought therefore to have no doubt that the Lord here lays down this universal rule, namely, that knowing that every individual is set over us by his appointment, we should render him reverence, gratitude, obedience, and every duty in our power. And it makes no difference whether those on whom the honor is conferred deserve it or not. Whatever they may be, the Almighty, by giving them their station, shows that he wants them honored. The commandment specifies the reverence due to those to whom we owe our being. Nature herself should in some measure teach us this. For they are monsters, not men, who insolently and contemptuously violate a father's authority. Hence the Lord commands that all who rebel against their parents be put to death, since they are, as it were, unworthy of the light, paying no respect to those to whom they owe the sight of it. And it is evident from the various additions to the Law that we were right in stating that the honor referred to here consists of three parts: reverence, obedience, and gratitude. The first the Lord enforces when he commands that whoever curses his father or his mother shall be put to death. In this way he avenges insult and contempt. The second he enforces when he pronounces the punishment of death on disobedient and rebellious children. To the third belongs our Savior's declaration that God requires us to do good to our parents (Matthew 15). And whenever Paul mentions this commandment, he interprets it as requiring obedience. Exodus 21:17; Leviticus 20:9; Proverbs 20:20; Deuteronomy 21:18; Matthew 25:4; Ephesians 6:1; Colossians 3:20.

37¶

A promise is added by way of commendation, the better to remind us how pleasing to God is the submission required here. Paul applies that spur to rouse us from our sluggishness when he calls this the first commandment with promise, since the promise contained in the First Table is not attached specially to any one commandment but extends to the whole law. Moreover, the sense in which the promise is to be taken is as follows. The Lord spoke to the Israelites specifically of the land which he had promised them as an inheritance. If, then, possession of the land was a pledge of the divine favor, we cannot wonder that the Lord was pleased to bear witness to his favor by granting long life, since in this way they were able to enjoy his kindness for a long time. The meaning therefore is: Honor your father and your mother, so that you may be able, through the course of a long life, to enjoy possession of the land which is to be given you as a token of my favor. But since the whole earth is blessed to believers, we rightly count the present life among the divine blessings. So this promise likewise has reference to us also, since the length of the present life is a proof of God's goodwill toward us. It is not promised to us, nor was it promised to the Jews, as though in itself it made up happiness, but because it is an ordinary sign of the divine favor to the godly. Therefore, if anyone who is obedient to his parents happens to be cut off before reaching maturity (which happens not infrequently), the Lord nevertheless keeps his promise as faithfully as when he gives a hundred acres of land where he had promised only one. The whole matter lies in this: we must consider that long life is promised only in so far as it is a blessing from God, and that it is a blessing only in so far as it is a display of divine favor. This favor, however, he testifies and truly displays to his servants more richly and substantially by death.

38¶

Moreover, while the Lord promises the blessing of the present life to children who show proper respect to their parents, he at the same time indicates that an inescapable curse hangs over the rebellious and disobedient; and so that it may not fail to be carried out, he pronounces sentence of death on them in his Law and orders it to be inflicted. If they escape the judgment, he will take vengeance in one way or another. For we see how great a number of people of this kind fall either in battle or in brawls; others are overtaken by unusual disasters, and almost all of them are proof that the threatening is not made in vain. But if any do escape until extreme old age, yet, because they are deprived of the blessing of God in this life, they only waste away in wickedness and are reserved for severer punishment in the world to come; so they are far from sharing in the blessing promised to obedient children. It should be observed in passing that we are commanded to obey parents only in the Lord. This is clear from the principle already laid down: for the place they occupy is one to which the Lord has raised them by imparting to them a portion of his own honor. Therefore the submission rendered to them should be a step in our ascent to the Supreme Parent; and so, if they urge us to break the law, they deserve to be regarded not as parents but as strangers trying to lure us away from obedience to our true Father. The same holds in the case of rulers, masters, and superiors of every kind. For it would be unfitting and absurd for the honor of God to be impaired by their exaltation, an exaltation which, being derived from him, ought to lead us up to him. The French adds, "et la doit plustost augmenter, qu'amoindrir confirmer que viloer"; and ought to increase rather than diminish it, to confirm rather than violate it.

THOU SHALT NOT KILL.

39¶

The point of this commandment is that, since the Lord has bound the whole human race together by a kind of unity, the safety of all ought to be regarded as entrusted to each. In general, therefore, all violence and injustice, and every kind of harm by which our neighbor's body suffers, is forbidden. Accordingly, we are required to do faithfully whatever lies in our power to defend our neighbor's life; to promote whatever contributes to his peace, to be watchful in warding off harm, and, when danger comes, to help in removing it. Remembering that it is the Divine Lawgiver who speaks in this way, consider further that he requires you to apply the same rule in governing your mind. It would be ridiculous for him who sees the thoughts of the heart and has special regard to them to train only the body in uprightness. This commandment, therefore, forbids the murder of the heart and requires a sincere desire to preserve our brother's life. The hand, indeed, commits the murder, but the mind, under the influence of wrath and hatred, conceives it. How can you be angry with your brother without passionately longing to do him harm? If you must not be angry with him, neither must you hate him, since hatred is nothing but settled anger. However you may disguise the fact or try to escape it by vain pretexts, where there is either wrath or hatred, there is an inclination to do harm. If you still persist in evasion, the mouth of the Spirit has declared that "whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer" (1 John 3:15); and the mouth of our Savior has declared that "whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment: and whosoever shall say to his brother, Raca, shall be in danger of the council: but whosoever shall say, Thou fool, shall be in danger of hell fire" (Matthew 5:22).

40¶

Scripture notes a twofold principle of equity on which this commandment is founded. Man is both the image of God and our own flesh. Therefore, if we would not violate the image of God, we must hold the person of man sacred; if we would not strip ourselves of humanity, we must cherish our own flesh. The practical inference to be drawn from the redemption and gift of Christ will be considered elsewhere. Book 3, chapter 7, sections 4–7; chapter 20, sections 38, 45; Book 4, chapter 1, sections 13–19; chapter 17, sections 38, 40.

THOU SHALT NOT COMMIT ADULTERY.

41¶

The point of this commandment is that, since God loves chastity and purity, we ought to guard against all uncleanness. The substance of the commandment, therefore, is that we must not defile ourselves with any impurity or lustful excess. To this corresponds the positive command that we must order every part of our conduct chastely and with self-control. The thing expressly forbidden is adultery, to which lust naturally tends, so that its filthiness (being of a grosser and more obvious kind, since it leaves a stain even on the body) may lead us to abhor every form of lust. Since the law under which man was created was not that he should live a solitary life but that he should enjoy a helper suited to him, and since ever since he fell under the curse the need for this way of life has increased, the Lord made the necessary provision for us in this respect by the institution of marriage, which, entered into under his authority, he has also sanctified with his blessing. Hence it is evident that any form of cohabitation other than marriage is cursed in his sight, and that the married relationship was ordained as a necessary means of keeping us from giving way to unbridled lust. Let us beware, therefore, of yielding to indulgence, since we are assured that the curse of God rests on every man and woman who live together without marriage.

42¶

Now, since natural feeling and the passions inflamed by the fall make the marriage bond doubly necessary, except in the case of those whom God has exempted by special grace, let every individual consider how the matter stands with himself. Virginity, I admit, is a virtue not to be despised; but since it is denied to some, and granted to others only for a time, those who are assailed by incontinence and are unable to fight against it successfully should turn to the remedy of marriage, and so practice chastity in the way of their calling. Those who are incapable of self-restraint, if they do not use the remedy allowed and provided for lack of self-control, are at war with God and resist his ordinance. And let no one tell me (as many do in the present day) that he can do all things with God's help! The help of God is present only with those who walk in his ways (Psalm 91:14), that is, in his callings, from which all withdraw themselves who, neglecting the remedies provided by God, vainly and presumptuously strive to struggle against and overcome their natural feelings. That continence is a special gift from God, and one of those which are not given indiscriminately to the whole body of the Church but only to a few of its members, our Lord affirms (Matthew 19:12). He first describes a certain class of people who have made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven's sake, that is, so that they may devote themselves with more freedom and less hindrance to the things of heaven. But so that no one would suppose such a sacrifice was in every man's power, he had shown a little before that not all are capable of it, but only those to whom it is specially given from above. Hence he concludes, "He that is able to receive it, let him receive it." Paul asserts the same thing still more plainly when he says, "Every man has his proper gift of God, one after this manner, and another after that" (1 Corinthians 7:7).

43¶

Since we are reminded by an express declaration that it is not in every man's power to live chastely in celibacy, even though it may be his most earnest study and aim to do so, and that this is a special grace which the Lord grants only to certain individuals so that they may be less encumbered in his service, do we not oppose God, and nature as he has constituted it, if we do not suit our way of life to the measure of our ability? The Lord forbids fornication; therefore he requires purity and chastity. The only way each person has of preserving it is to measure himself by his capacity. Let no one rashly despise marriage as something useless or superfluous for him; let no one long for celibacy unless he is able to do without the married state. And even here let him not consult the ease or convenience of the flesh, but only this: that, freed from this bond, he may be readier and better prepared for all the duties of piety. And since there are many on whom this blessing is conferred only for a time, let everyone who abstains from marriage do so only as long as he is fit to endure celibacy. If he does not have the power to subdue his passion, let him understand that the Lord has made it his duty to marry. The Apostle shows this when he commands, "Nevertheless, to avoid fornication, let every man have his own wife and let every woman have her own husband." "If they cannot contain, let them marry." He first indicates that most men are liable to incontinence, and then, of those so liable, he commands all without exception to turn to the only remedy by which unchastity may be prevented. The incontinent, therefore, who neglect to cure their weakness by this means, sin by the very fact of disobeying the Apostle's command. And let no man flatter himself that, because he abstains from the outward act, he cannot be accused of unchastity. His mind may meanwhile be inwardly inflamed with lust. For Paul's definition of chastity is purity of mind combined with purity of body: "The unmarried woman careth for the things of the Lord, that she may be holy both in body and spirit" (1 Corinthians 7:34). Therefore, when he gives a reason for the earlier precept, he not only says that it is better to marry than to live in fornication, but that it is better to marry than to burn.

44¶

Moreover, when spouses are made aware that their union is blessed by the Lord, they are reminded by this that they must not give way to intemperate and unrestrained indulgence. For though honorable marriage covers the shame of incontinence, it does not follow that it ought immediately to become a spur to it. Therefore let spouses consider that not all things are lawful for them. Let there be sobriety in the conduct of the husband toward the wife, and of the wife in turn toward the husband, each acting so as to do nothing unworthy of the dignity and temperance of married life. Marriage contracted in the Lord ought to show measure and modesty, and not run to the extreme of wantonness. Ambrose censured this excess severely, but not undeservedly, when he described the man who shows no modesty or decency in married intercourse as committing adultery with his wife. See Ambrose, De Philosophia, quoted by Augustine in his book Contra Julianum, book 2.

THOU SHALT NOT STEAL.

45¶

The point is that, since injustice is an abomination to God, we must render to every man what is his due. In substance, then, the commandment forbids us to covet other men's goods, and accordingly requires every man to exert himself honestly in preserving his own. For we must consider that what each individual possesses has not come to him by chance, but by the distribution of the sovereign Lord of all, so that no one can turn his means to bad purposes without committing a fraud against a divine dispensation. There are very many kinds of theft. One consists in violence, as when a man's goods are plundered and carried off by force; another in malicious deceit, as when they are fraudulently intercepted; a third in the more hidden craftiness which takes possession of them under a show of justice; and a fourth in flattery, which coaxes them away under the pretense of a gift. But not to dwell too long on listing the different kinds, we know that all the arts by which we gain possession of our neighbors' goods and money, when instead of sincere affection we put an eagerness to deceive or harm them in any way, are to be regarded as thefts. Though such goods may be obtained by a lawsuit, God gives a different verdict. He sees the long train of deception by which the crafty man begins to lay nets for his simpler neighbor until he entangles him in their meshes; he sees the harsh and cruel laws by which the more powerful oppresses and crushes the weak; he sees the enticements by which the more cunning baits the hook for the less wary, though all these escape the judgment of man and no notice is taken of them. Nor is the violation of this commandment confined to money, or merchandise, or lands, but it extends to every kind of right; for we defraud our neighbors to their harm if we refuse any of the duties we are bound to perform toward them. If an agent or an idle steward wastes his employer's substance or does not pay proper attention to the management of his property; if he unjustly squanders or extravagantly wastes the means entrusted to him; if a servant mocks his master, divulges his secrets, or in any way is treacherous toward his life or his goods; if, on the other hand, a master cruelly torments his household, he is guilty of theft before God; since everyone who does not perform what he owes to others in the exercise of his calling keeps back or makes away with what does not belong to him.

46¶

We shall therefore properly obey this commandment if, content with our own lot, we seek to acquire nothing but honest and lawful gain; if we do not long to grow rich by injustice, nor to plunder our neighbor of his goods so that our own may be increased by it; if we do not hurry to heap up wealth cruelly wrung from the blood of others; if we do not, with excessive eagerness and by means lawful and unlawful, scrape together whatever may glut our greed or feed our extravagance. On the other hand, let it be our constant aim faithfully to lend our counsel and help to all, so as to assist them in keeping their property; or if we have to deal with the treacherous or crafty, let us rather be ready to give up something of our right than to contend with them. And not only so, but let us contribute to the relief of those whom we see under the pressure of hardship, helping their need out of our abundance. Finally, let each of us consider how far he is bound in duty to others, and in good faith pay what we owe. In the same way, let the people render all due honor to their rulers, submit patiently to their authority, obey their laws and orders, and refuse nothing which they can bear without forfeiting the favor of God. Let rulers, again, take proper care of their people, preserve the public peace, protect the good, restrain the bad, and conduct themselves throughout as men who must give an account of their office to God, the Judge of all. Let the ministers of churches faithfully attend to the ministry of the word, and not corrupt the doctrine of salvation, but deliver it purely and sincerely to the people of God. Let them teach not only by doctrine but by example; in short, let them play the part of good shepherds toward their flocks. Let the people in turn receive them as the messengers and apostles of God, render them the honor which their Supreme Master has bestowed on them, and supply them with what is necessary for their livelihood. Let parents be careful to bring up, guide, and teach their children as a trust committed to them by God. Let them not provoke or alienate them by cruelty, but cherish and embrace them with the gentleness and indulgence that befits their role. The regard children owe their parents has already been mentioned. Let the young respect those advanced in years, as the Lord has been pleased to make that age honorable. Let the aged also, by their prudence and experience (in which they are far superior), guide the weakness of youth, not attacking them with harsh and noisy rebukes, but tempering strictness with gentleness and kindness. Let servants show themselves diligent and respectful in obeying their masters, and this not with eye-service, but from the heart, as servants of God. Let masters also not be stern and unkind to their servants, nor harass them with excessive harshness, nor treat them with insult, but rather let them acknowledge them as brothers and fellow servants of our heavenly Master, whom they are therefore bound to treat with mutual love and kindness. Let everyone, I say, consider in this way what he owes to his neighbors in his own place and rank, and pay what he owes. Moreover, we must always keep the Lawgiver in view, and so remember that the law requiring us to promote and defend the interests and welfare of our fellow men applies equally to our minds and our hands.

THOU SHALT NOT BEAR FALSE WITNESS AGAINST THY NEIGHBOUR.

47¶

The point of the commandment is that, since God, who is truth, abhors falsehood, we must practice unfeigned truth toward one another. The sum, therefore, will be that we must not harm our neighbor's name by slanders and false accusations, or damage his fortunes by falsehood; in short, that we must not harm anyone out of insolence or a love of evil-speaking. To this prohibition corresponds the command that we must faithfully help everyone, as far as lies in us, in asserting the truth, for the maintenance of his good name and his estate. The Lord seems to have intended to explain the commandment in these words: "Thou shalt not raise a false report: put not thine hand with the wicked to be an unrighteous witness." "Keep thee far from a false matter" (Exodus 23:1, 7). In another passage he not only forbids that kind of falsehood which consists in playing the part of talebearers among the people, but says, "Neither shalt thou stand against the blood of thy neighbour" (Leviticus 19:16). Both transgressions are distinctly forbidden. Indeed, there can be no doubt that, as in the previous commandments he forbade cruelty, unchastity, and greed, so here he forbids falsehood, which consists of the two parts we have mentioned. By malicious or wicked detraction we sin against our neighbor's good name; by lying, and sometimes even by casting a slur on him, we harm him in his estate. It makes no difference whether you suppose that formal, judicial testimony is meant here, or the ordinary testimony given in private conversation. For we must always come back to the consideration that for each kind of transgression one instance is set forth by way of example, so that the others may be referred to it, and that the instance chiefly chosen is the one in which the shamefulness of the transgression is most apparent. It seems proper, however, to extend it more generally to the slander and spiteful insinuations by which our neighbors are unjustly wronged. For falsehood in a court of justice is always accompanied by perjury. But against perjury, in so far as it profanes and violates the name of God, there is sufficient provision in the third commandment. Hence the lawful keeping of this precept consists in using the tongue to uphold the truth, so as to promote both the good name and the prosperity of our neighbor. The equity of this is perfectly clear. For if a good name is more precious than riches, a man who is robbed of his good name is no less injured than if he were robbed of his goods; while in the latter case false testimony is sometimes no less harmful than robbery committed by the hand.

48¶

And yet it is strange with what careless complacency men everywhere sin in this respect. Indeed, very few are found who are not notoriously afflicted with this disease, such is the poisonous delight we take both in prying into and in exposing our neighbor's faults. Let us not imagine that it is a sufficient excuse to say that on many occasions our statements are not false. He who forbids us to defame our neighbor's reputation by falsehood wants us to keep it unstained as far as truth will allow. Though the commandment is directed only against falsehood, it indicates that the preservation of our neighbor's good name is commended to us. It ought to be inducement enough for us to guard our neighbor's good name that God takes an interest in it. Evil-speaking in general, therefore, is undoubtedly condemned. Moreover, by evil-speaking we do not mean rebuke given with a view to correction; nor accusation or judicial sentence, by which a remedy for evil is sought; nor public censure, which tends to strike terror into other offenders; nor disclosure to those whose safety depends on being forewarned, so that they may not unknowingly be brought into danger; but the hateful accusation which springs from a malicious and insolent love of slander. Indeed, the commandment extends so far as to include that coarse, affected wit, full of invective, by which the failings of others are bitterly attacked under a show of playfulness, as some are accustomed to do who court a reputation for cleverness, even if it should bring on a blush or inflict a bitter pang. By insolence of this kind our brothers are sometimes grievously wounded. THOU SHALT NOT COVET THY NEIGHBOUR'S HOUSE, THOU SHALT NOT COVET THY NEIGHBOUR'S WIFE NOR HIS MAN-SERVANT, NOR HIS MAID-SERVANT, NOR HIS OX NOR HIS ASS, NOR ANYTHING THAT IS THY NEIGHBOUR'S.

49¶

The point is this: since the Lord wants the whole soul to be filled with love, every feeling of a contrary nature must be banished from our minds. The sum, therefore, will be that no thought may be allowed to steal into our minds and fill them with a harmful desire tending to our neighbor's loss. To this corresponds the opposite precept, that everything we conceive, deliberate, will, or plan be joined with the good and advantage of our neighbor. But here, it seems, we meet a great and perplexing difficulty. For if it was rightly said above that under the words adultery and theft, lust and an intention to injure and deceive are forbidden, it may seem superfluous afterwards to use a separate commandment to forbid a covetous desire for our neighbor's goods. The difficulty will easily be removed by distinguishing between design and covetousness. See above, chapter 2, end of section 24; and Book 3, chapter 3, sections 11, 12, 13; and Book 4, chapter 15, sections 11, 12.

50¶

Nor is such uprightness demanded without reason. For who can deny that it is right for all the powers of the mind to be occupied with love? If the mind ceases to have love as its aim, who can doubt that it is diseased? How does it happen that so many desires harmful to your brother enter your mind, if not because you disregard him and think only of yourself? If your mind were wholly filled with love, no part of it would be left open for such thoughts to enter. In so far, therefore, as the mind is empty of love, it must be under the influence of concupiscence. Someone will object that those fancies which arise in the mind by chance and immediately vanish cannot properly be condemned as concupiscence, which has its seat in the heart. I answer that the question here concerns fancies of a kind which, while they present themselves to our thoughts, at the same time impress and stir the mind with desire, since the mind never thinks of choosing something without the heart being excited and inclining toward it. God therefore commands a strong and ardent affection, an affection not to be hindered by any portion of concupiscence, however small. He requires a mind so admirably ordered as not to be moved in the slightest degree against the law of love. Lest you should imagine that this view has no weighty authority behind it, I may mention that it was first suggested to me by Augustine. See Augustine, Epistle 200, to Asellicus, and Quæstiones, book 88, near the end, Question 66; but especially Concio 8 on Psalm 118. The subject is also touched on in Psalm 142, and De Tempore, Sermon 45, and Retractations, book 1, chapter 5, and De Continentia, chapter 8.

Such, then, is the Second Table of the Law, in which we are sufficiently instructed in the duties we owe to man for the sake of God, on a consideration of whose nature the whole system of love is founded. It would be vain, therefore, to impress on people the various duties taught in this table without resting your instructions on the fear and reverence of God as their proper foundation. I need not tell the thoughtful reader that those who make two precepts out of the prohibition of covetousness perversely split one thing into two. There is nothing in the repetition of the words "Thou shalt not covet." The "house" is put down first, and its different parts are then listed, beginning with the "wife"; and so it is clear that the whole ought to be read continuously, as the Jews rightly do. The sum of the whole commandment, therefore, is that whatever each individual possesses should remain whole and secure, not only from injury or the wish to injure, but also from the slightest feeling of covetousness which can spring up in the mind.

51¶

It will not now be difficult to discover the general end in view in the whole Law, namely, the fulfillment of righteousness, so that man may shape his life on the model of the divine purity. For in it God has so portrayed his own character that anyone who carried out in action what is commanded would in some measure display a living image of God. Therefore Moses, when he wanted to fix a summary of the whole in the memory of the Israelites, addressed them thus: "And now, Israel, what does the Lord thy God require of thee, but to fear the Lord thy God, to walk in all his ways, and to love him, and to serve the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, to keep the commandments of the Lord and his statutes which I command thee this day for thy good?" (Deuteronomy 10:12, 13). And he never stopped repeating the same thing whenever he had occasion to mention the end of the Law. The teaching of the Law has this so much in view that it joins man to his God by holiness of life and, as Moses expresses it elsewhere (Deuteronomy 6:5; 11:13), makes him cleave to him. Moreover, this holiness of life is contained under the two heads mentioned above: "Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind, and with all thy strength, and thy neighbour as thyself." First, our mind must be completely filled with love to God, and then this love must immediately flow out toward our neighbor. The Apostle shows this when he says, "The end of the commandment is charity out of a pure heart, and a good conscience, and of faith unfeigned" (1 Timothy 1:5). You see that conscience and unfeigned faith are placed at the head, in other words, true piety; and that love is derived from this. It is a mistake, then, to suppose that only the rudiments and first principles of righteousness are delivered in the Law, to form a kind of introduction to good works, as it were, and not to guide us to their perfect performance. For complete perfection nothing more can be required than what is expressed in these passages of Moses and Paul. How far, I ask, would a man want to go who is not satisfied with the instruction that directs man to the fear of God, to spiritual worship, to practical obedience, and finally to purity of conscience, unfeigned faith, and love? This confirms that interpretation of the Law which searches out and finds in its precepts all the duties of piety and love. Those who look only for dry and meager elements, as though it taught the will of God only by halves, by no means understand its end, as the Apostle bears witness.

52¶

Since Christ and the Apostles, in giving a summary of the Law, sometimes leave out the First Table, very many fall into the mistake of supposing that their words apply to both tables. In Matthew, Christ calls "judgment, mercy, and faith" the "weightier matters of the Law." I think it clear that by faith here is meant truthfulness toward men. But in order to extend the words to the whole Law, some take it to mean piety toward God. This is surely to no purpose. For Christ is speaking of those works by which a man ought to prove himself just. If we attend to this, we will cease to wonder why elsewhere, when the young man asks him, "What good thing shall I do, that I may have eternal life?" he simply answers that he must keep the commandments: "Thou shalt do no murder, Thou shalt not commit adultery, Thou shalt not steal, Thou shalt not bear false witness, Honour thy father and thy mother: and, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself" (Matthew 19:16, 18). For obedience to the First Table consisted almost entirely either in the inward affection of the heart or in ceremonies. The affection of the heart was not visible, and hypocrites were diligent in observing ceremonies; but the works of love were of such a nature as to be a solid proof of righteousness. The same thing occurs so often in the Prophets that it must be familiar to everyone who has any reasonable acquaintance with them. Isaiah 1:17; 57:6; Jeremiah 7:5, 6; Ezekiel 18:7, 8; Hosea 6:6; Zechariah 7:9, 10.

53¶

Is it true, then, you will ask, that to live innocently with men is a more complete summary of righteousness than to live piously toward God? By no means. But because no one consistently practices love in every respect unless he seriously fears God, such practice is a proof of piety as well. To this we may add that the Lord, knowing well that none of our good deeds can reach him (as the Psalmist declares, Psalm 16:2), does not demand from us duties toward himself, but exercises us in good works toward our neighbor. Hence the Apostle, not without cause, makes the whole perfection of the saints consist in love (Ephesians 3:19; Colossians 3:14). And in another passage he quite properly calls it the "fulfilling of the law," adding that "he that loveth another has fulfilled the law" (Romans 13:8). And again, "All the law is fulfilled in this: Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself" (Galatians 5:14). For this is the very thing Christ himself teaches when he says, "All things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets" (Matthew 7:12). It is certain that in the law and the prophets faith, and whatever belongs to the proper worship of God, holds the first place, and that love is made subordinate to this; but our Lord means that in the Law the practice of justice and equity toward men is prescribed as the means we are to use in bearing witness to a pious fear of God, if we truly have it.

54¶

Let us hold, therefore, that our life will be shaped in closest agreement with the will of God and the requirements of his Law when it is in every respect most beneficial to our brothers. But in the whole Law there is not one syllable that lays down a rule about what a man is to do or avoid for the benefit of his own carnal nature. And indeed, since men are by nature prone to excessive self-love, which they always retain however far they may stray from the truth, there was no need of a law to inflame a love that already exists in excess. Hence it is perfectly plain, See Book 3, chapter 7, section 4. Also Augustine, De Doctrina Christiana, book 1, chapter 23 and following.

55¶

Since our Savior has shown in the parable of the Samaritan (Luke 10:36) that the term neighbor includes the most distant stranger, there is no reason to limit the precept of love to our own connections. I do not deny that the closer the relationship, the more frequent our acts of kindness should be. For the condition of humanity requires that there be more duties in common between those who are more closely joined by the ties of kinship, or friendship, or neighborhood. And this is done without any offense to God, by whose providence we are in a manner driven to do it. But I say that the whole human race, without exception, is to be embraced with one feeling of love: that here there is no distinction of Greek or Barbarian, worthy or unworthy, friend or enemy, since all are to be viewed not in themselves but in God. If we turn aside from this view, it is no wonder that we entangle ourselves in error. Therefore, if we would hold the true course in love, our first step must be to turn our eyes not to man, the sight of whom might more often produce hatred than love, but to God, who requires that the love we bear to him be spread among all mankind; so that our fundamental principle must always be: whatever a man may be, he is still to be loved, because God is loved.

56¶

Therefore nothing could be more pernicious than the ignorance or wickedness of the Schoolmen in turning the precepts about revenge and the love of enemies (precepts which had formerly been delivered to all the Jews, and were then delivered universally to all Christians) into counsels which one was free to obey or disobey, confining the necessary observance of them to the monks, who were made more righteous than ordinary Christians by the simple fact of voluntarily binding themselves to obey counsels. The reason they give for not receiving them as laws is that they seem too heavy and burdensome, especially for Christians, who are under the law of grace. Do they really have the audacity to remodel the eternal law of God concerning the love of our neighbor? Is there a page of the Law on which any such distinction exists? Or rather, do we not meet on every page with commands which, in the strictest terms, require us to love our enemies? What is meant by commanding us to feed our enemy if he is hungry, to bring back his ox or his ass if we meet it going astray, or to help it up if we see it lying under its burden (Proverbs 25:21; Exodus 23:4)? Shall we show kindness to cattle for man's sake and have no goodwill toward the man himself? What? Is not the word of the Lord eternally true: "Vengeance is mine, I will repay" (Deuteronomy 32:35)? This is stated more explicitly elsewhere: "Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people" (Leviticus 19:18). Let them either erase these passages from the Law, or acknowledge the Lord as a Lawgiver, and not falsely pretend that he is merely a counselor.

57¶

And what, I ask, is meant by the following passage, which they have dared to insult with this absurd gloss? "Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you; that ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven" (Matthew 5:44, 45). Who does not agree here with the reasoning of Chrysostom (De Compunctione Cordis, and on Romans 7), that the nature of the motive makes it plain that these are not exhortations but precepts? For what is left to us if we are shut out from the number of the children of God? According to the Schoolmen, monks alone will be the children of our Father in heaven; monks alone will dare to call on God as their Father. And meanwhile, what will become of the Church? By the same rule, she will be confined to heathens and tax collectors. For our Savior says, "If ye love them which love you, what reward have ye? do not even the publicans the same?" It will truly be well with us if we are left only the name of Christians while we are deprived of the inheritance of the kingdom of heaven! Nor is the argument of Augustine less forceful: "When the Lord forbids adultery, he forbids it with regard to the wife of an enemy no less than the wife of a friend; when he forbids theft, he does not allow stealing of any kind, whether from a friend or an enemy" (Augustine, De Doctrina Christiana). Now these two commandments, "Thou shalt not steal, Thou shalt not commit adultery," Paul brings under the rule of love; indeed, he says that they are briefly summed up in this saying, "Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself" (Romans 13:9). Therefore either Paul must be a false interpreter of the Law, or we must necessarily conclude that under this precept we are bound to love our enemies just as our friends. Those, then, show themselves to be in truth the children of Satan who so wantonly throw off a yoke common to the children of God. It may be doubted whether, in promoting this dogma, they have shown greater stupidity or shamelessness. There is no ancient writer who does not hold it as certain that these are pure precepts. It was not even doubted in the age of Gregory, as is plain from his firm assertion, for he holds it to be beyond dispute that they are precepts. And how stupidly they argue! The burden, they say, would be too difficult for Christians to bear! As if anything could be imagined more difficult than to love the Lord with all the heart, and soul, and strength. Compared with this Law, there is none that may not seem easy, whether it be to love our enemy or to banish every feeling of revenge from our minds. To our weakness, indeed, everything, down to the smallest tittle of the Law, is hard and difficult. In the Lord we have strength. It is his to give what he commands, and to command what he wills. That Christians are under the law of grace does not mean that they are to wander unrestrained without law, but that they are grafted into Christ, by whose grace they are freed from the curse of the Law, and by whose Spirit they have the Law written on their hearts. Paul has called this grace a law, though not in the proper sense of the term, alluding to the Law of God, with which he was contrasting it. The Schoolmen, seizing on the term Law, make it the foundation of their vain speculations. The French is "Ces folastres sans propos prennent un grand mystére en ce mot de Loy"; these foolish fellows absurdly find a great mystery in this term Law.

58¶

The same must be said of their application of the term venial sin both to the hidden impiety which violates the First Table and to the direct transgression of the last commandment of the Second Table. See Book 3, chapter 4, section 28, where it is also shown that this is not the dogma of the Stoics, that all sins are equal.

59¶

I wish they would consider what our Savior meant when he said, "Whosoever shall break one of these least commandments, and shall teach men so, he shall be called the least in the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 5:19). Are they not of this number when they presume to make light of the transgression of the Law, as though it did not deserve death? The proper course would have been to consider not simply what is commanded, but who it is that commands, because every least transgression of his Law detracts from his authority. Do they count it a small matter to insult the majesty of God in any one respect? Again, since God has made his will known in the Law, everything contrary to the Law displeases him. Will they pretend that the wrath of God is so disarmed that the punishment of death will not immediately follow? He has declared plainly (if they could be persuaded to listen to his voice instead of darkening his clear truth with their tasteless subtleties), "The soul that sinneth it shall die" (Ezekiel 18:20). Again, in the passage quoted a moment ago, "The wages of sin is death." What these men acknowledge to be sin, because they cannot deny it, they contend is not mortal. Having indulged this madness too long already, let them learn to repent; or, if they persist in their folly, let the children of God take no further notice of them and remember that all sin is mortal, because it is rebellion against the will of God and necessarily provokes his anger, and because it is a violation of the Law, against every violation of which, without exception, the judgment of God has been pronounced. The faults of the saints are indeed venial, not, however, by their own nature, but because, through the mercy of God, they obtain pardon.

Here Calvin expounds the moral law and lays down the abiding rule for reading it — that each commandment reaches far beyond the bare letter, that where a sin is forbidden the opposite duty is commanded, and that God weighs the heart and not merely the hand. This unsettles the comfortable modern conscience that congratulates itself for crimes uncommitted. In 2026, a man may never strike his brother and yet harbor the contempt our Lord called murder; a household may keep an outward Sabbath of leisure while the heart wanders far from worship. Calvin's exposition refuses us that shelter — the law is spiritual, and it searches us. For the family, this means catechizing not only against the obvious transgressions but toward the affections God requires: honor, contentment, fidelity, mercy. Teach your children that the commandments are not a fence to skirt but a portrait of the love we owe to God and neighbor — and that seeing how far we fall short is itself the law's mercy, sending us again to the obedience of Christ counted as ours.
