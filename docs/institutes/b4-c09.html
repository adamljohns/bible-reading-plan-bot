Of Councils and Their Authority

Since Papists regard their Councils as expressing the sentiment and consent of the Church, particularly as regards the authority of declaring dogmas and the exposition of them, it was necessary to treat of Councils before proceeding to consider that part of ecclesiastical power which relates to doctrine. I. First, the authority of Councils in delivering dogmas is discussed, and it is shown that the Spirit of God is not so bound to the Pastors of the Church as opponents suppose. Their objections refuted, sec. 1-7. II. The errors, contradictions, and weaknesses, of certain Councils exposed. A refutation of the subterfuge, that those set over us are to be obeyed without distinction, sec. 8-12. III. Of the authority of Councils as regards the interpretation of Scripture, sec. 13, 14. 1. The true nature of Councils. 2. Whence the authority of Councils is derived. What meant by assembling in the name of Christ. 3. Objection, that no truth remains in the Church if it be not in Pastors and Councils. Answer, showing by passages from the Old Testament that Pastors were often devoid of the spirit of knowledge and truth. 4. Passages from the New Testament showing that our times were to be subject to the same evil. This confirmed by the example of almost all ages. 5. All not Pastors who pretend to be so. 6. Objection, that General Councils represent the Church. Answer, showing the absurdity of this objection from passages in the Old Testament. 7. Passages to the same effect from the New Testament. 8. Councils have authority only in so far as accordant with Scripture. Testimony of Augustine. Councils of Nice, Constantinople, and Ephesus, Subsequent Councils more impure, and to be received with limitation. 9. Contradictory decisions of Councils. Those agreeing with divine truth to be received. Those at variance with it to be rejected. This confirmed by the example of the Council of Constantinople and the Council of Nice; also of the Council of Chalcedon, and second Council of Ephesus. 10. Errors of purer Councils. Four causes of these errors. An example from the Council of Nice. 11. Another example from the Council of Chalcedon. The same errors in Provincial Councils. 12. Evasion of the Papists. Three answers. Conclusion of the discussion as to the power of the Church in relation to doctrine. 13. Last part of the chapter. Power of the Church in interpreting Scripture. From what source interpretation is to be derived. Means of preserving unity in the Church. 14. Impudent attempt of the Papists to establish their tyranny refuted. Things at variance with Scripture sanctioned by their Councils. Instance in the prohibition of marriage and communion in both kinds.

1¶

Even if I were now to concede everything they ask concerning the Church, it would not greatly help them toward their goal. For everything that is said of the Church they immediately transfer to councils, which in their opinion represent the Church. Indeed, when they contend so stubbornly for the power of the Church, their only aim is to hand over the whole of what they extort to the Roman Pontiff and his conclave. Before I begin to discuss this question, two points must be briefly stated in advance. First, though I intend to be stricter in discussing this subject, it is not because I value ancient councils less than I ought. I revere them from my heart, and would have all men hold them in due honor. French, "Si je tien ici la bride roide pour ne lascher rien facilement à nos adversaires, ce n'est pas a dire pourtant que je prise les conciles anciens moins que je ne doy. Car je les honore de bonne affection, et desire que chacun les estime, et les ait en reverence." — If I here keep the reins tight, and do not easily yield anything to our opponents, it is not because I prize ancient councils less than I ought. For I honor them sincerely, and desire that every man esteem them and hold them in reverence.

2¶

Let us now come to the subject itself. If we consult Scripture on the authority of councils, there is no promise more remarkable than the one contained in these words of our Savior: "Where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them." But this applies just as much to any particular meeting as to a universal council. And yet the main point of the question does not lie here, but in the condition that is added — namely, that Christ will be in the midst of a council provided it is assembled in his name. Therefore, though our opponents should name councils of thousands of bishops, it will do them little good; nor will they persuade us to believe that these are, as they maintain, guided by the Holy Spirit, until they make it credible that they assemble in the name of Christ. For it is as possible for wicked and dishonest bishops to conspire against Christ as for good and honest bishops to meet together in his name. Of this we have a clear proof in a great many of the decrees that have come out of councils. But this will be seen later. For now I reply in a single word: our Savior's promise is made only to those who assemble in his name. How, then, is such an assembly to be defined? I deny that those assemble in the name of Christ who, disregarding his command by which he forbids anything to be added to the word of God or taken from it, decide everything at their own pleasure; who, not content with the oracles of Scripture, that is, with the only rule of perfect wisdom, devise some novelty out of their own heads (Deuteronomy 4:2; Revelation 22:18). Certainly, since our Savior has not promised to be present at all councils of every description, but has given a distinctive mark for distinguishing true and lawful councils from others, we must by no means lose sight of the distinction. The covenant that God made long ago with the Levitical priests was that they should teach from his mouth (Malachi 2:7). This he always required of the prophets, and we see also that it was the law given to the apostles. On those who violate this covenant God bestows neither the honor of the priesthood nor any authority. Let my opponents resolve this difficulty if they wish to subject my faith to the decrees of men without authority from the word of God.

3¶

Their notion that the truth cannot remain in the Church unless it exists among the pastors, and that the Church herself cannot exist unless she is displayed in general councils, is very far from true if the prophets have left us a correct description of their own times. In the time of Isaiah there was a Church at Jerusalem which the Lord had not yet abandoned. Yet of the pastors he speaks this way: "His watchmen are blind; they are all ignorant, they are all dumb dogs, they cannot bark; sleeping, lying down, loving to slumber. Yea, they are greedy dogs which never have enough, and they are shepherds that cannot understand: they all look to their own way" (Isaiah 56:10, 11). In the same way Hosea says, "The watchman of Ephraim was with my God: but the prophet is a snare of a fowler in all his ways, and hatred in the house of his God" (Hosea 9:8). Here, by ironically linking them with God, he shows that their claim to the priesthood was empty. There was also a Church in the time of Jeremiah. Let us hear what he says of the pastors: "From the prophet even unto the priest, every one dealeth falsely." Again, "The prophets prophesy lies in my name: I sent them not, neither have I commanded them, neither spake unto them" (Jeremiah 6:13; 14:14). And not to drag on with quotations, read the whole of his thirty-third and fortieth chapters. Then, on the other side, Ezekiel denounces them in terms no milder: "There is a conspiracy of her prophets in the midst thereof, like a roaring lion ravening the prey; they have devoured souls." "Her priests have violated my law, and profaned mine holy things" (Ezekiel 22:25, 26). There is more to the same effect. Similar complaints abound throughout the prophets; nothing recurs more often.

4¶

But perhaps, though this great evil prevailed among the Jews, our own age is free from it. Would that it were; but the Holy Spirit declared that it would be otherwise. For Peter's words are clear: "But there were false prophets among the people, even as there shall be false teachers among you, who privily will bring in damnable heresies" (2 Peter 2:1). See how he predicts a coming danger, not from ordinary believers, but from those who would pride themselves on the name of pastors and teachers. Besides, how often did Christ and his apostles foretell that the greatest dangers threatening the Church would come from pastors (Matthew 24:11, 24)? Indeed, Paul openly declares that Antichrist would have his seat in the temple of God (2 Thessalonians 2:4), thereby indicating that the dreadful calamity of which he was speaking would come only from those who would have their seat in the Church as pastors. And in another passage he shows that the arrival of this great evil was almost at hand. For in addressing the elders of Ephesus he says, "I know this, that after my departing shall grievous wolves enter in among you, not sparing the flock. Also of your own selves shall men arise, speaking perverse things, to draw away disciples after them" (Acts 20:29, 30). How great a corruption might a long series of years bring in among pastors, when they could degenerate so far within so short a time? And not to fill my pages with details, the examples of almost every age remind us that the truth is not always cherished in the hearts of pastors, and that the safety of the Church does not depend on their condition. It was fitting that those appointed to preserve the peace and safety of the Church should be its overseers and guardians; but it is one thing to perform what you owe, and another to owe what you do not perform.

5¶

Let no one, however, take me to mean that I wish rashly and without reserve to overthrow the authority of pastors in everything. French, "Toutesfois je ne veux point que ces propos soyent entendus comme si je vouloye amoindrir l'authorité des pasteurs, et induire le peuple à la mepriser legerement." — However, I would not have these statements understood as if I wished to lessen the authority of pastors, and to lead the people to despise it lightly.

6¶

From this it is easy to answer their claim concerning general councils. It cannot be denied that the Jews had a true Church under the prophets. But had a general council then been made up of the priests, what kind of appearance would the Church have presented? We hear the Lord pronouncing judgment not against one or two of them, but against the whole order: "The priests shall be astonished, and the prophets shall wonder" (Jeremiah 4:9). Again, "The law shall perish from the priest, and counsel from the ancients" (Ezekiel 7:26). Again, "Therefore night shall be unto you, that ye shall not have a vision; and it shall be dark unto you, that ye shall not divine; and the sun shall go down over the prophets, and the day shall be dark over them," and so on (Micah 3:6). Now, had all men of this kind been gathered together, what spirit would have presided over their meeting? Of this we have a notable instance in the council that Ahab convened (1 Kings 22:6, 22). Four hundred prophets were present. But because they had met with no other purpose than to flatter the ungodly king, Satan is sent by the Lord to be a lying spirit in all their mouths. The truth is there condemned with one voice. Micaiah is judged a heretic, is struck, and is thrown into prison. So it was done to Jeremiah, and so to the other prophets.

7¶

But there is one memorable example that may serve for all. In the council which the priests and Pharisees assembled at Jerusalem against Christ (John 11:47), what is lacking, so far as outward appearance is concerned? Had there been no Church at Jerusalem then, Christ would never have taken part in the sacrifices and other ceremonies. A solemn meeting is held; the high priest presides; the whole priestly order takes its seats; and yet Christ is condemned, and his doctrine is driven out. This atrocity proves that the Church was not at all contained in that council. But, they say, there is no danger that anything of the kind will happen among us. Who has told us so? Too much confidence in a matter of such great importance is open to the charge of sloth. Indeed, when the Spirit, by the mouth of Paul, foretells in plain terms that a falling away will take place, a falling away that cannot come until pastors first forsake God (2 Thessalonians 2:3), why do we willingly walk blindfolded to our own destruction? Therefore we cannot on any account admit that the Church consists in a meeting of pastors, of whom the Lord has nowhere promised that they would always be good, but has sometimes foretold that they would be wicked. When he warns us of danger, it is to make us more cautious.

8¶

What, then, you will say, is there no authority in the definitions of councils? Yes, indeed there is; for I do not contend that all councils are to be condemned and all their acts annulled, or, as the saying goes, wiped out with one stroke. But, it will be said, you are bringing them all into subjection, and so leaving everyone free to receive or reject the decrees of councils as he pleases. By no means. But whenever the decree of a council is brought forward, the first thing I would wish done is to examine at what time it was held, on what occasion, with what intention, and who were present at it; next I would bring the subject under discussion to the standard of Scripture. And I would do this in such a way that the decision of the council should have its weight and be regarded as a prior judgment, yet not so as to prevent the application of the test I have mentioned. I wish all had kept the method that Augustine prescribes in his Third Book against Maximinus, when he wished to silence this heretic's cavils against the decrees of councils: "I ought not to set the Council of Nice against you, nor ought you to set that of Ariminum against me, as prejudging the question. I am not bound by the authority of the latter, nor you by that of the former. Let thing contend with thing, cause with cause, reason with reason, on the authority of Scripture, an authority not peculiar to either, but common to all." In this way councils would be given due respect, and yet the highest place would be given to Scripture, with everything brought to it as the test. Thus those ancient Councils of Nice, Constantinople, the first of Ephesus, Chalcedon, and the like, which were held to refute errors, we gladly embrace and revere as sacred, so far as doctrines of faith are concerned; for they contain nothing but the pure and genuine interpretation of Scripture, which the holy Fathers with spiritual prudence employed to crush the enemies of religion who had then arisen. In some later councils also we see a true zeal for religion displayed, and moreover unmistakable marks of ability, learning, and prudence. But as things usually go from bad to worse, it is easy to see in more recent councils how far the Church gradually degenerated from the purity of that golden age. I do not doubt, however, that even in those more corrupt ages councils had their bishops of better character. But it happened with them as the Roman senators of old complained concerning their own decrees: the votes being counted, not weighed, the better were forced to give way to the greater number. They certainly put forth many ungodly opinions. There is no need to collect instances here, both because it would be tedious, and because others have done it so carefully as to leave little to be added.

9¶

Moreover, why should I review the conflicts of council with council? Nor is there any ground for whispering to me that when councils disagree, one or the other of them is not a lawful council. For how shall we determine this? Simply, if I am not mistaken, by judging from Scripture that the decrees are not orthodox. For this alone is the sure rule of discernment. It is now about nine hundred years since the Council of Constantinople, convened under the Emperor Leo, decided that the images set up in churches were to be thrown down and broken to pieces. Shortly afterward the Council of Nice, assembled by Irene out of hatred for the former, decreed that the images were to be restored. Which of the two councils shall we acknowledge as lawful? The latter has generally prevailed and secured a place for images in churches. But Augustine maintains that this could not be done without the greatest danger of idolatry. Epiphanius, at a later time, speaks much more harshly (Epist. ad Joann. Hierosolym. et Lib. 3 contra Hæres.). For he says it is an unspeakable abomination to see images in a Christian church. Could men who speak this way approve of that council if they were alive today? But if the historians speak the truth, and we believe its acts, not only the images themselves but the worship of them was sanctioned there. Now it is plain that this decree came from Satan. Do they not show, by corrupting and twisting Scripture, that they held it in contempt? This I have made sufficiently clear in an earlier part of the work (see Book 1, chapter 11, section 14). Be that as it may, we shall never be able to distinguish between contradictory and dissenting councils, which have been many, unless we weigh them all in that balance for men and angels, I mean, the word of God. Thus we embrace the Council of Chalcedon and reject the second of Ephesus, because the latter sanctioned the ungodliness of Eutyches and the former condemned it. The judgment of these holy men was founded on the Scriptures; and while we follow it, we desire that the word of God, which enlightened them, may now enlighten us also. Let the Romanists now go and boast in their usual way that the Holy Spirit is fixed and tied to their councils.

10¶

Even in their ancient and purer councils there is something lacking: either because the men who attended, learned and prudent in other respects, were absorbed in the business at hand and did not attend to many other things; or because, occupied with weightier and more serious measures, they overlooked some of lesser importance; or simply because, being men, they were deceived through ignorance, or were sometimes carried headlong by some excess of feeling. Of this last case (which seems the hardest of all to avoid) we have a striking example in the Council of Nice, which has been received by all, as it deserves, with the utmost reverence. For when the chief article of our faith was there in danger, and Arius, its enemy, was present, ready to engage anyone in combat, and it was of the utmost importance that those who had come to attack Arius should be agreed, they nevertheless felt secure amid all these dangers. Indeed, as if forgetting their gravity, modesty, and courtesy, and laying aside the discussion before them (as though they had met for the express purpose of pleasing Arius), they began to give way to internal quarrels, and to turn against one another the pen that should have been used against Arius. Foul accusations were heard, libels flew back and forth, and they would never have ceased from their strife until they had stabbed one another with mutual wounds, had not the Emperor Constantine intervened. Declaring that the examination of their lives was a matter beyond his jurisdiction, he restrained their intemperance by flattery rather than rebuke. In how many respects is it likely that the councils held after this one have erred? Nor does the fact need a long demonstration; anyone who reads their acts will notice many weaknesses, not to use a stronger term.

11¶

Even Leo, the Roman Pontiff, does not hesitate to charge the Council of Chalcedon, which he admits to be orthodox in its doctrines, with ambition and thoughtless rashness. He does not deny that it was lawful, but openly maintains that it could have erred. Some may think me foolish for laboring to point out errors of this kind, since my opponents admit that councils may err in things not necessary to salvation. My labor, however, is not superfluous. For although under compulsion they admit this in word, yet by forcing on us the determinations of all councils, in all matters without distinction, as the oracles of the Holy Spirit, they demand more than they had granted at the outset. By acting this way, what do they maintain but simply that councils cannot err, or that if they err, it is unlawful for us to see the truth or to refuse assent to their errors? At the same time, all I mean to infer from what I have said is this: though councils that were otherwise godly and holy were governed by the Holy Spirit, he nevertheless allowed them to share the lot of humanity, so that we should not put too much confidence in men. This is a much better view than that of Gregory Nanzianzen, who says (Ep. 55) that he never saw any council end well. In asserting that all without exception ended badly, he leaves them little authority. There is no need to make separate mention of provincial councils, since it is easy to judge from the case of general councils how much authority they ought to have in framing articles of faith and deciding what kind of doctrine is to be received.

12¶

But our Romanists, when in defending their cause they see every rational ground slip out from under them, take refuge in a last miserable subterfuge. Although the pastors should be dull in understanding and counsel, and utterly depraved in heart and will, still, they say, the word of the Lord remains, which commands us to obey those who have the rule over us (Hebrews 13:17). Is that really so? What if I should deny that those who act this way have the rule over us? They ought not to claim for themselves more than Joshua had, who was both a prophet of the Lord and an excellent pastor. Let us hear, then, in what terms the Lord installed him in his office: "This book of the law shall not depart out of your mouth; but you shall meditate therein day and night, that you may observe to do according to all that is written therein: for then shall you make your way prosperous, and you shall have good success" (Joshua 1:7, 8). Our spiritual rulers, therefore, will be those who do not turn aside from the law of the Lord to the right hand or to the left. But if the doctrine of all pastors is to be received without hesitation, why are we so often and so earnestly warned by the Lord not to listen to false prophets? "Thus saith the Lord of Hosts, Hearken not unto the words of the prophets that prophesy unto you; they make you vain: they speak a vision of their own heart, and not out of the mouth of the Lord" (Jeremiah 23:16). Again, "Beware of false prophets, which come to you in sheep's clothing but inwardly they are ravening wolves" (Matthew 7:15). In vain also would John urge us to try the spirits whether they are of God (1 John 4:1). From this judgment not even angels are exempt (Galatians 1:8), far less Satan with his lies. And what is meant by the saying, "If the blind lead the blind, both shall fall into the ditch" (Matthew 15:14)? Does it not declare plainly enough that there is a great difference among the pastors who are to be heard, and that not all are to be heard without distinction? Therefore they have no ground for frightening us with their name in order to draw us into a share of their blindness, since we see, on the contrary, that the Lord has taken special care to guard us from allowing ourselves to be led away by the errors of others, whatever the mask under which they may hide. For if our Savior's answer is true, blind guides, whether high priests, prelates, or pontiffs, can do nothing more than hurry us over the same cliff with themselves. Therefore let no names of councils, pastors, and bishops (which may be used under false pretenses as well as truly) keep us from heeding the evidence both of words and of facts, and from bringing all spirits to the test of the divine word, so that we may prove whether they are of God.

13¶

Having proved that no power was given to the Church to set up any new doctrine, let us now treat of the power attributed to councils in the interpretation of Scripture. We readily admit that when any doctrine is brought into dispute, there is no better or surer remedy than for a council of true bishops to meet and examine the point in controversy. A decision of this kind, which the pastors of the churches have agreed on in common after calling on the Spirit of Christ, will carry much more weight than if each one, adopting it for himself, were to deliver it to his people, or a few individuals were to meet in private and decide. Secondly, when bishops have assembled in one place, they deliberate more conveniently in common, settling both the doctrine and the form of teaching it, so that diversity may not give offense. Thirdly, Paul prescribes this method of determining doctrine. For when he gives the power of deciding to a single church, he shows what the course of procedure should be in more important cases — namely, that the churches together are to take joint cognizance. And the very instinct of godliness tells us that if anyone troubles the Church with some novelty in doctrine, and the matter is carried so far that there is danger of greater dissension, the churches should first meet, examine the question, and at length, after due discussion, decide according to Scripture. Such a decision may both put an end to doubt among the people and stop the mouths of wicked and restless men, so as to keep the matter from going further. Thus when Arius arose, the Council of Nice was convened, and by its authority it both crushed the wicked attempts of this ungodly man, restored peace to the churches he had troubled, and asserted the eternal divinity of Christ against his sacrilegious dogma. Afterward, when Eunomius and Macedonius stirred up new disturbances, their madness was met with a similar remedy by the Council of Constantinople; and the ungodliness of Nestorius was defeated by the Council of Ephesus. In short, this was from the beginning the usual method of preserving unity in the Church whenever Satan began his schemes. But let us remember that not all ages and places are blessed with an Athanasius, a Basil, a Cyril, and similar defenders of sound doctrine, whom the Lord raised up at that time. Indeed, let us consider what happened in the second Council of Ephesus, when the Eutychian heresy prevailed. Flavianus, of holy memory, was driven into exile together with some godly men, and many similar crimes were committed, because, instead of the Spirit of the Lord, Dioscorus, a factious man of very bad disposition, presided. But, it is said, the Church was not there. I admit it; for I always hold that the truth does not perish in the Church even though it is oppressed by one council, but is wonderfully preserved by the Lord to rise again and prove victorious in his own time. I deny, however, that every interpretation of Scripture that has received the votes of a council is true and certain.

14¶

But the Romanists have another end in view when they say that the power of interpreting Scripture belongs to councils, and that without appeal. For they use it as a pretext for giving the name of an interpretation of Scripture to everything that is decided in councils. Of purgatory, the intercession of saints, auricular confession, and the like, not one syllable can be found in Scripture. But since all these have been sanctioned by the authority of the Church, or, to speak more correctly, have been received through opinion and practice, every one of them is to be held as an interpretation of Scripture. And not only so, but whatever a council has decided against Scripture is to bear the name of an interpretation. Christ bids all drink of the cup that he holds out in the Supper. The Council of Constance forbade giving it to the people and decreed that the priest alone should drink. Though this is diametrically opposed to the institution of Christ (Matthew 26:26), they insist that it be regarded as his interpretation. Paul calls the prohibition of marriage a doctrine of devils (1 Timothy 4:1, 3), and the Spirit elsewhere declares that "marriage is honorable in all" (Hebrews 13:4). Having afterward forbidden their priests to marry, they insist on this as a true and genuine interpretation of Scripture, though nothing more foreign to it can be imagined. Should anyone dare to open his lips against it, he will be judged a heretic, since the determination of the Church is without appeal, and it is unlawful to have any doubt about the accuracy of her interpretation. Why should I attack such effrontery? To point it out is to condemn it. Their dogma concerning the power of approving Scripture I deliberately pass over. For to subject the oracles of God in this way to the judgment of men, and to hold that they are valid because they please men, is a blasphemy that does not deserve to be mentioned. Besides, I have already touched on it (Book 1, chapter 7; chapter 8, section 9). I will ask them one question, however. If the authority of Scripture is founded on the approval of the Church, will they cite the decree of a council to that effect? I believe they cannot. Why, then, did Arius allow himself to be defeated at the Council of Nice by passages brought forward from the Gospel of John? By their reasoning he was free to reject them, since they had not previously been approved by any general council. They appeal to an old catalogue, which they call the Canon, and say that it originated in a decision of the Church. But I ask again, in what council was that Canon published? Here they must fall silent. Besides, I should like to know what they believe that Canon to be. For I see that the ancients are little agreed about it. If effect is to be given to what Jerome says (Præf. in Lib. Solom.), the Maccabees, Tobit, Ecclesiasticus, and the like must take their place among the Apocrypha; but this they will not tolerate on any account.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Councils have genuine weight, Calvin grants, and he reverences the ancient ones from his heart — yet their authority is never absolute, for the Spirit is not chained to assemblies of men. The test of any council is whether its decrees accord with Scripture; even gatherings of thousands of bishops have erred, contradicted one another, and condemned the truth, as the council that sentenced our Lord makes terribly clear. For the believer in 2026, this is a charge to discernment without cynicism. We honor the creeds and the faithful labors of the saints who went before, receiving them gratefully — and we still bring every claim to the balance of God's Word, refusing to let any majority, ancient or modern, stand in the place of Scripture. Truth has often survived in a faithful remnant while the powerful condemned it; the Lord preserves it to rise again in his own time. Hold fast to what has been tested and proven, and let the Word, which illumined the Fathers, illumine you.
