How Christ Performed the Office of Redeemer in Procuring Our Salvation — The Death, Resurrection, and Ascension of Christ

This chapter contains four leading heads—I. A general consideration of the whole subject, including a discussion of a necessary question concerning the justice of God and his mercy in Christ, sec. 1–4. II. How Christ fulfilled the office of Redeemer in each of its parts, sec. 5–17. His death, burial, descent to hell, resurrection, ascension to heaven, seat at the right hand of the Father, and return to judgment. III. A great part of the Creed being here expounded, a statement is given of the view which ought to be taken of the Creed commonly ascribed to the Apostles, sec. 18. IV. Conclusion, setting forth the doctrine of Christ the Redeemer, and the use of the doctrine, sec. 19.

1¶

All that we have said of Christ up to this point leads to this one result: that, condemned, dead, and lost in ourselves, we must seek in him righteousness, deliverance, life, and salvation, as we are taught by the well-known words of Peter, "Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men whereby we must be saved" (Acts 4:12). The name of Jesus was not given him at random, or by chance, or by the will of man, but was brought from heaven by an angel, as the herald of the supreme decree; Latin, "Supremi decreti." French, "Decret eternel et inviolable" — Eternal and inviolable decree.

2¶

But before we go further, we must consider in passing how it can be said that God, who goes before us with his mercy, was our enemy until he was reconciled to us by Christ. For how could he have given us in his only-begotten Son a singular pledge of his love, if he had not already embraced us with free favor? Since some appearance of contradiction arises here, I will resolve the difficulty. The way the Spirit usually speaks in Scripture is that God was the enemy of men until they were restored to favor by the death of Christ (Romans 5:10); that they were cursed until their iniquity was expiated by the sacrifice of Christ (Galatians 3:10, 13); and that they were separated from God until, by means of Christ's body, they were received into union with him (Colossians 1:21, 22). Such expressions are accommodated to our capacity, so that we may better understand how miserable and calamitous our condition is without Christ. For if it were not said in plain terms that divine wrath and vengeance and eternal death lay upon us, we would be less aware of our wretchedness without the mercy of God, and less disposed to value the blessing of deliverance. For example, suppose a person is told: "If God, at the time you were a sinner, had hated you and cast you off as you deserved, horrible destruction would have been your doom; but of his own accord and free kindness he kept you in his favor, not allowing you to be estranged from him, and in this way rescued you from danger." That person will indeed be moved, and will feel in some degree how much he owes to the mercy of God. But suppose instead he is told, as Scripture teaches, that he was estranged from God by sin, an heir of wrath, exposed to the curse of eternal death, shut out from all hope of salvation, a complete stranger to the blessing of God, the slave of Satan, a captive under the yoke of sin; in short, doomed to horrible destruction and already caught up in it; that then Christ stepped in, took the punishment upon himself, and bore what by the just judgment of God was hanging over sinners; that with his own blood he expiated the sins which made them hateful to God, by this expiation satisfied and duly propitiated God the Father, by this intercession appeased his anger, on this foundation established peace between God and men, and by this bond secured the divine goodwill toward them. Will not these considerations move him all the more deeply, the more vividly they set before him the greatness of the calamity from which he was delivered? In short, since our mind cannot lay hold of life through the mercy of God with enough eagerness, or receive it with fitting gratitude, unless it is first struck with fear of the divine anger and dismayed at the thought of eternal death, we are so instructed by divine truth as to perceive that without Christ God is in a certain sense hostile to us, and has his arm raised for our destruction. Taught in this way, we look to Christ alone for divine favor and fatherly love.

3¶

Though this is said in accommodation to the weakness of our capacity, it is not said falsely. For God, who is perfect righteousness, cannot love the iniquity which he sees in all of us. All of us, therefore, have within us something that deserves the hatred of God. Hence, with respect first to our corrupt nature, and second to the depraved conduct that follows from it, we are all offensive to God, guilty in his sight, and by nature children of hell. But since the Lord does not will to destroy in us what is his own, he still finds something in us which in his kindness he can love. For though it is by our own fault that we are sinners, we are still his creatures; though we have brought death upon ourselves, he created us for life. So it is pure, unmerited love that moves him to receive us into favor. But if there is a perpetual and irreconcilable opposition between righteousness and iniquity, then as long as we remain sinners we cannot be fully received. Therefore, so that every ground of offense may be removed and he may fully reconcile us to himself, he abolishes all the evil that is in us by means of the expiation set forth in the death of Christ, so that we, who were formerly impure and unclean, now appear righteous and holy in his sight. Accordingly, God the Father by his love goes before and anticipates our reconciliation in Christ. Indeed, it is because he first loves us that he afterward reconciles us to himself. But because the iniquity which deserves God's indignation remains in us until the death of Christ comes to our aid, and that iniquity is accursed and condemned in his sight, we are not admitted to full and sure communion with God except in so far as Christ unites us to him. And therefore, if we would cherish the hope of having God placable and propitious toward us, we must fix our eyes and minds on Christ alone, since it is to him alone that we owe it that our sins, which necessarily provoked the wrath of God, are not imputed to us.

4¶

For this reason Paul says that God "has blessed us with all spiritual blessings in heavenly places in Christ: according as he has chosen us in him before the foundation of the world" (Ephesians 1:3, 4). These things are clear and agree with Scripture, and they admirably reconcile the passages in which it is said that "God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son" (John 3:16), and yet that it was "when we were enemies" that "we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son" (Romans 5:10). But to give further assurance to those who require the authority of the ancient Church, I will quote a passage of Augustine to the same effect: "The love of God is incomprehensible and unchangeable. For it was not after we were reconciled to him by the blood of his Son that he began to love us, but he loved us before the foundation of the world, so that together with his only begotten Son we too might be sons of God before we were anything at all. Our being reconciled by the death of Christ must not be understood as if the Son reconciled us so that the Father, who then hated us, might begin to love us, but rather that we were reconciled to him who already loved us, though he was at enmity with us because of sin. To the truth of both statements the Apostle bears witness: 'God commendeth his love toward us, in that while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us' (Romans 5:8). Therefore he had this love toward us even when we were at enmity with him and were workers of iniquity. Thus, in a wondrous and divine way, he loved us even when he hated us. For he hated us as we were, such as he had not made us; and yet, because our iniquity had not destroyed his work in every respect, he knew how, in each one of us, both to hate what we had made and to love what he had made." Such are the words of Augustine (Tractates on John, 110).

5¶

When it is asked, then, how Christ, by abolishing sin, removed the enmity between God and us, and purchased a righteousness which made God favorable and kind to us, the general answer is that he accomplished this by the whole course of his obedience. This is proved by the testimony of Paul: "As by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous" (Romans 5:19). And indeed elsewhere he extends the ground of the pardon which frees us from the curse of the law to the whole life of Christ: "When the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth his Son, made of a woman, made under the law, to redeem them that were under the law" (Galatians 4:4, 5). So even at his baptism he declared that a part of righteousness was fulfilled by his obeying the command of the Father. In short, from the moment he took on the form of a servant, he began to pay the price of deliverance in order to redeem us. Scripture, however, in order to define the way of salvation more precisely, ascribes it particularly and specially to the death of Christ. He himself declares that he gave his life a ransom for many (Matthew 20:28). Paul teaches that he died for our sins (Romans 4:25). John the Baptist exclaimed, "Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world" (John 1:29). Paul in another passage declares "that we are justified freely by his grace, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus: whom God has set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood" (Romans 3:25). Again, "being justified by his blood, we shall be saved from wrath through him" (Romans 5:9). Again, "He has made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him" (2 Corinthians 5:21). I will not search out all the passages, for the list would be endless, and many will be quoted later in their place. In the Confession of Faith called the Apostles' Creed, the transition is admirably made from the birth of Christ to his death and resurrection, in which the completion of a perfect salvation consists. Still, the other part of the obedience which he rendered in his life is not excluded. So Paul includes, from the beginning to the end, his having taken the form of a servant, humbled himself, and become obedient unto death, even the death of the cross (Philippians 2:7). And indeed, the first step in his obedience was his voluntary submission; for the sacrifice would have been of no avail for justification if it had not been offered freely. Hence our Lord, after testifying, "I lay down my life for the sheep," expressly adds, "No man taketh it from me" (John 10:15, 18). In the same sense Isaiah says, "Like a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he opened not his mouth" (Isaiah 53:7). The Gospel history relates that he went out to meet the soldiers, and that in the presence of Pilate, instead of defending himself, he stood to receive judgment. He did this, indeed, not without a struggle, for he had taken on our infirmities as well, and in this way it was fitting for him to prove that he was rendering obedience to his Father. It was no ordinary display of incomparable love toward us to wrestle with dreadful terrors, and amid fearful torments to cast away all care for himself so that he might provide for us. We must bear in mind that Christ could not duly propitiate God without renouncing his own feelings and subjecting himself entirely to his Father's will. To this effect the Apostle fittingly quotes a passage from the Psalms: "Lo, I come (in the volume of the book it is written of me) to do thy will, O God" (Hebrews 10:5; Psalm 40:7, 8). Thus, since troubled consciences find no rest without a sacrifice and cleansing by which sins are expiated, we are rightly directed there, for the source of our life is placed in the death of Christ. Moreover, since the curse that follows upon guilt awaited the final judgment of God, one principal point in the narrative is his condemnation before Pontius Pilate, the governor of Judea, to teach us that the punishment to which we were liable was inflicted on that Righteous One. We could not escape the fearful judgment of God; and Christ, in order to rescue us from it, submitted to be condemned by a mortal man, and indeed by a wicked and profane one. For the governor's name is mentioned not only to support the credibility of the narrative, but to remind us of what Isaiah says, that "the chastisement of our peace was upon him," and that "with his stripes we are healed" (Isaiah 53:5). For in order to remove our condemnation, it was not enough to endure any kind of death whatever. To satisfy for our ransom, it was necessary to choose a kind of death in which he might deliver us both by giving himself up to condemnation and by undertaking our expiation. Had he been cut down by assassins, or killed in a seditious riot, there could have been no kind of satisfaction in such a death. But when he is placed as a criminal at the bar, where witnesses are brought to testify against him, and the mouth of the judge condemns him to die, we see him bearing the character of an offender and evildoer. Here we must attend to two points, both of which had been foretold by the prophets, and which serve admirably to comfort and confirm our faith. When we read that Christ was led away from the judgment seat to execution, and was crucified between thieves, we have the fulfillment of the prophecy quoted by the Evangelist, "He was numbered with the transgressors" (Isaiah 53:12; Mark 15:28). Why was it so? That he might bear the character of a sinner, not of a righteous or innocent person, since he met death on account not of innocence but of sin. On the other hand, when we read that he was acquitted by the same lips that condemned him (for Pilate was forced again and again to bear public testimony to his innocence), let us call to mind what another prophet says, "I restored that which I took not away" (Psalm 69:4). Thus we see Christ bearing the character of a sinner and a criminal, while at the same time his innocence shines forth, and it becomes plain that he suffers for another's crime and not his own. He therefore suffered under Pontius Pilate, being in this way ranked among criminals by the formal sentence of the judge, and yet declared innocent by the same judge when he affirms that he finds no cause of death in him. Our acquittal consists in this: that the guilt which made us liable to punishment was transferred to the head of the Son of God (Isaiah 53:12). We must especially remember this substitution, so that we may not spend all our lives in fear and anxiety, as if the just vengeance which the Son of God transferred to himself were still hanging over us.

6¶

The very form of his death embodies a striking truth. The cross was accursed not only in the opinion of men but by the enactment of the divine Law. Hence Christ, while hanging upon it, subjects himself to the curse. And it had to be done in this way, so that the whole curse which, on account of our iniquities, awaited us, or rather already lay upon us, might be taken from us by being transferred to him. For the Son of God, though spotlessly pure, took upon himself the disgrace and shame of our iniquities, and in return clothed us with his purity. He seems to refer to the same thing when he says that he "condemned sin in the flesh" (Romans 8:3), the Father having destroyed the power of sin when it was transferred to the flesh of Christ. This term, therefore, indicates that Christ in his death was offered to the Father as a propitiatory victim, so that, expiation having been made by his sacrifice, we might cease to tremble at the divine wrath. It is now clear what the prophet means when he says that "the Lord has laid upon him the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:6); namely, that since he was to wash away the pollution of sins, they were transferred to him by imputation. The cross to which he was nailed was a symbol of this, as the Apostle declares: "Christ has redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree: that the blessing of Abraham might come on the Gentiles through Jesus Christ" (Galatians 3:13, 14). In the same way Peter says that he "bare our sins in his own body on the tree" (1 Peter 2:24), since from the very symbol of the curse we perceive more clearly that the burden under which we were crushed was laid upon him. Nor are we to understand that he himself was overwhelmed by the curse which he endured, but rather that by enduring it he checked, broke, and destroyed all its force. Accordingly, faith grasps acquittal in the condemnation of Christ, and blessing in his curse. Hence it is not without reason that Paul magnificently celebrates the triumph which Christ won upon the cross, as if the cross, the symbol of shame, had been turned into a triumphal chariot. For he says that he blotted out the handwriting of ordinances that was against us, which was contrary to us, and took it out of the way, nailing it to his cross; and that "having spoiled principalities and powers, he made a show of them openly, triumphing over them in it" (Colossians 2:14, 15). Nor is this to be wondered at; for, as another Apostle declares, Christ "through the eternal Spirit offered himself without spot to God" (Hebrews 9:14), and hence comes that transformation of the cross which would otherwise be contrary to its nature. But in order that these things may take deep root and have their seat in our inmost hearts, we must never lose sight of sacrifice and cleansing. 7¶

The Creed next states that he "was dead and buried." Here again we must consider how he put himself in our place in order to pay the price of our redemption. Death held us under its yoke, but he, in our place, delivered himself into its power so that he might set us free from it. This is what the Apostle means when he says "that he tasted death for every man" (Hebrews 2:9). By dying he kept us from dying; or (which is the same thing) by his death he purchased life for us (see Calvin, Psychopannychia). But in this he differed from us: in allowing himself to be overcome by death, he did not do so in order to be swallowed up in its abyss, but rather to destroy it, as it would otherwise have destroyed us. He did not allow himself to be so subdued by it as to be crushed by its power; rather, he laid it low when it was hanging over us and already exulting over us as conquered. In short, his purpose was "that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil, and deliver them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage" (Hebrews 2:14, 15). This is the first fruit which his death has produced for us. Another is that, by our fellowship with him, he puts to death our earthly members so that they may no longer exert themselves in action, and kills the old man so that he may no longer flourish and bear fruit. A further effect of his burial is that we, as his companions, are buried to sin. For when the Apostle says that we are grafted into the likeness of Christ's death, and that we are buried with him unto sin, that by his cross the world is crucified to us and we to the world, and that we are dead with him, he does not merely exhort us to display an imitation of his death, but declares that there is a power in it which ought to appear in all Christians, if they would not make his death fruitless and useless. Accordingly, in the death and burial of Christ a twofold blessing is set before us: namely, deliverance from death, to which we were enslaved, and the mortification of our flesh (Romans 6:5; Galatians 2:19, 6:14; Colossians 3:3).

8¶

Here we must not omit the descent into hell, which was of no small importance to the accomplishment of redemption. 10¶

But apart from the Creed, we must look for a surer explanation of Christ's descent into hell; and the word of God furnishes us with one that is not only pious and holy, but full of excellent consolation. Nothing would have been accomplished if Christ had endured only bodily death. In order to stand between us and God's anger, and to satisfy his righteous judgment, it was necessary that he should feel the weight of divine vengeance. Hence it was also necessary that he should grapple, as it were, at close quarters with the powers of hell and the horrors of eternal death. We quoted a little earlier from the Prophet that "the chastisement of our peace was laid upon him," that he "was bruised for our iniquities," and that he "bore our infirmities." These expressions indicate that, like a sponsor and surety for the guilty, and as if himself subjected to condemnation, he undertook and paid all the penalties which would have been exacted from them, with this one exception, that the pains of death could not hold him. Hence there is nothing strange in its being said that he descended into hell, seeing that he endured the death which is inflicted on the wicked by an angry God. It is frivolous and ridiculous to object that in this way the order is reversed, since it is absurd that an event which came before the burial should be placed after it. But after explaining what Christ endured in the sight of men, the Creed fittingly adds the invisible and incomprehensible judgment which he endured before God, to teach us that not only was the body of Christ given up as the price of redemption, but that there was a greater and more excellent price: that he bore in his soul the torments of a condemned and ruined man.

11¶

In this sense Peter says that God raised up Christ, "having loosed the pains of death: because it was not possible he should be holden of it" (Acts 2:24). He does not mention death simply, but says that the Son of God endured the pains produced by the curse and wrath of God, which are the source of death. How small a thing it would have been to come forth securely, and as if in play to undergo death! But here was a true proof of boundless mercy, that he did not shrink from the death he so greatly dreaded. And there can be no doubt that in the Epistle to the Hebrews the Apostle means to teach the same thing when he says that he "was heard in that he feared" (Hebrews 5:7). 12¶

Here certain miserable creatures, who, though unlearned, are driven more by malice than by ignorance, cry out that I am offering an atrocious insult to Christ, because it would be utterly unfitting to hold that he feared for the safety of his soul. And then, in harsher terms, they press the slanderous charge that I attribute despair to the Son of God, a feeling that is the very opposite of faith. First, they wickedly raise a controversy over the fear and dread which Christ felt, though these are openly affirmed by the Evangelists. For before the hour of his death arrived, he was troubled in spirit and stricken with grief; and at the very onset he began to be exceedingly amazed. To speak of these feelings as merely put on for show is a shameful evasion. It is our duty, therefore (as Ambrose rightly teaches), boldly to confess the agony of Christ, if we are not ashamed of the cross. And certainly, had his soul not shared in the punishment, he would have been a Redeemer of bodies only. The purpose of his struggle was to raise up those who were lying prostrate; and so far is this from detracting from his heavenly glory that his goodness, which can never be sufficiently praised, becomes all the more conspicuous in this, that he did not refuse to bear our infirmities. Hence also comes that comfort for our anxieties and griefs which the Apostle sets before us: "We have not an high priest who cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities; but was in all respects tempted like as we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15). These men pretend that something by its nature sinful is wrongly ascribed to Christ, as if they were wiser than the Spirit of God, who in the same passage reconciles the two things — namely, that he was tempted in all respects like as we are, and yet was without sin. There is no reason, therefore, to take alarm at infirmity in Christ, an infirmity to which he submitted not under the compulsion of force and necessity, but simply because he loved and pitied us. Whatever he suffered of his own accord detracts in no degree from his majesty. One thing that misleads these detractors is that they do not recognize in Christ an infirmity which was pure and free from every kind of taint, since it was kept within the bounds of obedience. Because no moderation can be seen in the depravity of our nature, in which all the affections rush with turbulent violence beyond their proper limits, they wrongly apply the same standard to the Son of God. But since he was upright, all his affections were under such restraint as to prevent anything like excess. Hence he could resemble us in grief, fear, and dread, but still with this mark of distinction. Refuted on this point, they fly off to another quibble: that although Christ feared death, he did not fear the curse and wrath of God, from which he knew he was safe. But let the godly reader consider how far it honors Christ to make him more effeminate and timid than the generality of men. Robbers and other criminals rush defiantly to death; many men nobly despise it; others meet it calmly. If the Son of God was amazed and terror-struck at the prospect of it, where was his firmness or greatness of soul? We are even told something that in an ordinary death would have been thought most extraordinary: that in the depth of his agony his sweat was like great drops of blood falling to the ground. Nor was this a spectacle put on for the eyes of others, since it was from a secluded spot that he poured out his groans to his Father. And so that no doubt may remain, it was necessary that angels should come down from heaven to strengthen him with miraculous consolation. How shamefully effeminate would it have been (as I have observed) to be so tormented by the fear of an ordinary death as to sweat drops of blood, and not even to be revived by the presence of angels! What? Does not that prayer, three times repeated, "Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me" (Matthew 26:39), a prayer dictated by incredible bitterness of soul, show that Christ had a fiercer and harder struggle than one with ordinary death?

Hence it is plain that these triflers with whom I am disputing presume to talk about what they do not know, never having seriously considered what is meant and involved in ransoming us from the justice of God. It is important to understand rightly how much our salvation cost the Son of God. If anyone now asks, "Did Christ descend into hell at the time when he prayed to be spared death?" I answer that this was the beginning, and that from it we may infer how dire and dreadful were the torments which he endured when he felt himself standing at the bar of God as a criminal in our place. And although the divine power of the Spirit veiled itself for a moment, so that it might give place to the infirmity of the flesh, we must understand that the trial arising from feelings of grief and fear was such as not to be at odds with faith. And in this was fulfilled what is said in Peter's sermon about his having been loosed from the pains of death, because "it was not possible he could be holden of it" (Acts 2:24). Though he felt, as it were, forsaken by God, he did not cease in the slightest degree to trust in his goodness. This appears from the well-known prayer in which, in the depth of his agony, he cried out, "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46). In all his agony he does not cease to call upon his God, even while crying out that he is forsaken by him. This refutes the Apollinarian heresy, as well as that of those who are called Monothelites. Apollinaris claimed that in Christ the eternal Spirit took the place of a soul, so that he was only half a man; as if he could have expiated our sins in any other way than by obeying the Father. But where does the feeling or desire of obedience reside, if not in the soul? And we know that his soul was troubled in order that ours, being freed from fear, might obtain peace and rest. Moreover, against the Monothelites, we see that in his human nature he felt a reluctance toward what he willed in his divine nature. I say nothing of his overcoming the fear of which we have spoken by a contrary affection. This appearance of reluctance is plain in the words, "Father, save me from this hour: but for this cause came I unto this hour. Father, glorify thy name" (John 12:27, 28). Still, in this perplexity there was no violent agitation, such as we display when we strive with all our might to master our own feelings.

13¶

Next follows the resurrection from the dead, without which all that has been said so far would be incomplete. For since nothing but weakness appears in the cross, death, and burial of Christ, faith must go beyond all these in order to be furnished with its full strength. Hence, although in his death we have the effectual completion of salvation, because by it we are reconciled to God, satisfaction is made to his justice, the curse is removed, and the penalty is paid, still it is not by his death but by his resurrection that we are said to be begotten again to a living hope (1 Peter 1:3); because, just as he became victorious over death by rising again, so the victory of our faith consists only in his resurrection. Its nature is better expressed in the words of Paul: "Who (Christ) was delivered for our offences, and was raised again for our justification" (Romans 4:25); as if he had said, "By his death sin was taken away; by his resurrection righteousness was renewed and restored." For how could he by dying have freed us from death, if he had yielded to its power? How could he have won the victory for us, if he had fallen in the contest?

Our salvation may thus be divided between the death and the resurrection of Christ: by the former sin was abolished and death destroyed; by the latter righteousness was restored and life revived, while the power and efficacy of the former are still conveyed to us by means of the latter. Accordingly Paul affirms that he was declared to be the Son of God by his resurrection (Romans 1:4), because he then fully displayed that heavenly power which is both a bright mirror of his divinity and a sure support of our faith; as he also teaches elsewhere, that "though he was crucified through weakness, yet he liveth by the power of God" (2 Corinthians 13:4). In the same sense, in another passage dealing with perfection, he says, "That I may know him and the power of his resurrection" (Philippians 3:10). Immediately afterward he adds, "being made conformable unto his death." In full agreement with this is the passage in Peter, that God "raised him up from the dead, and gave him glory, that your faith and hope might be in God" (1 Peter 1:21). Not that faith resting on his death alone is wavering, but that the divine power by which he upholds our faith is most conspicuous in his resurrection. Let us remember, therefore, that whenever his death alone is mentioned, everything belonging to the resurrection is included at the same time; and that there is a similar synecdoche in the term resurrection whenever it is used apart from death, everything belonging to death being included. But since by rising again he won the victory and became the resurrection and the life, Paul rightly argues, "If Christ be not raised, your faith is vain; ye are yet in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:17). Accordingly, in another passage, after glorying in the death of Christ against the terrors of condemnation, he enlarges on it in this way: "Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us" (Romans 8:34). Then, just as we have already explained that the mortification of our flesh depends on communion with the cross, so we must also understand that a corresponding benefit is derived from his resurrection. For as the Apostle says, "Like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:4). Accordingly, just as in another passage he urges, from our being dead with Christ, "Mortify therefore your members which are upon the earth" (Colossians 3:5), so from our being risen with Christ he infers, "seek those things which are above, where Christ sitteth at the right hand of God" (Colossians 3:1). In these words we are not only urged by the example of a risen Savior to pursue newness of life, but are taught that by his power we are renewed unto righteousness. A third benefit derived from it is that, like a pledge, it assures us of our own resurrection, of which his is certainly the surest representation. This subject is discussed at length in 1 Corinthians 15. But it should be noted in passing that when he is said to have "risen from the dead," these words express the reality of both his death and his resurrection, as if it had been said that he died the same death that other men naturally die, and received immortality in the same mortal flesh which he had assumed.

14¶

The resurrection is naturally followed by the ascension into heaven. For although Christ, by rising again, began fully to display his glory and power, having laid aside the lowly and humble condition of a mortal life and the shame of the cross, yet it was only by his ascension into heaven that his reign truly began. The Apostle shows this when he says that he ascended "that he might fill all things" (Ephesians 4:10), reminding us that beneath the appearance of contradiction there is a beautiful harmony, since, though he departed from us, it was so that his departure might be more useful to us than that presence which was confined in a lowly tabernacle of flesh during his stay on earth. Hence John, after repeating the well-known invitation, "If any man thirst, let him come unto me and drink," immediately adds, "the Holy Ghost was not yet given; because that Jesus was not yet glorified" (John 7:37, 39). Our Lord himself also declared this to his disciples: "It is expedient for you that I go away: for if I go not away the Comforter will not come unto you" (John 16:7). To console them for his bodily absence, he tells them that he will not leave them comfortless, but will come to them again in a way that is invisible indeed, but more to be desired, because they would then be taught by surer experience that the government he had obtained and the power he exercises would enable his faithful followers not only to live well, but also to die happily. And indeed we see how much more abundantly his Spirit was poured out, how much more gloriously his kingdom was advanced, and how much greater power was put forth in helping his followers and routing his enemies. Being taken up into heaven, he withdrew his bodily presence from our sight, not so that he might cease to be with his followers, who are still pilgrims on the earth, but so that he might rule both heaven and earth more directly by his power. Or rather, the promise which he made to be with us even to the end of the world he fulfilled by this ascension, by which, just as his body has been raised above all heavens, so his power and efficacy have been spread and diffused beyond all the bounds of heaven and earth. I prefer to explain this in the words of Augustine rather than my own: "Through death Christ was to go to the right hand of the Father, from where he is to come to judge the living and the dead, and that in bodily presence, according to sound doctrine and the rule of faith. For in spiritual presence he was to be with them after his ascension" (Augustine, Tractates on John, 109). In another passage he is fuller and more explicit: "With regard to his ineffable and invisible grace, what he said is fulfilled, Lo, I am with you alway, even to the end of the world (Matthew 28:20); but with regard to the flesh which the Word assumed, with regard to his being born of a virgin, his being seized by the Jews, nailed to the tree, taken down from the cross, wrapped in linen clothes, laid in the tomb, and manifested at his resurrection, it may be said, Me ye have not always with you. Why? Because in bodily presence he conversed with his disciples for forty days, and, leading them out where they saw him but did not follow, he ascended into heaven and is not here: for there he sits at the right hand of the Father. And yet he is here, for the presence of his Godhead was not withdrawn. Therefore, as regards his divine presence, we have Christ always; as regards his bodily presence, it was truly said to the disciples, Me ye have not always. For a few days the Church had him bodily present. Now she lays hold of him by faith, but does not see him with the eye" (Augustine, Tractates on John, 51).

15¶

Hence it is immediately added that he "sitteth at the right hand of God the Father," a comparison borrowed from princes, who have their assessors to whom they entrust the office of ruling and issuing commands. So Christ, in whom the Father is pleased to be exalted, and by whose hand he is pleased to reign, is said to have been received up and seated at his right hand (Mark 16:19); as if it had been said that he was installed in the government of heaven and earth, and formally put in possession of the administration committed to him, and not admitted only once, but to continue until he comes down to judgment. For this is how the Apostle interprets it when he says that the Father "set him at his own right hand in the heavenly places, far above all principality, and power, and might, and dominion, and every name that is named not only in this world, but also in that which is to come; and has put all things under his feet, and given him to be the head over all things to the Church." It is most comforting to think that judgment is entrusted to him who has already appointed us to share with him in the honor of judging (Matthew 19:28); so far is it from being true that he will ascend the judgment seat to condemn us. Here is the heart of it: condemned, dead, and lost in ourselves, we find in Christ — in his obedience, his cross, his descent into the very horror of God's wrath, his resurrection, ascension, and coming again — the whole of our salvation, nothing left over to seek elsewhere. He was numbered with transgressors yet declared innocent, that the just vengeance which loomed over us might fall on him alone. This is not a doctrine to admire from a distance but a ransom that reaches into your daily fear. When conscience accuses and the age offers a hundred cheaper comforts, fix your eyes on the One who tasted death so that you need not be engulfed by it, who rose so that your hope might be living and not vain. Raise your children to know that the same Christ who hung between thieves now sits at the Father's right hand and will return as Judge — and that this Judge is their Redeemer. Beware deriving even the smallest part of your salvation from any other quarter; the whole sum of it is in him, and to look beside him is to look away from life itself.
