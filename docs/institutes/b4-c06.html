Of the Primacy of the Romish See

The divisions of this chapter are,—I. Question stated, and an argument for the primacy of the Roman Pontiff drawn from the Old Testament refuted, sec. 1, 2. II. Reply to various arguments in support of the Papacy founded on the words, “Thou art Peter,” &c., sec. 3-17. 1. Brief recapitulation. Why the subject of primacy not yet mentioned. Represented by Papists as the bond of ecclesiastical unity. Setting out with this axiom, they begin to debate about their hierarchy. 2. Question stated. An attempted proof from the office of High Priest among the Jews. Two answers. 3. Arguments for primacy from the New Testament. Two answers. 4. Another answer. The keys given to the other apostles as well as to Peter. Other two arguments answered by passages of Cyprian and Augustine. 5. Another argument answered. 6. Answer to the argument that the Church is founded on Peter, from its being said, “Upon this rock I will build my Church.” 7. Answer confirmed by passages of Scripture. 8. Even allowing Peter’s superiority in some respect, this is no proof of the primacy of the Roman Pontiff. Other arguments answered. 9. Distinction between civil and ecclesiastical government. Christ alone the Head of the Church. Argument that there is still a ministerial head answered. 10. Paul, in giving a representation of the Church, makes no mention of this ministerial head. 11. Even though Peter were ministerial head, it does not follow that the Pope is so also. Argument founded on Paul’s having lived and died at Rome. 12. On the hypothesis of the Papists, the primacy belongs to the Church of Antioch. 13. Absurdity of the Popish hypothesis. 14. Peter was not the Bishop of Rome. 15. Same subject continued. 16. Argument that the unity of the Church cannot be maintained without a supreme head on earth. Answer, stating three reasons why great respect was paid in early times to the See of Rome. 17. Opinion of early times on the subject of the unity of the Church. No primacy attributed to the Church of Rome. Christ alone regarded as the Head of the Universal Church.

1¶

Up to this point we have reviewed those church orders which existed in the government of the early Church, but which, corrupted afterward by time and then more and more debased, now keep only the name in the Papal Church and are in fact nothing but empty masks, so that the comparison will enable the godly reader to judge what kind of church that is, for departing from which we are charged with schism. But on the head and crown of the whole matter, I mean the primacy of the Roman See, from which they undertake to prove that the Catholic Church is to be found only among them, see Calvin, Adversus Concilium Tridentinum. Also Adversus Theologos Parisienses.

2¶

The question, then, may be stated in this way: Is it necessary for the true order of the hierarchy (as they call it), or of church order, that one See should surpass the others in dignity and power, so as to be the head of the whole body? We subject the Church to unjust laws if we lay this necessity upon her without warrant from the word of God. Therefore, if our opponents would prove what they maintain, they must first of all show that this arrangement was instituted by Christ. For this purpose they point to the office of high priest under the law, and to the supreme jurisdiction which God appointed at Jerusalem. French, "Pour ce faire, ils alleguent la pretrise souveraine qui etoit en la loy, et la jurisdiction souveraine du grand sacrificateur, que Dieu avoit establie en Jerusalem." That is: For this purpose they allege the sovereign priesthood which was under the law, and the sovereign jurisdiction of the high priest which God had established at Jerusalem.

"Car c'est tout ainsi comme si quelcun debattoit que le monde doit etre gouverné par un baillie ou seneschal parce que chacune province a le sien." That is: For it is just as if someone were to argue that the whole world ought to be governed by one bailiff or seneschal, because each province has its own.

3¶

That example, therefore, which is plainly seen to have been temporary, they have no right to bind upon us as a perpetual law. In the New Testament there is nothing they can produce in support of their opinion except that it was said to one man, "You are Peter, and upon this rock I will build my Church" (Matthew 16:18). Again, "Simon, son of Jonas, do you love me?" "Feed my lambs" (John 21:15). But to give these proofs any force, they must first show that to him who is ordered to feed the flock of Christ, power is given over all churches, and that to bind and loose is nothing else than to preside over the whole world. But as Peter had received a command from the Lord, so he exhorts all other presbyters to feed the Church (1 Peter 5:2). From this we are entitled to infer that by that saying of Christ nothing more was given to Peter than to the others, or else that the right which Peter had received he shared equally with others. But not to argue to no purpose, we have elsewhere, from the lips of Christ himself, a clear explanation of what it is to bind and loose. It is simply to retain and remit sins (John 10:23). The manner of loosing and binding is explained throughout Scripture, but especially in that passage in which Paul declares that the ministers of the Gospel are commissioned to reconcile men to God, and at the same time to exercise discipline over those who reject the benefit (2 Corinthians 5:18; 10:16).

4¶

How improperly they twist the passages on binding and loosing I have touched on elsewhere, and will shortly explain more fully. For now it is worth our while merely to see what they can draw out of our Savior's celebrated answer to Peter. He promised him the keys of the kingdom of heaven, and said that whatever he bound on earth would be bound in heaven (Matthew 16:19). The moment we agree on the meaning of the keys and the manner of binding, all dispute will cease. For the Pope will gladly leave aside that office assigned to the apostles, which, being full of labor and toil, would interfere with his luxuries without bringing him any gain. Since heaven is opened to us by the teaching of the Gospel, it is by an elegant metaphor given the name of keys. Again, the only way in which men are bound and loosed is this: they are loosed when they are reconciled to God by faith, and bound when they are held more strictly by unbelief. If this were all that the Pope claimed for himself, I believe there would be no one to envy him or to raise the question. But because this laborious and far from profitable succession does not please the Pope at all, the dispute at once arises as to what it was that Christ promised to Peter. From the very nature of the case I infer that nothing more is meant than the dignity which cannot be separated from the burden of the apostolic office. For if the definition I have given is admitted (and it cannot be rejected without effrontery), nothing is given to Peter here that he did not share in common with his colleagues. On any other view, not only would injustice be done to their persons, but the very majesty of the doctrine would be diminished. They object; but what, I ask, is gained by striking against this stone? The most they can make out is that, as the preaching of the same gospel was committed to all the apostles, so the power of binding and loosing was bestowed on them in common. Christ (they say) appointed Peter prince of the whole Church when he promised to give him the keys. But what he then promised to one he elsewhere delivers, and as it were hands over, to all the rest. If the same right that was promised to one is bestowed on all, in what respect is that one superior to his colleagues? He excels (they say) in this, that he receives both in common and by himself what is given to the others only in common. What if I should answer with Cyprian and Augustine that Christ did not do this to prefer one man to another, but in order to commend the unity of his Church? For Cyprian speaks thus: "In the person of one man he gave the keys to all, that he might signify the unity of all; the rest, therefore, were the same as Peter was, being admitted to an equal share of honor and power, but a beginning is made from unity, so that the Church of Christ may be shown to be one" (Cyprian, de Simplic. Prælat.). Augustine's words are: "Had not the mystery of the Church been in Peter, our Lord would not have said to him, I will give you the keys. For if this was said to Peter, the Church does not have them; but if the Church has them, then when Peter received the keys he represented the whole Church" (August. Hom. in Joann. 50). Again: "All were asked, but Peter alone answers, You are the Christ; and it is said to him, I will give you the keys, as if he alone had received the power of loosing and binding; whereas he both spoke for all and received in common with all, being, as it were, the representative of unity. One received for all, because there is unity in all" (Hom. 124).

5¶

But, they say, we nowhere read of its being said to any other, "You are Peter, and upon this rock I will build my Church" (Matthew 16:18); as if Christ then affirmed anything else of Peter than what Paul and Peter himself affirm of all Christians (Ephesians 2:20; 1 Peter 2:5). The former describes Christ as the chief cornerstone, on whom all are built who grow into a holy temple in the Lord; the latter describes us as living stones that are founded on that elect and precious stone, and, being so joined and fitted together, are united to our God and to each other. Peter (they say) is above the others, because the name was given to him in particular. I willingly grant Peter the honor of being placed among the first in the building of the Church, or (if they prefer) of being the first among the faithful; but I will not allow them to infer from this that he holds a primacy over others. For what kind of inference is this? Peter surpasses others in fervent zeal, in doctrine, in greatness of soul; therefore he has power over them. As if we might not with greater plausibility infer that Andrew comes before Peter in order, because he preceded him in time and brought him to Christ (John 1:40, 42); but this I pass over. Let Peter have the preeminence; there is still a great difference between the honor of rank and the possession of power. We see that the apostles commonly left it to Peter to address the assembly, and in some measure to take the lead in reporting, exhorting, and admonishing; but we nowhere read anything at all about power.

6¶

Though we have not yet come to that part of the discussion, I would merely observe for the present how idly those men argue who, out of the bare name of Peter, would raise up a governing power over the whole Church. For the ancient quibble which they at first used to give it some color, namely, The Church is founded upon Peter, because it is said, "On this rock," and so on, is unworthy of notice, not to say of refutation. Some of the Fathers so expounded! French, "Ils ont Four leur bouelier, qu'aucuns des Peres les ont ainsi exposees." That is: They regard it as their shield that some of the Fathers have so expounded them.

7¶

And yet, in truth, no one can settle this question better than Scripture, if we compare all the passages in which it shows what office and power Peter held among the apostles, how he conducted himself among them, and how he was received by them (Acts 15:7). Go through all these passages, and the most you will find is that Peter was one of twelve, their equal and colleague, not their master. He does indeed bring the matter before the council when anything is to be done, and gives advice as to what is necessary; but at the same time he listens to the others, not only granting them an opportunity to express their views, but allowing them to decide; and when they have decided, he follows and obeys. When he writes to pastors, he does not command with authority as a superior, but makes them his colleagues, and courteously advises them as equals are accustomed to do (1 Peter 5:1). When he is accused of having gone in to the Gentiles, though the accusation is groundless, he answers it and clears himself (Acts 11:3). When his colleagues order him to go with John into Samaria, he does not refuse (Acts 8:14). The apostles, by sending him, declare that they by no means regard him as a superior, while he, by obeying and undertaking the mission entrusted to him, confesses that he is their associate and has no authority over them. But even if none of these facts existed, the one Epistle to the Galatians would easily remove all doubt, since there are almost two chapters in which the whole point Paul contends for is that, in regard to the honor of the apostleship, he is the equal of Peter (Galatians 1:18; 2:8). So he states that he went to Peter, not to acknowledge subjection, but only to make their agreement in doctrine plain to all; that Peter himself asked for no acknowledgment of the kind, but gave him the right hand of fellowship, so that they might be fellow laborers in the vineyard; that no less grace was bestowed on him among the Gentiles than on Peter among the Jews; and finally, that Peter, when he was not acting with strict faithfulness, was rebuked by him and submitted to the rebuke (Galatians 2:11). All these things make it plain either that there was an equality between Paul and Peter, or at least that Peter had no more authority over the rest than they had over him. This point, as I have said, Paul treats deliberately, so that no one might give Peter or John, who were colleagues and not masters, a preference over him in respect of the apostleship.

8¶

But even if I were to grant them what they ask with regard to Peter, namely, that he was the chief of the apostles and surpassed the others in dignity, there is no ground for making a universal rule out of a particular example, or for twisting a single fact into a perpetual law, since the two things are widely different. One man was chief among the apostles precisely because they were few in number. If one man presided over twelve, does it follow that one ought to preside over a hundred thousand? That twelve had one among them to direct all is nothing strange. Nature allows, and the human mind requires, that in every assembly, though all are equal in power, there should be one as a kind of moderator to whom the others look. There is no senate without a consul, no bench of judges without a president or chancellor, no college without a provost, no company without a master. So there would be nothing absurd in our admitting that the apostles had conferred such a primacy on Peter. But an arrangement that works among a few must not at once be transferred to the whole world, which no one man is able to govern. But (they say) it is observed that, no less in nature as a whole than in each of its parts, there is one supreme head. They are pleased to draw proof of this from cranes and bees, which always place themselves under the guidance of one, not of several. I accept the examples they bring forward; but do bees flock together from every part of the world to choose one queen? Each queen is content with her own hive. So among cranes, each flock has its own king. What can they prove from this, except that each church ought to have its own bishop? They point us to the examples of states, quoting from Homer, Οὐκ ἀγαθον πολυκοιρανιη, "a many-headed rule is not good," and other passages to the same effect from pagan writers in praise of monarchy. The answer is easy. Monarchy is not praised by Homer's Ulysses, or by others, as if one individual ought to govern the whole world; rather, they mean to indicate that one kingdom does not admit of two kings, and that power, as one writer puts it (Lucan, Book 1), cannot bear a partner.

9¶

Grant, however, that it is as they would have it (though the thing is utterly absurd; grant it), that it would be good and useful for the whole world to be under one monarchy; I will not on that account admit that the same thing should take effect in the government of the Church. Her only Head is Christ, under whose government we are all united to one another, according to that order and form of government which he himself has prescribed. Therefore they offer a gross insult to Christ when under this pretext they would have one man preside over the whole Church, since the Church can never be without a head, "even Christ, from whom the whole body fitly joined together, and compacted by that which every joint supplies, according to the effectual working in the measure of every part, makes increase of the body" (Ephesians 4:15, 16). See how all men without exception are placed in the body, while the honor and name of Head is left to Christ alone. See how to each member is assigned a certain measure, a finite and limited function, while both the fullness of grace and the supreme power of government reside in Christ alone. I am not unaware of the quibbling objection they are accustomed to raise, namely, that Christ is properly called the only Head because he alone reigns by his own authority and in his own name, but that there is nothing in this to prevent what they call another ministerial head from being under him and acting as his substitute. But this quibble cannot help them until they first show that this office was ordained by Christ. For the apostle teaches that the whole ministry of supply is spread through the members, while the power flows from one heavenly Head; The French adds, "Vision receue du Seigneur; Le Seigneur des armees l'a dit," that is: A vision received from the Lord; The Lord of hosts has spoken it.

10¶

Paul at times paints a living picture of the Church, but makes no mention of a single head. On the contrary, we may infer from his description that such a head is foreign to the institution of Christ. Christ by his ascension took his visible presence away from us, and yet he ascended that he might fill all things; now, therefore, he is present in the Church, and always will be. When Paul would show the way in which he makes himself present, he directs our attention to the ministerial offices which he employs: "To every one of us is given grace according to the measure of the gift of Christ"; "And he gave some, apostles; and some, prophets; and some, evangelists; and some, pastors and teachers" (Ephesians 4:10, 7, 11).

11¶

I will now make them another concession, which they will never obtain from men of sound mind, namely, that the primacy of the Church was fixed in Peter with the intention that it should remain forever by perpetual succession. Even so, how will they prove that his See was so fixed at Rome that whoever becomes bishop of that city is to preside over the whole world? By what authority do they attach this dignity to a particular place, when it was given without any mention of place? Peter, they say, lived and died at Rome. What did Christ himself do? Did he not discharge his episcopate while he lived, and complete the office of the priesthood by dying at Jerusalem? The Prince of pastors, the chief Shepherd, the Head of the Church, could not obtain honor for a place, and Peter, so far beneath him, could! Is this not worse than childish trifling? Christ conferred the honor of primacy on Peter; Peter had his See at Rome; therefore he fixed the seat of the primacy there. By this reasoning the Israelites of old must have placed the seat of the primacy in the wilderness, where Moses, the chief teacher and prince of prophets, carried out his ministry and died.

12¶

Let us see, however, how admirably they reason. Peter, they say, had the first place among the apostles; therefore the church in which he sat ought to have the privilege. But where did he sit first? At Antioch, they say. Therefore the church of Antioch rightly claims the primacy. They acknowledge that she was once the first, but say that Peter, by moving away from it, transferred to Rome the honor which he had brought with him. For there is extant, under the name of Pope Marcellus, a letter to the presbyters of Antioch, in which he says, "The See of Peter was at the outset with you, and was afterward, by the order of the Lord, moved here." So the church of Antioch, which was once the first, gave way to the See of Rome. But by what oracle did that good man learn that the Lord had so ordered? For if the question is to be settled in proper form, they must say whether they hold the privilege to be personal, or real, or mixed. It must be one of the three. If they say personal, then it has nothing to do with place; if real, then once it is given to a place it is not lost by the death or departure of the person. It remains that they must hold it to be mixed; in that case the mere consideration of place is not enough unless the person also corresponds. Let them choose whichever they will; I will at once infer, and easily prove, that Rome has no ground to claim the primacy.

13¶

However, let it be so. Let the primacy have been (as they vainly allege) transferred from Antioch to Rome. Why did Antioch not keep the second place? For if Rome has the first simply because Peter had his See there at the end of his life, to what place should the second be given sooner than to the place where he first had his See? How is it, then, that Alexandria takes precedence over Antioch? How can the church of a disciple be superior to the See of Peter? If honor is due to a church according to the dignity of its founder, what shall we say of other churches? Paul names three men who seemed to be pillars, namely, James, Peter, and John (Galatians 2:9). If in honor of Peter the first place is given to the Roman See, do not the churches of Ephesus and Jerusalem, where John and James were settled, deserve the second and third places? But in ancient times Jerusalem held the last place among the Patriarchates, and Ephesus could not secure even the lowest corner. Other churches too have passed away, churches which Paul founded and over which the apostles presided. The See of Mark, who was only one of the disciples, has obtained honor. Let them either confess that the arrangement was absurd, or grant that it is not always true that each church is entitled to the degree of honor which its founder possessed.

14¶

But I do not see that any credit is due to their claim that Peter occupied the Roman See. Certainly the statement of Eusebius, that he presided over it for twenty-five years, is easily refuted. For it appears from the first and second chapters of Galatians that he was at Jerusalem about twenty years after the death of Christ, and afterward came to Antioch. 110 D110 Calvin apparently believed that Paul's conversion took place about three years after the death of Christ; that Paul visited Peter in Jerusalem three years later (Galatians 1:18; Acts 9:26); that Paul saw Peter again at the Jerusalem Council fourteen years later (Galatians 2:1, 2:9; Acts 15:1-11); and that these three numbers were meant to be added together. He therefore places Peter at Jerusalem about twenty years after the death of Christ.

111 D111 Nero committed suicide in A.D. 68. Subtracting thirty-seven years brings us back to A.D. 31 as the date of Christ's death. Adding twenty years would place Peter in Jerusalem until A.D. 51. And after this date, Peter went to Antioch.

112 D112 Paul's arrival in Rome as a prisoner is placed at A.D. 60. If his epistle to the Romans was written four years before, then that book should be dated A.D. 56. In this letter there is no greeting to Peter (as would be expected if Peter had been bishop of Rome). In fact, there is not so much as a mention of him! (although many other names, some of them obscure, appear in the closing chapter). The implication that Peter was not in Rome at this time (A.D. 56) seems difficult to avoid.

15¶

Paul is afterward brought to Rome as a prisoner. Luke relates that he was received by the brothers, but says nothing of Peter. From Rome he writes to many churches. He even sends greetings from certain individuals, but does not by a single word indicate that Peter was there at the time. Who, I ask, will believe that he would have said nothing of him if he had been present? Indeed, in the Epistle to the Philippians, after saying that he had no one who cared for the work of the Lord as faithfully as Timothy, he complains that "all seek their own" (Philippians 2:21). 113 D113 During his first imprisonment in Rome (from A.D. 60 to 62), Paul wrote his epistle to the Philippians. No mention is made of Peter, but there is a strong commendation of Timothy, who not only was with Paul in Rome, but also sought the things which are Christ's (Philippians 2:19-21). In addition to Paul's own epistle, Luke's account of Paul's two-year imprisonment in Rome (in Acts 28) says nothing whatever about Peter. It would appear that Peter was not in Rome from A.D. 60 to 62.

114 D114 In Paul's second epistle to Timothy (the last of the Pauline epistles, dated A.D. 67), he states that at his first defense no one stood with him, but all forsook him (2 Timothy 4:17). Where, then, was Peter? A number of theories might be offered in reply to this question. Let us examine for: (1) Peter was in Rome, but in hiding. This theory is not very flattering to Peter, who in that case must have been among those who forsook Paul, and for whom Paul prayed that it might not be charged against them (implying wrongdoing on their part). (2) Peter, at the time 2 Timothy was written (in A.D. 67), had not yet arrived in Rome. This theory would hold that Peter arrived later in A.D. 67 and was martyred a short time after, perhaps in the spring of A.D. 68, with Paul. This view suffers from the fact that 2 Peter, believed to have been written by Peter at Rome, is dated A.D. 66. (3) Peter had already been martyred, and so obviously could not stand with Paul at his first defense. This theory would date Peter's death in Rome at A.D. 64, during Nero's persecution of the Christians following the great fire in Rome. Once again, this view conflicts with the date of the writing of 2 Peter, in A.D. 66. Peter's second epistle, plainly written by Peter, just as plainly could not have been written by someone who had died two years before he wrote it! (4) Peter was imprisoned in Rome at this time, expecting shortly to be executed (note 2 Peter 1:14), and so was simply unable to stand with Paul. This theory, although it has certain problems, nevertheless has one important feature in its favor. It can account for a two-year period of imprisonment (A.D. 66-68) ending in Peter's execution (during which period, in A.D. 67, he was unable to stand with Paul), and include within that period Peter's second epistle (dated A.D. 66).

115 D115 Calvin in this one sentence states his conclusions on three distinct questions: (a) Did Peter die in Rome? (b) Was Peter bishop of Rome? (c) If Peter was bishop of Rome, did he hold this office for a long period of time? With regard to the first question, Calvin does not dispute the claim that Peter died in Rome. Although there is no specific statement to this effect in Scripture, Peter's presence and martyrdom there are attested by so many early writers, including Clement of Rome, Ignatus, Papias, and Irenaeus, that there appears to be no sound reason to reject a tradition on which so many authors agree. With regard to the second and third questions, however, Calvin does not feel that he can answer yes. His reasons are found in the argument he has developed in this section and the previous one. If Calvin's development in these sections, and the appended notes, are substantially correct, then it would appear that at least three conclusions follow: (1) There is no evidence that Peter founded the church at Rome. (2) There is no evidence that Peter was in Rome for any considerable length of time. He may have been there, at the most, for six years (if he came in A.D. 62 and died in A.D. 68). He may have been there for five years (if he arrived in A.D. 66 and was executed in A.D. 68). (3) There is no evidence that Peter was the (first) bishop of Rome, or that such an office, distinct from that of elder, even existed at this early date. Such an office, clearly extra-biblical, appeared only later in the history of the Church.

16¶

Let us now come to the early Church, so that it may also appear that our opponents pride themselves on its support no less falsely and rashly than on the testimony of the word of God. They lay it down as an axiom that the unity of the Church cannot be maintained unless there is one supreme head on earth whom all the members must obey, and that accordingly our Lord gave the primacy to Peter, and after him, by right of succession, to the See of Rome, there to remain to the very end; and they assert that this has always been observed from the beginning. But since they improperly twist many passages, I would first state at the outset that I do not deny that the early Christians uniformly give high honor to the Roman Church and speak of it with reverence. This, I think, is owing chiefly to three causes. The opinion which had prevailed (I do not know how), that that Church was founded and established by the ministry of Peter, had great effect in winning it influence and authority. Hence in the East it was called, as a mark of honor, the Apostolic See. Secondly, since the seat of empire was there, and it was for this reason to be presumed that the men most distinguished for learning, prudence, skill, and experience were found there more than elsewhere, account was rightly taken of this circumstance, lest the fame of the city, and the far more excellent gifts of God as well, should seem to be despised. To these was added a third cause: when the churches of the East, of Greece, and of Africa were kept in constant turmoil by differences of opinion, the Church of Rome was calmer and less troubled. Because of this, godly and holy bishops, when driven from their sees, often took refuge in Rome as an asylum or haven. For as the people of the West are of a less sharp and restless turn of mind than those of Asia or Africa, so they are less eager for innovations. It therefore added very great authority to the Roman Church that in those uncertain times it was not so unsettled as others, and held more firmly to the doctrine once delivered, as will shortly be better explained. For these three causes, I say, she was held in no ordinary esteem, and received many distinguished testimonies from ancient writers.

17¶

But when our opponents would build on this a primacy and supreme authority over other churches, they, as I have said, greatly err. That this may appear more clearly, I will first briefly show what the views of early writers are concerning this unity which they press so strongly. Jerome, writing to Nepotian, after listing many examples of unity, comes down at length to the church hierarchy. He says, "Every bishop of a church, every archpresbyter, every archdeacon, and the whole ecclesiastical order depends on its own rulers." Here a Roman presbyter speaks and commends unity in church order. Why does he not mention that all the churches are bound together by one Head as a common bond? Nothing was more fitting to the point at hand, and it cannot be said that he left it out through forgetfulness; there was nothing he would more gladly have mentioned, had the fact allowed it. He therefore undoubtedly acknowledges that the true method of unity is the one which Cyprian admirably describes in these words: "The episcopate is one, a part of which is held in full by each bishop; and the Church is one, which by the increase of its fruitfulness spreads more widely in numbers. As there are many rays of the sun but one light, many branches of a tree but one trunk, upheld by its firm root, and as very many streams flow from one fountain, and though their number seems spread abroad by the abundance of the overflowing supply, yet unity is preserved whole at the source, so the Church, filled with the light of the Lord, sends her rays over the whole globe, and yet is one light, which is spread everywhere without dividing the unity of the body; she extends her branches over the whole globe and sends forth flowing streams; still the head is one, and the source one" (Cyprian, de Simplie. Prælat.). Afterward he says, "The bride of Christ cannot be an adulteress: she knows one house, and with chaste modesty keeps the sanctity of one bed." See how he makes the bishopric of Christ alone universal, as embracing the whole Church under it. See how he says that a part of it is held in full by all who discharge the episcopal office under this Head. Where is the primacy of the Roman See, if the entire bishopric resides in Christ alone, and a part of it is held in full by each? My purpose in these remarks is to show the reader, in passing, that that axiom of an earthly unity in the hierarchy, which the Romanists assume as admitted and beyond doubt, was altogether unknown to the ancient Church.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin's burden in this chapter is plain: the headship of the Church belongs to Christ alone, and no See, however ancient or honored, may claim a primacy over the whole body that Scripture never granted. Peter himself was colleague, not master; the keys were given to all, that the unity of the Church might be commended, not lodged in one man. For a believer or congregation in 2026, the lesson cuts in several directions. We live in an age eager to crown human authorities — celebrity pastors, vast networks, the personality whose word settles every question. Calvin would have us remember that the Church can never be without a head, even Christ, from whom the whole body grows. Honor your elders, prize faithful teaching, but anchor your soul to him alone whose right it is to rule his people. Where men exalt themselves, watch and test; where Christ is preached, gather and rest. The Church has one Head, and he is enough.
