Book 1 · Chapter 6 · USMC modern English

The Need of Scripture, as a Guide and Teacher, in Coming to God as a Creator

▶ Listen — read by Alan (modernized for 2026)
Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. God gives his elect a better help to the knowledge of himself—viz. the Holy Scriptures. This he did from the very first.
  2. 2. First, By oracles and visions, and the ministry of the Patriarchs. Secondly, By the promulgation of the Law, and the preaching of the Prophets. Why the doctrines of religion are committed to writing.
  3. 3. This view confirmed, 1. By the depravity of our nature making it necessary in every one who would know God to have recourse to the word; 2. From those passages of the Psalms in which God is introduced as reigning.
  4. 4. Another confirmation from certain direct statements in the Psalms. Lastly, From the words of our Saviour.
1¶

The brightness that meets every eye, both in the heavens and on the earth, leaves the ingratitude of mankind without excuse. To bring the whole human race under the same condemnation, God holds out to all people, without exception, a mirror of his deity in his works. Even so, another and better help must be given to guide us rightly to God as our Creator. It was not in vain, then, that he added the light of his Word, so that he might make himself known to us for our salvation. He granted this privilege to those whom he was pleased to bring into a nearer and more familiar relationship with himself. For he saw how the minds of people were carried back and forth, finding no sure resting place. So he chose the Jews as a people set apart, and then fenced them in, so that they might not wander off into error as others had done. And it is not in vain that, by this same means, he keeps us in the knowledge of himself. For without it, even those who seem to stand strong in comparison with others would quickly fall away. Consider the elderly, or those whose eyesight is failing. When a beautiful book is set before them, they can tell that something is written there, yet they can barely make out two words in a row. But once they are helped by glasses, they begin to read clearly. In the same way, Scripture gathers together the impressions of God's deity, which until then lay confused in our minds. It scatters the darkness and shows us the true God clearly. God therefore gives a gift of singular value when, for the instruction of the Church, he uses not silent teachers only, but opens his own sacred mouth. He does more than proclaim that some god must be worshiped: he declares that he is the God to whom worship is due. He does more than teach his chosen people to regard God: he reveals himself as the God they are to regard.

From the very beginning, this was the way God dealt with his Church: he supplemented these common proofs by adding his Word, as a surer and more direct means of disclosing himself. And there can be no doubt that it was by this help that Adam, Noah, Abraham, and the other patriarchs reached that intimate knowledge which, in a sense, set them apart from unbelievers. I am not speaking now of the particular doctrines of faith that lifted them up to the hope of eternal blessedness. In passing from death to life, they had to know God not only as Creator but also as Redeemer, and both kinds of knowledge they certainly received from the Word. In order, however, the knowledge first given was the kind that made them acquainted with the God by whom the world was made and is governed. To this first knowledge was later added the deeper knowledge which alone brings dead souls to life. By it God is known not only as the Creator of all things and the sole author and disposer of all that happens, but also as a Redeemer, in the person of the Mediator. But since the fall and the corruption of our nature have not yet been considered, I will set aside the question of the remedy for now (for that, see Book 2, chapter 6 and following). So let the reader remember that I am not now dealing with the covenant by which God adopted the children of Abraham, nor with that branch of teaching by which believers, grounded in Christ, have in every age been properly set apart from the ungodly nations. I am only showing that we must turn to Scripture in order to learn the sure marks that distinguish God, the Creator of the world, from the whole crowd of made-up gods. We will consider the work of redemption later, in its proper place. Meanwhile, though we will bring forward many passages from the New Testament, and some from the Law and the Prophets as well, in which Christ is expressly mentioned, our only aim will be to show that God, the Maker of the world, is revealed to us in Scripture, and that his true character is set forth there, so that we are not left wandering up and down, as in a maze, searching for some uncertain deity.

2¶

Whether God revealed himself to the fathers by oracles and visions (that is to say, by testimonies from heaven),

Tertullian, in his Apology against the Nations, writes that, so that we might approach more fully and more deeply both God himself and his arrangements and his will, he added the instrument of Scripture, and so on.

3¶

If we consider how easily the human mind slips into forgetfulness of God, how readily it is drawn toward every kind of error, and how it leans, again and again, toward inventing new and made-up religions, it becomes easy to see how necessary it was to establish a storehouse of doctrine. Such a record would keep that teaching from perishing through neglect, from fading away amid our errors, or from being corrupted by the reckless boldness of people. It is plain, then, that God, foreseeing that the image he had stamped on the beautiful form of the universe would not be enough, has given the help of his Word to all whom he has ever been pleased to teach effectively. We too must follow this straight path, if we genuinely long for a true vision of God. We must go, I say, to the Word, where the character of God, drawn from his works, is described accurately and to the life. There his works are weighed not by our corrupt judgment, but by the standard of eternal truth. If we turn aside from the Word, then no matter how fast we move, we will never reach the goal, because we are off the course. We should bear in mind that the brightness of God's face, which even an apostle calls unapproachable (1 Timothy 6:16), is a kind of maze, a maze we cannot escape unless the Word serves as a thread to guide our way. It is better to limp along the right path than to run with great speed off of it. So the Psalmist, after repeatedly declaring (in Psalms 93, 96, 97, 99, and others) that superstition must be banished from the world so that pure religion may flourish, presents God as reigning. By that word he means not the power God possesses and exercises in governing all of nature, but the teaching by which he upholds his rightful supremacy. For error can never be rooted out of the human heart until the true knowledge of God has been planted in it.

4¶

So the same prophet, after saying that the heavens declare the glory of God, that the sky shows forth the work of his hands, and that the steady succession of day and night proclaims his majesty, goes on to speak of the Word. "The law of the Lord," he says, "is perfect, converting the soul; the testimony of the Lord is sure, making wise the simple. The statutes of the Lord are right, rejoicing the heart; the commandment of the Lord is pure, enlightening the eyes" (Psalm 19:1-9). For although the law serves other purposes as well (on which see Book 2, chapter 7, sections 6, 10, and 12), its general meaning is that it is the proper school for training the children of God. The invitation given to all nations to behold him in the heavens and the earth proved of no use. The same view appears in Psalm 29, where the Psalmist, after speaking of the dreadful voice of God, which in thunder, wind, rain, whirlwind, and storm shakes the earth, makes the mountains tremble, and breaks the cedars, ends by saying that in his temple everyone speaks of his glory. Unbelievers, meanwhile, are deaf to all God's words when they ring out in the open air. In the same way, another Psalm, after describing the raging waves of the sea, concludes, "Your testimonies are very sure; holiness becomes your house forever" (Psalm 93:5). The words of our Savior to the Samaritan woman point the same way, when he told her that her nation and all other nations worshiped what they did not know, and that the Jews alone gave worship to the true God (John 4:22). Since the human mind, in its weakness, was wholly unable to come to God unless helped and held up by his sacred Word, it followed of necessity that all people, except the Jews, were toiling in emptiness and error, because they sought God apart from the Word.

Calvin's point here is plain and bracing — the works of creation leave every man without excuse, yet they cannot lead us home; for that, God adds his Word, the spectacles through which dim eyes finally read his true character clearly. A faithless age tells us the divine is whatever the sunset stirs in us, and many who name Christ drift on that same current, content with a God they have felt but never heard. We are not. The Lord did not leave his Church to grope after a doubtful deity in the labyrinth of her own impressions; he opened his sacred mouth, and the family that would know him must go where he has spoken. So gather your household to Scripture, not to vague spirituality — read it at the table, bind it on your children, let it correct what nature only hints. The heavens declare his glory, but his Word converts the soul; keep your feet on that straight path, for it is better to limp in the way than to run swiftly out of it.
