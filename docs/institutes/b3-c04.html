1¶

I come now to examine what the scholastic sophists teach concerning repentance. I will do this as briefly as possible, for I have no intention of taking up every point, lest this work, which I wish to frame as a compendium of doctrine, should exceed all bounds. They have managed to wrap a matter that is otherwise not very complicated in so many perplexities that, once you are plunged even a little way into their mire, it will be hard to find a way out. And first, in giving a definition, they plainly show that they never understood what repentance means. For they fasten on certain expressions in the writings of the Fathers which come nowhere near expressing the nature of repentance. For instance, that to repent is to deplore past sins and not to commit what is to be deplored. Again, that it is to bewail past evils and not to do again what is to be bewailed. Again, that it is a kind of grieving revenge, punishing in oneself what one grieves to have committed. Again, that it is sorrow of heart and bitterness of soul for the evils which a person has committed, or to which he has consented. The first definition is Gregory's, and is contained in Sentent. Lib. 4 Dist. 14, c. 1. The second, which is Ambrose's, is given in the same place, and also in Decret. Dist. 3, de Pœnitentia C. Pœnit. Prior. The third is Augustine's, as stated in the same place, and in C. Pœnit Poster. The fourth is from Ambrose, and is given in Dist. 1, de Pœnit C. Vera Pœitentia.

3¶

If they say that this is slander on my part, let them come forward and point out a single person who, through this doctrine of contrition, has not either been driven to despair, or else set up a pretended fear, in place of a true one, against the justice of God. We have observed elsewhere that forgiveness of sins can never be obtained without repentance, because none but the afflicted, and those wounded by a consciousness of their sins, can sincerely implore the mercy of God. But we added at the same time that repentance cannot be the cause of the forgiveness of sins, and we also did away with that torment of souls, the dogma that it must be performed as something owed. Our doctrine was that the soul looks not to its own compunction or its own tears, but fixes both eyes on the mercy of God alone. We only observed that those who labor and are heavy laden are called by Christ, seeing that he was sent "to preach good tidings to the meek," "to bind up the brokenhearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives, and the opening of the prison to them that are bound," "to comfort all that mourn." (Matthew 11:28; Isaiah 59:1; Luke 4:18.)

4¶

Confession has always been a subject of sharp contention between the Canonists and the Scholastic Theologians, the former contending that confession is of divine authority, the latter insisting, on the contrary, that it is merely enjoined by ecclesiastical constitution. In this contest great effrontery has been displayed by the Theologians, who have corrupted and violently twisted every passage of Scripture they have quoted in their favor. Erasmus, in a letter to Augustine Steuchus in 1531, while flattering him, at the same time laughs at him for thinking that the fifth chapter of Numbers sufficiently proves, against Luther, that auricular confession is from God.

French, "N'est ce pas bien se jouer des Escritures, de les tourner en ceste facon?" — is this not truly to make sport of Scripture, to twist it in this fashion?

The French is, "Car ce que Jesus Christ laisse aux Prestres de la loy, n'appartient en rien à ses vrais ministres;" — for what Jesus Christ leaves to the priests does not belong in any respect to his true ministers.

5¶

They draw their second argument from the same fountain, I mean allegory, as if allegories were of much value in confirming any doctrine. But, indeed, let them have their value, if those which I am able to produce are not more plausible than theirs. They say, then, that the Lord, after raising Lazarus, commanded his disciples to "loose him and let him go" (John 11:44). Their first statement is untrue: we nowhere read that the Lord said this to the disciples. It is much more probable that he spoke to the Jews who were standing by, so that, with no room for suspicion of fraud, the miracle might be more evident, and his power might be all the more conspicuous because he raised the dead without touching him, by a mere word. In the same way, I understand that our Lord, to leave the Jews no ground for suspicion, wished them to roll back the stone, smell the stench, perceive the sure signs of death, see him rise by the mere power of a word, and be the first to handle him when alive. And this is the view of Chrysostom (Serm. C. Jud. Gent. et Haeret). But granting that it was said to the disciples, what can they gain by it? That the Lord gave the apostles the power of loosing? How much more aptly and skillfully might we allegorize and say that by this symbol the Lord meant to teach his followers to loose those whom he raises up; that is, not to bring to remembrance the sins which he himself had forgotten, not to condemn as sinners those whom he had acquitted, not to keep reproaching those whom he had pardoned, not to be stern and severe in punishing while he himself was merciful and ready to forgive. Certainly nothing should more incline us to pardon than the example of the Judge who threatens that he will be inexorable toward the rigid and inhumane. Let them go now and peddle their allegories. French, "Qu'ils voisent maintenant, et facent un bouclier de leur allegories;" — let them go now and make a shield of their allegories.

6¶

They now come to closer quarters, as they support their view with passages of Scripture which they think are clearly in their favor. Augustin. Epist. 54.

7¶

I wonder at their effrontery in venturing to maintain that the confession of which they speak is of divine authority. We admit that its use is very ancient, but we can easily prove that at one time it was free. It certainly appears from their own records that no law or constitution concerning it was enacted before the days of Innocent III. Surely, if there had been a more ancient law, they would have fastened on it, instead of being content with the decree of the Lateran Council and so making themselves ridiculous even to children. In other matters they do not hesitate to coin fictitious decrees, which they ascribe to the most ancient Councils, in order to blind the eyes of the simple with veneration for antiquity. In this instance it did not occur to them to practice this deception; and hence, by their own testimony, three centuries have not yet passed since the bridle was put on, and the necessity of confession imposed, by Innocent III. And to say nothing of the time, the mere barbarism of the terms used destroys the authority of the law. For when these worthy fathers enjoin that every person of both sexes (utriusque sexus) must once a year confess his sins to his own priest, men of wit humorously object that the precept binds hermaphrodites only, and does not apply to anyone who is either a male or a female. A still grosser absurdity has been displayed by their disciples, who are unable to explain what is meant by one's own priest (proprius sacerdos). Let all the hired ravers of the Pope babble as they may. French, "Quoy que tous les advocats et procureurs du Pape, et tous les caphars qu'il a à louage gazouillent:" — whatever all the advocates and procurators of the Pope, and all the caphars whom he has in his pay, may gabble.

8¶

This abrogation is so clearly attested in so many passages by Chrysostom, who lived at Constantinople and was himself a prelate of the church there, that it is strange they can venture to maintain the contrary. "Tell your sins," he says, "that you may blot them out. If you are ashamed to tell another what sins you have committed, tell them daily in your soul. I do not say, tell them to your fellow servant, who may reproach you, but tell them to God, who heals them. Confess your sins upon your bed, so that your conscience may there daily recognize its iniquities." Again: "Now, however, it is not necessary to confess before witnesses; let the examination of your faults be made in your own thought; let the judgment be without a witness; let God alone see you confessing." Again: "I do not lead you publicly into the view of your fellow servants; I do not force you to disclose your sins to men; review your conscience and lay it open before God. Show your wounds to the Lord, the best of physicians, and seek medicine from him. Show them to him who does not reproach, but heals most kindly." Again: "Certainly do not tell it to man, lest he reproach you. Nor must you confess to your fellow servant, who may make it public; but show your wounds to the Lord, who cares for you, who is kind and can heal." He afterward introduces God speaking thus: "I do not oblige you to come into the middle of a theater and have many witnesses; tell your sins to me alone in private, that I may heal the ulcer." Chrysost. Hom. 2 in Psal. 1. Serm. de pœnit. et Confess. Hom. 5 De Incomprehensibili Dei. Nat. cont. Anomeos. Item, Hom. 4 de Lazaro.

9¶

But so that the whole matter may be unfolded more plainly, we shall first honestly state the nature of confession as delivered in the word of God, and afterward add their inventions — not all of them, indeed (for who could drink up that boundless sea?), but only those which contain the sum of their secret confession. Here I am grieved to mention how frequently the old interpreter Latin, "Vetus interpres." French, "Le translateur tant Grec qui Latin;" — the Greek as well as the Latin translator.

10¶

He who has embraced this confession from the heart, and as in the presence of God, will doubtless have a tongue ready to confess whenever there is occasion among men to proclaim the mercy of God. He will not be content to whisper the secret of his heart once into the ear of one individual, but will often, and openly, and in the hearing of the whole world, candidly make mention both of his own disgrace and of the greatness and glory of the Lord. In this way David, after he was accused by Nathan, being stung in his conscience, confesses his sin before God and men. "I have sinned unto the Lord," he says (2 Samuel 12:13); that is, I now have no excuse, no evasion; all must judge me a sinner; and what I wished to keep secret with the Lord must also be made manifest to men. Hence the secret confession which is made to God is followed by voluntary confession to men, whenever that serves the glory of God or our own humbling. For this reason the Lord in ancient times commanded the people of Israel to repeat the words after the priest and to make public confession of their iniquities in the temple, because he foresaw that this was a necessary help to enable each one to form a just estimate of himself. And it is fitting that by confessing our misery we should make manifest the mercy of our God both among ourselves and before the whole world.

11¶

It is proper that this kind of confession should both be ordinary in the Church and also be used especially on extraordinary occasions, when the people as a whole happen to have fallen into some fault. Of this latter kind we have an example in the solemn confession which the whole people made under the authority and guidance of Ezra and Nehemiah (Nehemiah 1:6, 7). For since their long captivity, the destruction of the temple, and the suppression of their religion had been the common punishment of their defection, they could not make a fitting acknowledgment of the blessing of deliverance without first confessing their guilt. And it does not matter that in one assembly it may sometimes happen that a few are innocent, since the members of a languid and sickly body cannot boast of soundness. Indeed, it is scarcely possible that these few have not contracted some taint, and so bear part of the blame. Therefore, as often as we are afflicted with pestilence, or war, or famine, or any other calamity whatever, if it is our duty to betake ourselves to mourning, fasting, and other signs of guilt, confession also, on which all the others depend, is not to be neglected. As for that ordinary confession which the Lord has, moreover, expressly commended, no sober man who has reflected on its usefulness will venture to disapprove of it. Since in every sacred assembly we stand in the sight of God and the angels, how should our service begin except by acknowledging our own unworthiness? But this, you will say, is done in every prayer; for as often as we pray for pardon, we confess our sins. I admit it. But if you consider how great is our carelessness, or drowsiness, or sloth, you will grant me that it would be a salutary ordinance if the Christian people were exercised in humility by some formal method of confession. For though the ceremony which the Lord enjoined on the Israelites belonged to the tutelage of the Law, yet the thing itself belongs in some respect to us also. And indeed, in all well-ordered churches, in keeping with a useful custom, the minister each Lord's Day frames a form of confession in his own name and that of the people, in which he makes a common confession of iniquity and asks pardon from the Lord. In short, by this key a door of prayer is opened privately for each and publicly for all.

12¶

Scripture approves two other forms of private confession. The one is made on our own account, and to it the passage in James refers, "Confess your sins one to another" (James 5:16); for the meaning is that by disclosing our weaknesses to each other we are to obtain the help of mutual counsel and consolation. The other is to be made for the sake of our neighbor, to appease and reconcile him if he has been injured in any respect by our fault. In the former, although James, by not naming any particular individual into whose bosom we are to unburden our feelings, leaves us free to confess to any member of the church who may seem fittest, yet since pastors are for the most part to be presumed better qualified than others, our choice ought chiefly to fall on them. And the ground of this preference is that the Lord, by calling them to the ministry, points them out as the persons by whose lips we are to be taught to subdue and correct our sins and to draw consolation from the hope of pardon. For as the duty of mutual admonition and correction is committed to all Christians, but is especially enjoined on ministers, so, while we all ought to console one another and confirm one another in confidence in the divine mercy, we see that ministers, to assure our consciences of the forgiveness of sins, are appointed to be its witnesses and sponsors, so that they themselves are said to forgive sins and loose souls (Matthew 16:19; 18:18). When you hear this attributed to them, reflect that it is for your benefit. Let every believer, therefore, remember that if in private he is so agonized and afflicted by a sense of his sins that he cannot obtain relief without the help of others, it is his duty not to neglect the remedy which God provides for him — namely, to have recourse for relief to private confession to his own pastor, and, for consolation, privately to implore the help of him whose task it is, both in public and in private, to comfort the people of God with the doctrine of the Gospel. But we must always use moderation, lest in a matter for which God prescribes no fixed rule our consciences be burdened with a definite yoke. Hence it follows, first, that confession of this kind ought to be free, so as not to be exacted of all, but only recommended to those who feel that they need it; and, secondly, that even those who use it according to their need must neither be compelled by any precept nor artfully induced to enumerate all their sins, but only so far as they judge it to be for their good, so that they may obtain the full benefit of consolation. Faithful pastors, if they would avoid both tyranny in their ministry and superstition in the people, must not only leave this liberty to the churches, but defend it and strenuously vindicate it.

13¶

Of the second form of confession our Savior speaks in Matthew: "If thou bring thy gift to the altar, and there remember that thy brother has ought against thee; leave there thy gift before the altar; first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift" (Matthew 5:23, 24). Thus love, which has been interrupted by our fault, must be restored by acknowledging the fault and asking pardon for it. Under this head is included the confession of those who by their sin have given offense to the whole Church (above, section 10). For if Christ attaches so much importance to the offense of one individual that he forbids the sacrifice of all who have sinned in any respect against their brethren until by due satisfaction they have regained their favor, how much greater reason is there that he who by some evil example has offended the Church should be reconciled to it by acknowledging his fault? Thus the member of the Church of Corinth was restored to communion after he had humbly submitted to correction (2 Corinthians 2:6). This form of confession existed in the ancient Christian Church, as Cyprian relates: "They practice repentance," he says, "for a proper time; then they come to confession, and by the laying on of the hands of the bishop and clergy they are admitted to communion." Scripture knows nothing of any other form or method of confessing, and it is not for us to bind new chains upon consciences which Christ most strictly forbids to be brought into bondage. Meanwhile, as for the flock presenting themselves before the pastor whenever they would partake of the Holy Supper, I am so far from disapproving of it that I very much desire it to be observed everywhere. For those whose conscience is troubled may obtain singular benefit from it, and those who need admonition thus provide an opportunity for it; provided always that no countenance is given to tyranny and superstition.

16¶

To each of these views I will briefly reply. As to their binding the souls of believers by their laws, whether justly or unjustly, I say nothing at present, since that will be seen in its proper place. But their enacting it as a law that all sins are to be enumerated; their denying that sin is discharged except on condition that the resolution to confess has been firmly conceived; their pretense that there is no entrance into Paradise if the opportunity of confession has been neglected — these are things impossible to bear. Are all sins to be enumerated? But David, who, I presume, had honestly pondered within himself the confession of his sins, exclaimed, "Who can understand his errors? Cleanse thou me from secret faults" (Psalm 19:12); and in another passage, "Mine iniquities are gone over my head: as a heavy burden they are too heavy for me" (Psalm 38:4). He knew how deep the abyss of our sins was, how numerous the forms of wickedness, how many heads the hydra carried, how long a tail it drew. Therefore he did not sit down to make a catalog, but from the depth of his distress cried to the Lord, "I am overwhelmed, and buried, and sore vexed; the gates of hell have encircled me: let thy right hand deliver me from the abyss into which I am plunged, and from the death which I am ready to die." Who can now think of reckoning up his sins when he sees that David was unable to number his?

17¶

By this ruinous procedure the souls of those who were touched with some sense of God have been most cruelly racked. First they betook themselves to calculation, proceeding according to the formula given by the Schoolmen and dividing their sins into boughs, branches, twigs, and leaves; then they weighed the qualities, quantities, and circumstances; and in this way, for a time, matters went forward. But after they had advanced farther and looked around, nothing was seen but sea and sky; no road, no harbor. The longer the distance they ran over, the longer still the distance that met the eye; indeed, lofty mountains began to rise, and there seemed no hope of escape, at least not until after long wanderings. They were thus brought to a dead halt, until at length the only way out was found in despair. Here these cruel murderers, to ease the wounds which they had made, applied certain soothing poultices. Everyone was to do his best. But new cares again disturbed, indeed new torments tortured, their souls: "I have not spent enough time; I have not exerted myself enough; I have omitted many things through negligence; forgetfulness that comes from want of care is not excusable." Then new drugs were supplied to ease their pains: "Repent of your negligence; and provided it was not done carelessly, it will be pardoned." All these things, however, could not heal the wound, being not so much remedies for the sore as poison smeared with honey, so that its bitterness might not at once offend the taste, but might penetrate to the vitals before it could be detected. The dreadful voice, therefore, was always heard ringing in their ears, "Confess all your sins," and the dread it caused could not be quieted without sure consolation. Here let my readers consider whether it is possible to take account of the actions of a whole year, or even to gather up the sins committed in a single day, since every man's experience convinces him that at evening, in examining the faults of that single day, memory becomes confused, so great is the number and variety presented. I am not speaking of dull and heartless hypocrites who, after taking note of three or four of their grosser offenses, think the work finished, but of the true worshipers of God, who, after they have made their examination and feel themselves overwhelmed, still add the words of John: "If our heart condemn us, God is greater than our heart, and knoweth all things" (1 John 3:20); and who therefore tremble at the thought of that Judge whose knowledge far surpasses our comprehension.

18¶

Though a good part of the world rested in these soothing suggestions, by which this fatal poison was somewhat tempered, it was not because they thought that God was satisfied, or that they had fully satisfied themselves; it was rather like an anchor cast out in the middle of the deep, which interrupts the voyage for a little while, or like a weary, worn-out traveler who lies down by the way. The French is, "Mais comme les nautonniers fichans l'anchre au milieu de la mer, se reposent du trauail de leur navigation; ou comme un perlin lassé ou defaillant se sied au milieu de la voye pour reposer: en telle maniere ils prenoyent ce repos, combien qu'il ne leur fust suffisant;" — but as sailors casting anchor in the middle of the sea rest from the toil of navigation, or as a pilgrim, weary or faint, sits down in the middle of the road to rest himself, so they took this rest, though it was not sufficient for them.

"Tous ceux que nous lisons avoir obtenu de Christ la remission de leurs pechez, ne sont pas dits s'etre confessés à l'aureille de quelque Messire Jean;" — none of those whom we read of as having obtained the forgiveness of their sins from Christ are said to have confessed in the ear of some Mess John.

19¶

It is no wonder, therefore, that we condemn that auricular confession as a thing pestilent in its nature and in many ways harmful to the Church, and desire to see it abolished. But even if the thing were in itself indifferent, yet since it is of no use or benefit, and has given occasion to so much impiety, blasphemy, and error, who does not think that it ought to be abolished at once? They list some of its uses and boast of them as very beneficial, but these are either fictitious or of no importance. One thing they especially commend: that the blush of shame in the penitent is a severe punishment, which makes him more cautious for the future and anticipates divine punishment by his punishing himself. As if a man were not sufficiently humbled with shame when he is brought under the scrutiny of God at his supreme tribunal! Admirable progress indeed — if we cease to sin because we are ashamed to let one man know of it, and do not blush at having God as the witness of our evil conscience! The assertion as to the effect of shame, however, is most unfounded, for we may see everywhere that nothing gives men greater confidence and license in sinning than the idea that, after making confession to priests, they can wipe their lips and say, I have not done it. And not only do they grow bolder in sin throughout the whole year, but, secure against confession for the remainder of it, they never sigh after God, never examine themselves, but keep heaping sins upon sins until, as they suppose, they get rid of them all at once. And when they have got rid of them, they think they are relieved of their load, and imagine that they have deprived God of the right of judging by giving it to the priest, and have made God forgetful by making the priest aware. Moreover, who is glad when he sees the day of confession approaching? Who goes to confess with a cheerful mind, and does not rather go unwillingly and, as it were, struggling against it, as if he were being dragged to prison with a rope around his neck? — with the exception, perhaps, of the priests themselves, who take a fond delight in exchanging accounts of their own misdeeds as a kind of merry tales. I will not pollute my page by recounting the monstrous abominations with which auricular confession teems. I only say that if that holy man (Nectarius, of whom see above, section 7) did not act rashly when, because of one rumor of fornication, he banished confession from his church, or rather from the memory of his people, the innumerable acts of prostitution, adultery, and incest which it produces in the present day warn us of the necessity of abolishing it.

20¶

As to the pretense of the confessionaries concerning the power of the keys, and their placing in it, so to speak, the sum and substance of their kingdom, we must see what force it ought to have. Were the keys, then, given without a cause, they ask? Was it said without a cause, "Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth shall be bound in heaven, and whatsoever ye shall loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven" (Matthew 18:18)? Do we make void the word of Christ? I answer that there was a weighty reason for giving the keys, as I lately explained, and will show again at greater length when I come to treat of excommunication (Book 4, chapter 12). But what if I should cut off the handle for all such questions with one sword — namely, that priests are neither vicars nor successors of the Apostles? But that also will be considered elsewhere (Book 4, chapter 6). Now, at the very place where they are most eager to fortify themselves, they set up a battering-ram by which all their own machinations are overthrown. Christ did not give his Apostles the power of binding and loosing before he endowed them with the Holy Spirit. I deny, therefore, that any man who has not first received the Holy Spirit is competent to possess the power of the keys. I deny that anyone can use the keys unless the Holy Spirit goes before, teaching and dictating what is to be done. They pretend, indeed, that they have the Holy Spirit, but by their works they deny him; unless, indeed, we are to suppose that the Holy Spirit is some vain thing of no value, as they certainly do pretend, but we will not believe them. With this engine they are completely overthrown: whatever the door of which they boast of having the key, we must always ask whether they have the Holy Spirit, who is the arbiter and ruler of the keys. If they reply that they have, we must ask again whether the Holy Spirit can err. This they will not venture to say outright, although by their doctrine they indirectly insinuate it. We must therefore infer that no priestlings have the power of the keys, because everywhere and indiscriminately they loose what the Lord was pleased should be bound, and bind what he has ordered to be loosed.

21¶

When they see themselves convicted on the clearest evidence of loosing and binding worthy and unworthy without distinction, they lay claim to power without knowledge. And although they dare not deny that knowledge is required for its proper use, they still affirm that the power itself has been given to bad administrators. This, however, is the power: "Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth shall be bound in heaven, and whatsoever ye shall loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven." Either the promise of Christ must be false, or those who are endowed with this power bind and loose properly. There is no room for the evasion that the words of Christ are limited according to the merits of him who is loosed or bound. We admit that none can be bound or loosed but those who are worthy of being bound or loosed. But the preachers of the Gospel and the Church have the word by which they can measure this worthiness. By this word preachers of the Gospel can promise forgiveness of sins to all who are in Christ by faith, and can pronounce a sentence of condemnation against all and upon all who do not embrace Christ. In this word the Church declares that "neither fornicators, nor idolaters, nor adulterers," "nor thieves, nor covetous, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor extortioners shall inherit the kingdom of God" (1 Corinthians 6:9, 10). Such persons it binds in sure fetters. By the same word it looses and consoles the penitent. But what kind of power is it which does not know what is to be bound or loosed? You cannot bind or loose without knowledge. Why, then, do they say that they absolve by authority given to them, when the absolution is uncertain? As far as we are concerned, this power is merely imaginary if it cannot be used. Now, I hold that either there is no use, or one so uncertain as to be virtually no use at all. For when they confess that a good part of the priests do not use the keys rightly, and that power without its lawful use is ineffectual, who is to assure me that the one by whom I am loosed is a good dispenser of the keys? But if he is a bad one, what better has he given me than this worthless dispensation: What is to be bound or loosed in you I do not know, since I do not have the proper use of the keys; but if you deserve it, I absolve you? As much might be done, I do not say by a layman (since they would scarcely listen to such a statement), but by the Turk or the devil. For it amounts to saying, I do not have the word of God, the sure rule for loosing, but authority has been given me to absolve you, if you deserve it. We see, therefore, what their aim was when they defined the keys (see section 16) as authority to discern and power to execute, and said that knowledge is added as a counselor and advises the proper use: their aim was to reign wantonly and licentiously, without God and his word.

22¶

Should anyone object, first, that the lawful ministers of Christ will be no less perplexed in the discharge of their duty, because absolution, which depends on faith, will always be doubtful; and, secondly, that sinners will receive no comfort at all, or only cold comfort, because the minister, who is not a competent judge of their faith, is not certain of their absolution — we are prepared with an answer. They say that no sins are remitted by the priest except those of which he has knowledge; thus, according to them, remission depends on the judgment of the priest, and unless he discerns accurately who are worthy of pardon, the whole procedure is null and void. In short, the power of which they speak is a jurisdiction joined to an examination, to which pardon and absolution are restricted. Here no firm footing can be found; indeed, there is a bottomless abyss. For where confession is not complete, the hope of pardon also is defective; next, the priest himself must necessarily remain in suspense, since he does not know whether the sinner gives a faithful enumeration of his sins; lastly, such is the rudeness and ignorance of priests that the greater part of them are in no respect fitter to perform this office than a cobbler is to cultivate the fields, while almost all the rest have good reason to suspect their own fitness. Hence the perplexity and doubt about the Popish absolution, since they choose to found it on the person of the priest, and not on his person only, but on his knowledge, so that he can judge only of what is laid before him, investigated, and ascertained. Now, if anyone should ask these good doctors whether the sinner is reconciled to God when some of his sins are remitted, I do not know what answer they could give, unless they should be forced to confess that whatever the priest pronounces concerning the remission of the sins which have been enumerated to him will be of no avail, so long as the others are not exempted from condemnation. On the part of the penitent, again, it is obvious from this in what a state of harmful anxiety his conscience will be held; because, while he leans on what they call the discernment of the priest, he cannot come to any decision from the word of God. From all these absurdities the doctrine which we deliver is completely free. For absolution is conditional, allowing the sinner to trust that God is gracious to him, provided he sincerely seeks expiation in the sacrifice of Christ and accepts the grace offered to him. Thus he cannot err who, in the capacity of a herald, proclaims what has been dictated to him from the word of God. The sinner, again, can receive a clear and sure absolution when, in regard to embracing the grace of Christ, the simple condition attached is in keeping with the general rule of our Master himself — a rule impiously spurned by the Papacy — "According to your faith be it unto you" (Matthew 9:29).

34¶

He who, smitten by the hand of God, thinks that God is a judge inflicting punishment, cannot conceive of him except as angry and hostile toward him; he cannot but detest the rod of God as curse and condemnation; in short, he can never persuade himself that he is loved by God while he feels that God is still disposed to inflict punishment on him. He alone profits under the divine chastening who considers that God, though offended with his sins, is still gracious and favorable to him. Otherwise, his feeling must necessarily be what the Psalmist complains that he had experienced: "Thy wrath lieth hard upon me, and thou hast afflicted me with all thy waves." Also what Moses says: "For we are consumed by thine anger, and by thy wrath we are troubled. Thou hast set our iniquities before thee, our secret sins in the light of thy countenance. On the other hand, David, speaking of fatherly chastisements, to show how believers are helped by them rather than crushed, sings thus: "Blessed is the man whom thou chastenest, O Lord, and teachest him out of thy law; that thou mayest give him rest from the days of adversity, until the pit be digged for the wicked" (Psalm 94:12, 13). It is certainly a sore temptation when God, sparing unbelievers and overlooking their crimes, appears more severe toward his own people. Hence, to console them, he adds the admonition of the law, which teaches them that their salvation is being provided for when they are brought back to the right path, whereas the wicked are carried headlong in their errors, which lead at last to the pit. It does not matter whether the punishment is eternal or temporary. For disease, pestilence, famine, and war are curses from God, as much as the sentence of eternal death itself, whenever their tendency is to operate as instruments of divine wrath and vengeance against the reprobate.

35¶

All, if I am not mistaken, now see what the Lord had in view in chastening David: namely, to prove that murder and adultery are most offensive to God, and to manifest this offensiveness in a beloved and faithful servant, so that David himself might be taught never again to dare to commit such wickedness. Still, it was not a punishment designed as payment of a kind of compensation to God. In the same way are we to judge of that other correction, in which the Lord subjects his people to a grievous pestilence for the disobedience of David in so far forgetting himself as to number the people. He indeed freely forgave David the guilt of his sin; but because it was necessary, both as a public example to all ages and also to humble David himself, not to let such an offense go unpunished, he chastened him most sharply with his whip. We ought also to keep this in view in the universal curse of the human race. For since, after obtaining grace, we still continue to endure the miseries pronounced on our first parent as the penalty of transgression, we ought thereby to be reminded how offensive to God the transgression of his law is, so that, humbled and cast down by a consciousness of our wretched condition, we may aspire more ardently to true happiness. But it would be most foolish for anyone to imagine that we are subjected to the calamities of the present life for the guilt of sin. This seems to me to have been Chrysostom's meaning when he said, "If the purpose of God in inflicting punishment is to bring those persisting in evil to repentance, when repentance is manifested punishment would be superfluous" (Chrysos. Homily. 3 de Provid.). Therefore, as he knows what the disposition of each requires, he treats one with greater harshness and another with more indulgence. Accordingly, when he wishes to show that he is not excessive in exacting punishment, he rebukes a hardhearted and obstinate people because, after being smitten, they still continued in sin (Jeremiah 5:3). In the same sense he complains that "Ephraim is a cake not turned" (Hosea 7:8), because chastisement did not make a due impression on their minds and, by correcting their vices, make them fit to receive pardon. Surely he who speaks in this way shows that as soon as anyone repents he will be ready to receive him, and that the rigor which he exercises in chastising faults is wrung from him by our perverseness, since we ought to anticipate him by a voluntary correction. Since, however, such is the hardness and rudeness of all hearts that they universally stand in need of chastisement, our infinitely wise Parent has seen fit to exercise all without exception, throughout their whole lives, with chastisement. It is strange how they fix their eyes so intently on the one example of David, and are not moved by the many examples in which they might have beheld the free forgiveness of sins. The publican is said to have gone down from the temple justified (Luke 18:14); no punishment follows. Peter obtained the pardon of his sin (Luke 22:61). "We read of his tears," says Ambrose (Serm. 46, De Poenit. Petri); "we do not read of satisfaction." To the paralytic it is said, "Son, be of good cheer; thy sins be forgiven thee" (Matthew 9:2); no penance is enjoined. All the acts of forgiveness mentioned in Scripture are free. The rule ought to be drawn from these numerous examples rather than from one example which contains a kind of special feature.

36¶

Daniel, in exhorting Nebuchadnezzar to break off his sins by righteousness and his iniquities by showing mercy to the poor (Daniel 4:27), did not mean to suggest that righteousness and mercy are able to propitiate God and redeem from punishment (far be it from us to suppose that there was ever any other ἀπολύτροσις (ransom) than the blood of Christ); but the breaking off referred to in that passage has reference to man rather than to God. It is as if he had said: O king, you have exercised an unjust and violent dominion; you have oppressed the humble, plundered the poor, and treated your people harshly and unjustly; instead of unjust exaction, instead of violence and oppression, now practice mercy and justice. In like manner, Solomon says that love covers a multitude of sins — not, however, with God, but among men. For the whole verse reads thus: "Hatred stirreth up strifes; but love covereth all sins" (Proverbs 10:12). Here, after his manner, he contrasts the evils produced by hatred with the fruits of love, in this sense: Those who hate are constantly biting, carping at, reproaching, and tearing one another, making a fault of everything; but those who love conceal one another's faults, overlook many, forgive many — not that the one approves the vices of the other, but he bears with them and cures them by admonition rather than exasperating them by attack. That the passage is quoted by Peter (1 Peter 4:8) in the same sense we cannot doubt, unless we would charge him with corrupting or craftily twisting Scripture. When it is said that "by mercy and truth iniquity is purged" (Proverbs 16:6), the meaning is not that compensation is made to the Lord by them, so that he, being thus satisfied, remits the punishment which he would otherwise have exacted; but intimation is given, after the familiar manner of Scripture, that those who forsake their vices and iniquities and turn to the Lord in truth and piety will find him gracious: as if he had said that the wrath of God is calmed, and his judgment is at rest, whenever we rest from our wickedness. But indeed, it is not the cause of pardon that is described, but rather the mode of true conversion; just as the Prophets frequently declare that it is in vain for hypocrites to offer God fictitious rites instead of repentance, since his delight is in integrity and the duties of love. French, "Integrité, pitié, droiture, et choses semblables;" — integrity, pity, uprightness, and the like.

37¶

As to the passage in Luke (Luke 7:36, sq.), no man of sober judgment who reads the parable there employed by our Lord will raise any controversy with us. The Pharisee thought that the Lord did not know the character of the woman whom he had so readily admitted to his presence. For he presumed that he would not have admitted her if he had known what kind of sinner she was; and from this he inferred that one who could be deceived in this way was not a prophet. Our Lord, to show that she was not a sinner, inasmuch as she had already been forgiven, spoke this parable: "There was a certain creditor which had two debtors: the one owed five hundred pence, and the other fifty. And when they had nothing to pay, he frankly forgave them both. Tell me, therefore, which of them will love him most?" The Pharisee answers: "I suppose that he to whom he forgave most." Then our Savior replies: "Her sins, which are many, are forgiven; for she loved much." By these words it is plain that he does not make love the cause of forgiveness, but the proof of it. The comparison is borrowed from the case of a debtor to whom a debt of five hundred pence had been forgiven. It is not said that the debt is forgiven because he loved much, but that he loved much because it was forgiven. The comparison ought to be applied in this way: You think this woman is a sinner; but you ought to have acknowledged her as not a sinner, inasmuch as her sins have been forgiven her. Her love ought to have been to you a proof that she had obtained forgiveness, that love being an expression of gratitude for the benefit received. It is an argument a posteriori, by which something is demonstrated from the results it produces. Our Lord plainly attests the ground on which she had obtained forgiveness when he says, "Thy faith has saved thee." By faith, therefore, we obtain forgiveness; by love we give thanks and bear testimony to the loving-kindness of the Lord.

38¶

I am little moved by the numerous passages in the writings of the Fathers relating to satisfaction. I see, indeed, that some (I will frankly say almost all whose books are extant) have either erred in this matter or spoken too roughly and harshly; but I cannot admit that they were so ignorant and unskilled as to write these passages in the sense in which they are read by our new satisfactionaries. Chrysostom somewhere says, "When mercy is implored, interrogation ceases; when mercy is asked, judgment does not rage; when mercy is sought, there is no room for punishment; where there is mercy, no question is asked; where there is mercy, the answer gives pardon" (Chrysos. Hom. 2 in Psal. 50). However much these words may be twisted, they can never be reconciled with the dogmas of the Schoolmen. In the book De Dogmatibus Ecclesiasticis, which is attributed to Augustine, you read (cap. 54): "The satisfaction of repentance is to cut off the causes of sins, and not to allow an entrance to their suggestions." From this it appears that the doctrine of satisfaction, said to be paid for sins committed, was everywhere derided in those ages; for here the only satisfaction referred to is caution, abstinence from sin for the future. I am unwilling to quote what Chrysostom says (Hom. 10 in Genes), that God requires nothing more of us than to confess our faults before him with tears, since similar sentiments abound both in his writings and in those of others. Augustine, indeed, calls works of mercy remedies for obtaining forgiveness of sins (Enchir. ad Laur.); but lest anyone should stumble at the expression, he himself removes the difficulty in another passage. "The flesh of Christ," he says, "is the true and only sacrifice for sins — not only for those which are all blotted out in baptism, but also for those into which we are afterward betrayed through weakness, and because of which the whole Church daily cries, 'Forgive us our debts' (Matthew 6:12). And they are forgiven by that special sacrifice."

39¶

By satisfaction, however, they for the most part meant not compensation to be paid to God, but the public testimony by which those who had been punished with excommunication, and wished to be received again into communion, assured the Church of their repentance. For those penitents were enjoined certain fasts and other things, by which they might prove that they were truly, and from the heart, weary of their former life, or rather might blot out the remembrance of their past deeds. In this way they were said to give satisfaction, not to God, but to the Church. The same thing is expressed by Augustine in a passage in his Enchiridion ad Laurentium, cap. 65. It is quoted in the Decret. c. in Art. de Pœnit. Dist. 1.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin dismantles the medieval machinery of forced confession and human satisfaction and sets in its place the one foundation Scripture allows: Christ alone bore our sins in his own body, the sole propitiation, the perpetual Advocate, so that no penance of ours and no priest's tally can add a farthing to a finished work. Confession is owed first and freely to God, and where it is made to men it is for counsel and comfort, never to bind the conscience Christ died to free. For the believer in 2026 — surrounded by subtler systems that still tempt us to earn back God's favor by our own striving, our public displays, our self-imposed penances — this is liberating and sobering at once. Bring your sin to the throne in private and to a faithful pastor when your soul is too burdened to bear alone, but rest your peace nowhere but in the blood. Teach your household that grace cannot be purchased and need not be; it is received, with empty hands and a clear conscience, from the Lamb who took it all away.
