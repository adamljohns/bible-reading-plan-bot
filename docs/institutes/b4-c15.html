Book 4 · Chapter 15 · USMC modern English

Of Baptism

There are two parts of this chapter,—I. Dissertation on the two ends of Baptism, sec. 1-13. II. The second part may be reduced to four heads. Of the use of Baptism, sec. 14, 15. Of the worthiness or unworthiness of the minister, sec. 16-18. Of the corruptions by which this sacrament was polluted, sec. 19. To whom reference is had in the dispensation, sec. 20-22. 1. Baptism defined. Its primary object. This consists of three things. 1. To attest the forgiveness of sins. 2. Passages of Scripture proving the forgiveness of sins. 3. Forgiveness not only of past but also of future sins. This no encouragement to license in sin. 4 Refutation of those who share forgiveness between Baptism and Repentance. 5 Second thing in Baptism—viz. to teach that we are ingrafted into Christ for mortification and newness of life. 6. Third thing in Baptism—viz. to teach us that we are united to Christ so as to be partakers of all his blessings. Second and third things conspicuous in the baptism both of John and the apostles. 7. Identity of the baptism of John and the apostles. 8. An objection to this refuted. 9. The benefits of baptism typified to the Israelites by the passage of the Red Sea and the pillar of cloud. 10. Objection of those who imagine that there is some kind of perfect renovation after baptism. Original depravity remains after baptism. Its existence in infants. The elect after baptism are righteous in this life only by imputation. 11. Original corruption trying to the pious during the whole course of their lives. They do not, on this account, seek a licence for sin. They rather walk more cautiously and safely in the ways of the Lord. 12. The trouble occasioned by corruption, shown by the example and testimony of the Apostle Paul. 13. Another end of baptism is to serve as our confession to men. 14. Second part of the chapter. Of baptism as a confirmation of our faith. 15. This illustrated by the examples of Cornelius and Paul. Of the use of baptism as a confession of faith. 16. Baptism not affected by the worthiness or unworthiness of the minister. Hence no necessity to rebaptise those who were baptised under the Papacy. 17. Nothing in the argument that those so baptised remained some years blind and unbelieving. The promise of God remains firm. God, in inviting the Jews to repentance, does not enjoin them to be again circumcised. 18. No ground to allege that Paul rebaptised certain of John’s disciples. The baptism of John. What it is to be baptised in the name of Christ. 19. The corruptions introduced into baptism. The form of pure Christian baptism. Immersion or sprinkling should be left free. 20. To whom the dispensation of baptism belongs. Not to private individuals or women, but to the ministers of the Church. Origin of the baptism of private individuals and women. An argument in favour of it refuted. 21. Exploded also by Tertullian and Epiphanius. 22. Objection founded on the case of Zipporah. Answer. Children dying before baptism not excluded from heaven, provided the want of it was not caused by negligence or contempt.

1¶

Baptism is the initiatory sign by which we are admitted into the fellowship of the Church, so that, being grafted into Christ, we may be counted as children of God. The purpose for which God has given it (and I have shown this to be common to all the mysteries) is, first, that it may serve our faith in him, and second, that it may serve as a confession before others. I will explain the nature of both purposes in turn. Baptism contributes three things to our faith, and each of these must be treated separately. The first reason the Lord appointed it, then, is to be a sign and proof of our cleansing. Or, to make my meaning clearer, it is a kind of sealed certificate by which he assures us that all our sins are so blotted out, covered, and wiped away that they will never come before his sight, never be mentioned, and never be charged against us. For it is his will that all who have believed should be baptized for the forgiveness of sins. Some have therefore thought that baptism is nothing more than a badge or mark by which we profess our religion before others, just as soldiers display the insignia of their commander to show whom they serve. But such people have failed to notice the main thing in baptism, which is this: that we are to receive it together with the promise, "He who believes and is baptized will be saved" (Mark 16:16).

Scripture cited
Mark 16:16
2¶

It is in this sense that we should understand Paul's words, that "Christ loved the Church and gave himself for it, that he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the Word" (Ephesians 5:25, 26), and again, "Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, by the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit" (Titus 3:5). Peter also says that "baptism now saves us" (1 Peter 3:21). For Peter did not mean to suggest that our washing and salvation are accomplished by water, or that water has in itself the power to purify, regenerate, and renew, nor that it is the cause of salvation. He meant only that the knowledge and certainty of these gifts are received in this sacrament. The words themselves make this plain. For Paul joins together the Word of life and the baptism of water, as if he had said: by the gospel the message of our cleansing and sanctification is announced; by baptism this message is sealed. And Peter at once adds that this baptism is "not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God," which comes from faith. Indeed, the only cleansing that baptism promises is the cleansing that comes through the sprinkling of the blood of Christ, who is pictured by the water because it resembles cleansing and washing. Who, then, can say that we are cleansed by that water, when the water itself plainly testifies that the blood of Christ is our true and only washing? So we can have no stronger argument against the delusion of those who attribute everything to the power of the water than the very meaning of baptism itself, which leads us away both from the visible element presented to our eyes and from every other means, so that it may fix our minds on Christ alone.

Scripture cited
Eph 5:25, 26 · Titus 3:5 · 1 Peter 3:21
3¶

Nor should we suppose that baptism is given only with reference to the past, as though, for fresh sins into which we fall after baptism, we must seek new remedies of atonement in other so-called sacraments, as if the power of baptism had worn out. It was because of this error that, in ancient times, some refused to be baptized until their lives were in extreme danger and they were drawing their last breath, so that they might in this way obtain pardon for their whole past. The ancient bishops often denounce this backward kind of caution in their writings. We ought to recognize that, at whatever time we are baptized, we are washed and cleansed once for our entire life. Therefore, as often as we fall, we must recall the memory of our baptism and so strengthen our minds that we may feel certain and secure of the forgiveness of sins. For although baptism, once administered, may seem to be in the past, it is not cancelled by sins that come later. For the purity of Christ was offered to us in it; that purity is always in force and is destroyed by no stain; it wipes and washes away all our defilements. Yet from this we must not take license to sin in the future (there is certainly nothing in baptism to encourage such boldness). This teaching is meant only for those who, when they have sinned, groan under the weight and oppression of their sins, so that they may have something to support and comfort them and may not plunge headlong into despair. Thus Paul says that Christ was made a propitiation for us for the forgiveness of sins that are past (Romans 3:25). By this he does not deny that constant and perpetual forgiveness of sins is obtained through Christ even until death. He means only that this forgiveness is intended by the Father for those poor sinners who, wounded by remorse of conscience, long for the physician. To these the mercy of God is offered. But those who, hoping to escape punishment, seek license to sin only provoke the wrath and justice of God.

Scripture cited
Rom 3:25
4¶

I know it is commonly believed that the forgiveness which we receive at our first regeneration by baptism alone is, after baptism, obtained instead by means of penance and the keys (see chapter 19, section 17). But those who hold this fiction err because they fail to consider that the power of the keys they speak of depends so completely on baptism that it ought never, on any account, to be separated from it. The sinner receives forgiveness through the ministry of the Church; in other words, not apart from the preaching of the gospel. And what is the substance of this preaching? That we are washed from our sins by the blood of Christ. And what is the sign and proof of that washing, if not baptism? We see, then, that this forgiveness has reference to baptism. This error arose from the made-up sacrament of penance, which I have already touched on. The rest will be said in its proper place. It is no wonder that people who, because of the dullness of their minds, are excessively attached to outward things have shown the same fault here too. Not content with the pure institution of God, they have brought in new helps of their own devising, as if baptism were not itself a sacrament of penance. But if repentance is to be practiced throughout our whole life, then the power of baptism ought to reach just as far. Therefore there can be no doubt that all the godly may, throughout the whole course of their lives, whenever they are troubled by an awareness of their sins, recall the memory of their baptism, so that they may assure themselves of that one and perpetual cleansing which we have in the blood of Christ.

5¶

Another benefit of baptism is that it shows us our dying with Christ and our new life in him. "Do you not know," the apostle says, "that all of us who were baptized into Jesus Christ were baptized into his death? Therefore we are buried with him by baptism into death," so that we "should walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:3, 4). With these words he does not merely urge us to imitate Christ, as if to say that baptism reminds us to die to our sinful desires just as Christ died, and to rise to righteousness just as he rose. He traces the matter much deeper: that Christ, by baptism, has made us sharers in his death, grafting us into it. And just as a branch draws substance and nourishment from the root to which it is joined, so those who receive baptism with true faith truly feel the power of Christ's death in the putting to death of their flesh, and the power of his resurrection in the quickening of the Spirit. On this he builds his exhortation: that if we are Christians we should be dead to sin and alive to righteousness. He uses the same argument elsewhere, that we are circumcised and have put off the old self once we have been buried in Christ by baptism (Colossians 2:12). And in this sense, in the passage we quoted earlier, he calls it "the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit" (Titus 3:5). We are promised, first, the free pardon of sins and the imputation of righteousness, and second, the grace of the Holy Spirit to form us anew to newness of life.

Scripture cited
Rom 6:3, 4 · Col 2:12 · Tit 3:5
6¶

The last benefit our faith receives from baptism is that it assures us not only that we are grafted into the death and life of Christ, but that we are so united to Christ himself as to share in all his blessings. For he consecrated and sanctified baptism in his own body, so that he might share it with us as the firmest bond of the union and fellowship he was pleased to form with us. And so Paul proves that we are the sons of God from the fact that we put on Christ in baptism (Galatians 3:27). Thus we see that our baptism is fulfilled in Christ, whom for this reason we call the proper object of baptism. It is therefore not strange that the apostles are said to have baptized in the name of Christ, even though they were commanded to baptize in the name of the Father and the Spirit as well (Acts 8:16; 19:5; Matthew 28:19). For all the divine gifts held out in baptism are found in Christ alone. And yet whoever baptizes into Christ cannot help also calling on the name of the Father and the Spirit. For we are cleansed by Christ's blood precisely because our gracious Father, out of his incomparable mercy and his will to receive us into favor, appointed him as Mediator to bring about our reconciliation with himself. We obtain regeneration from his death and resurrection alone, when, sanctified by his Spirit, we are filled with a new and spiritual nature. Therefore we obtain, and in a way clearly perceive, in the Father the cause, in the Son the substance, and in the Spirit the effect of our cleansing and regeneration. This is how John baptized first, and how the apostles afterward baptized: with the baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins, understanding by the word "repentance" regeneration, and by "the forgiveness of sins" cleansing.

Scripture cited
Gal 3:27 · Acts 8:16 · Mt 28:19
7¶

This makes it perfectly certain that John's ministry was the very same as the ministry later entrusted to the apostles. For the different hands by which baptism is administered do not make it a different baptism; rather, sameness of doctrine proves it to be the same. John and the apostles agreed in one doctrine. Both baptized for repentance, both for the forgiveness of sins, and both in the name of Christ, from whom repentance and forgiveness of sins proceed. John pointed to him as the Lamb of God who takes away the sins of the world (John 1:29), describing him as the sacrifice accepted by the Father, the propitiation that satisfies righteousness, and the author of salvation. What could the apostles add to this confession? Therefore let no one be troubled because ancient writers labor to distinguish the one from the other. Their opinions ought not to weigh so heavily with us as to shake the certainty of Scripture. For who would listen to Chrysostom denying that the forgiveness of sins was included in John's baptism (Homily on Matthew 1:14), rather than to Luke, who on the contrary asserts that John preached "the baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins" (Luke 3:3)? Nor can we accept Augustine's subtlety, that by John's baptism sins were forgiven in hope, but by Christ's baptism they are forgiven in reality. For since the Evangelist plainly declares that John in his baptism promised the forgiveness of sins, why take away from this praise when no necessity requires it? If anyone should ask what difference the Word of God makes, he will find it to be nothing more than this: that John baptized in the name of the one who was to come, while the apostles baptized in the name of the one who had already been revealed (Luke 3:16; Acts 19:4).

Scripture cited
John 1:29 · Mt 1:14 · Luke 3:3 · Luke 3:16 · Acts 19:4
8¶

The fact that the gifts of the Spirit were poured out more generously after Christ's resurrection does not establish a difference of baptisms. For the baptism administered by the apostles while Christ was still on the earth was called his baptism, and yet the Spirit was not poured out more abundantly on it than on the baptism of John. Indeed, not even after the ascension did the Samaritans receive the Spirit beyond the ordinary measure given to earlier believers, until Peter and John were sent to lay hands on them (Acts 8:14-17). I suspect that what misled the ancient writers, and made them say that the one baptism was only a preparation for the other, was that they read how those who had received John's baptism were baptized again by Paul (Acts 19:3-5; Matthew 3:11). How greatly they are mistaken in this will be explained most clearly in its proper place. Why, then, did John say that he baptized with water, but that one was coming who would baptize with the Holy Spirit and with fire? This can be explained in a few words. He did not mean to distinguish one baptism from another. Rather, he contrasted his own person with the person of Christ, saying that, while he himself was a minister of water, Christ was the giver of the Holy Spirit, and would display this power by a visible miracle on the day he sent the Holy Spirit upon the apostles in the form of tongues of fire. What greater boast could the apostles make, and what greater those who baptize today? For they are only ministers of the outward sign, while Christ is the author of the inward grace, as those same ancient writers consistently teach, and Augustine in particular, who, in his refutation of the Donatists, builds chiefly on this principle: whoever it is that baptizes, Christ alone presides.

Scripture cited
Acts 8:14-17 · Acts 19:3-5 · Mt 3:11
9¶

What we have said, both about dying to sin and about cleansing, was foreshadowed among the people of Israel, who for that reason are described by the apostle as having been baptized in the cloud and in the sea (1 Corinthians 10:2). Their dying to sin was pictured when the Lord, rescuing them from the hand of Pharaoh and from cruel bondage, opened a way for them through the Red Sea and drowned Pharaoh himself and their Egyptian enemies, who were pressing close behind and threatening them with destruction. For in this way too he promises us in baptism, and shows by a given sign, that we are led by his power and delivered from the captivity of Egypt, that is, from the bondage of sin; that our Pharaoh is drowned, in other words, the devil, although he never stops trying to attack and harass us. But just as that Egyptian was not sunk into the depths of the sea but cast up on the shore, and still alarmed the Israelites by the terror of his appearance even though he could not harm them, so our enemy still threatens us, displays his weapons, and is felt, but cannot conquer. The cloud was a symbol of cleansing (Numbers 9:18). For just as the Lord then covered them with a sheltering cloud and kept them cool, so that they would not faint or waste away under the burning rays of the sun, so in baptism we recognize that we are covered and protected by the blood of Christ, so that the wrath of God, which is truly an unbearable flame, may not fall upon us. Although this mystery was then obscure and known to few, yet since there is no other way of obtaining salvation than through these two graces, God was pleased that the ancient fathers, whom he had adopted as heirs, should be furnished with both signs.

Scripture cited
1 Cor 10:2 · Num 9:18
10¶

It is now clear how false the teaching is which some held long ago, and which others still cling to: that by baptism we are released and set free from original sin and from the corruption passed down from Adam to all his descendants, and that we are restored to the same righteousness and purity of nature that Adam would have had if he had kept the integrity in which he was created. This kind of teacher never understands what is meant by original sin, original righteousness, or the grace of baptism. Now it has been shown earlier (Book 2, chapter 1, section 8) that original sin is the depravity and corruption of our nature, which first makes us liable to the wrath of God, and then produces in us works that Scripture calls the works of the flesh (Galatians 5:19). Two things, therefore, must be carefully observed: first, that we are corrupted and perverted in every part of our nature, and second, that on account of this corruption we are justly held to be condemned and convicted before God, to whom nothing is acceptable but purity, innocence, and righteousness. And so even infants bring their condemnation with them from their mother's womb. For although they have not yet brought forth the fruits of their unrighteousness, they carry its seed within them. Indeed, their whole nature is, as it were, a seed of sin, and therefore cannot but be hateful and detestable to God. Believers are assured by baptism that this condemnation is entirely taken away from them, since (as has been said) the Lord by this sign promises that a full and complete forgiveness has been made, both of the guilt charged against us and of the penalty that guilt deserves. They also lay hold of righteousness, but the kind of righteousness that the people of God can obtain in this life, that is, by imputation only, God in his mercy regarding them as righteous and innocent.

Scripture cited
Gal 5:19
11¶

Another point is that this corruption never stops working in us, but constantly produces new fruit, namely those works of the flesh we described earlier, just as a burning furnace endlessly sends out flame and sparks, or a fountain is always pouring out water. For sinful desire never wholly dies or is extinguished in us, until, set free by death from the body of death, we have entirely laid aside our own nature (Book 3, chapter 3, sections 10-13). Baptism, indeed, tells us that our Pharaoh is drowned and sin put to death; yet not so that it no longer exists or causes any trouble, but only so that it no longer has dominion. For as long as we live shut up in this prison of the body, the remnants of sin dwell in us; but if we faithfully hold to the promise God has given us in baptism, they will neither rule nor reign. But let no one deceive himself, and let no one look complacently on his disease, when he hears that sin always dwells in us. When we say this, it is not so that those who are already too inclined to sin may sleep securely in their sins, but only so that those who are tested and stung by the flesh may not faint and lose heart. Rather, let them reflect that they are still on the journey, and let them consider that they have made great progress when they feel their sinful desire growing somewhat weaker from day to day, until they reach the goal they aim at, namely the final death of the flesh, a death that will be completed at the end of this mortal life. Meanwhile, let them not stop striving hard, rousing themselves to further progress and pressing on to complete victory. Their efforts should be spurred on by the thought that, after a long struggle, much still remains to be done. We must hold firmly that we are baptized for the putting to death of our flesh, which is begun in baptism, carried on every day, and will be finished when we depart from this life to go to the Lord.

12¶

Here we say nothing more than what the apostle Paul sets out most clearly in the sixth and seventh chapters of the letter to the Romans. He had spoken of free justification; but because certain wicked men inferred from this that they could live however they pleased, since their acceptance with God was not earned by the merit of works, he adds that all who are clothed with the righteousness of Christ are at the same time regenerated by the Spirit, and that we have a pledge of this regeneration in baptism. He therefore urges believers not to let sin reign in their bodies. And because he knew that there is always some weakness in believers, so that they might not be cast down on this account, he adds, for their comfort, that they are not under the law. Again, since there might seem to be a danger that Christians would grow presumptuous because they were not under the yoke of the law, he shows what this abolition of the law really means, and at the same time what the use of the law is. This question he had already postponed a second time. The substance of it is this: that we are freed from the strictness of the law in order that we may cling to Christ, and that the office of the law is to convict us of our depravity and make us confess our helplessness and misery. Moreover, since this evil of our nature is not so easily seen in a godless man who indulges his passions without fear of God, Paul gives an example in a regenerate man, namely, in himself. He therefore says that he had a constant struggle with the remnants of his flesh, and was held in wretched bondage, so that he could not give himself entirely to the obedience of God's law. So he is driven to groan and cry out, "O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death?" (Romans 7:24). But if the children of God are held captive in prison as long as they live, they must necessarily feel deep anxiety at the thought of their danger, unless their fears are quieted. For this one purpose, then, he adds the comfort that there is "now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). He thus teaches that those whom the Lord has once received into favor, grafted into communion with Christ, and admitted into the fellowship of the Church by baptism, are freed from guilt and condemnation as long as they persevere in faith in Christ, even though they are still beset by sin and so carry sin about with them. If this is the plain and genuine meaning of Paul, we cannot think there is anything strange in the doctrine he sets forth here. We follow, then, the teaching of St. Paul word for word when we say that in baptism sin is forgiven as to its guilt, but that it always remains as to its substance in all Christians until death.

Scripture cited
Rom 7:24 · Rom 8:1
13¶

Baptism serves as our confession before others, in that it is a mark by which we openly declare that we want to be counted among the people of God; by which we testify that we join with all Christians in the worship of one God and in one religion; and by which, in short, we publicly assert our faith, so that not only do our hearts breathe out the praise of God, but our tongues also, and every member of our body, in every way they can, proclaim it. In this way, as is fitting, everything we have is made to serve the glory of God, which ought to be displayed everywhere, and others are stirred by our example to do the same. Paul was referring to this when he asked the Corinthians whether or not they had been baptized in the name of Christ (1 Corinthians 1:13), pointing out that, by the very fact of having been baptized in his name, they had given themselves to him, and had sworn and bound themselves in allegiance to him before others, so that they could no longer confess any other than Christ alone, unless they were willing to renounce the confession they had made in baptism.

Scripture cited
1 Cor 1:13
14¶

Now that I have explained the purpose God had in mind when he instituted baptism, it is easy to see how we ought to use it and receive it. Since it is given to lift up, nourish, and strengthen our faith, we are to receive it as from the hand of its author. We are to be firmly persuaded that it is he himself who speaks to us by means of the sign; that it is he himself who washes and purifies us, and blots out the memory of our faults; that it is he himself who makes us partakers of his death, destroys the kingdom of Satan, subdues the power of our sinful desires, and indeed makes us one with himself, so that, clothed with him, we may be counted the children of God. These things, I say, we ought to feel in our minds as truly and as certainly as we see our body washed, immersed, and surrounded with water. For this analogy gives us the surest rule in the sacraments: in physical things we are to see spiritual ones, just as if they were actually set before our eyes, since the Lord has been pleased to represent them by such figures. This does not mean that these graces are contained and bound up in the sacrament so as to be conferred by its own power. It means only that by this token the Lord declares to us that he is pleased to bestow all these things upon us. Nor does he merely feed our eyes with an empty show. He leads us to the reality itself, and effectually accomplishes what he pictures.

15¶

We have proof of this in Cornelius the centurion. After he had already been given the graces of the Holy Spirit, he was baptized for the remission of sins. He was not seeking a fuller forgiveness from baptism, but a surer exercise of faith—indeed, grounds for assurance drawn from a pledge. Someone may perhaps object: Why did Ananias say to Paul that he washed away his sins by baptism (Acts 22:16), if sins are not washed away by the power of baptism? I answer that we are said to receive, obtain, and lay hold of whatever, according to the perception of our faith, the Lord sets before us—whether he then attests it for the first time, or gives further confirmation to what he had already attested. So all that Ananias meant was this: Be baptized, Paul, so that you may be assured that your sins are forgiven you. In baptism the Lord promises forgiveness of sins; receive it, and rest secure. I have no wish, however, to take anything away from the power of baptism. I would only join to the sign the substance and the reality, since God works by external means. But from this sacrament, as from all the others, we gain nothing except so far as we receive in faith. If faith is lacking, baptism will be evidence of our ingratitude, by which we are proved guilty before God for not believing the promise given there. Insofar as it is a sign of our confession, we ought by it to testify that we trust in the mercy of God, and that we are pure through the forgiveness of sins which Christ Jesus has obtained for us; that we have entered into the Church of God, so that with one consent of faith and love we may live in harmony with all believers. This was Paul's meaning when he said that “by one Spirit are we all baptized into one body” (1 Cor. 12:13).

Scripture cited
Acts 22:16 · 1 Cor 12:13
16¶

Furthermore, if we have rightly concluded that a sacrament is not to be measured by the hand of the one who administers it, but is to be received as from the hand of God himself, from whom it most certainly came, then we may infer from this that its worth neither rises nor falls with the one who administers it. Among men, when a letter has been sent, if the handwriting and the seal are recognized, it makes not the slightest difference who or what the messenger was. In the same way, it ought to be enough for us to recognize the hand and seal of our Lord in his sacraments, whoever the one administering them may be. This refutes the error of the Donatists, who measured the power and worth of the sacrament by the worthiness of the minister. Such, in our own day, are our Anabaptists, who deny that we have been truly baptized, because we were baptized under the Papacy by wicked men and idolaters; and so they furiously insist that we must be baptized again. We will be sufficiently guarded against these absurdities if we consider that by baptism we were initiated not into the name of any man, but into the name of the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit; and therefore that baptism is not of man, but of God, by whomever it may have been administered. Grant that those who baptized us were utterly ignorant of God and of all godliness, or were despisers of him—still they did not baptize us into fellowship with their ignorance or sacrilege, but into the faith of Jesus Christ, because the name they called upon was not their own but God's, nor did they baptize into any other name. But if baptism was of God, then it certainly carried within it the promise of forgiveness of sins, mortification of the flesh, the quickening of the Spirit, and communion with Christ. So it did the Jews no harm to have been circumcised by impure and apostate priests. It did not void the symbol so as to make it necessary to repeat it. It was enough to return to its genuine origin. The objection that baptism ought to be celebrated in the assembly of the godly does not prove that it loses all its power because it is partly defective. When we show what ought to be done to keep baptism pure and free from every taint, we do not abolish God's institution, even though idolaters may corrupt it. Long ago circumcision was corrupted by many superstitions, and yet it did not cease to be regarded as a symbol of grace; nor did Josiah and Hezekiah, when they gathered out of all Israel those who had revolted from God, call them to be circumcised again.

17¶

Again, they ask us what faith followed our baptism for several years, hoping by this to prove that our baptism was in vain, since it is not made effective unless the word of the promise is received with faith. Our answer is this: being blind and unbelieving, we did not for a long time hold fast the promise that was given us in baptism; yet the promise, because it was of God, always remained fixed, firm, and true. Even if all men should be false and faithless, still God does not cease to be true (Rom. 3:3, 4); though all were lost, Christ remains secure. We acknowledge, then, that at that time baptism profited us nothing, since the promise offered to us—without which baptism is nothing—lay neglected in us. But now, when by the grace of God we begin to repent, we accuse our own blindness and hardness of heart for having been so long ungrateful for his great goodness. We do not, however, believe that the promise itself has vanished. Rather we reflect in this way: God in baptism promises the remission of sins, and he will certainly perform what he has promised to all believers. That promise was offered to us in baptism; let us therefore embrace it in faith. As far as we were concerned, it was indeed long buried because of our unbelief; now, then, let us receive it in faith. And so, when the Lord calls the Jewish people to repentance, he gives no command about a second circumcision, even though (as we have said) they had been circumcised by a wicked and sacrilegious hand and had long lived in the same ungodliness. All he presses upon them is conversion of heart. For however much the covenant may have been broken by them, the symbol of the covenant always remained, by the Lord's appointment, firm and inviolable. Solely on the condition of repentance, then, were they restored to the covenant which God had once made with them in circumcision—though this circumcision, which they had received at the hand of a covenant-breaking priest, they had themselves, as far as lay in their power, polluted and extinguished.

18¶

But they think the weapon they wield is unanswerable when they claim that Paul re-baptized those who had been baptized with the baptism of John (Acts 19:3, 5). For if, by our own confession, the baptism of John was the same as ours, then—just as those who had been wrongly taught were re-baptized into the true faith when they learned it—so a baptism that lacked true doctrine ought to be counted as nothing, and therefore we ought to be baptized anew into the true religion with which we are only now, for the first time, instructed. Some suppose that it was a foolish imitator of John who, by an earlier baptism, had initiated these men into empty superstition. This, they think, may be inferred from the fact that they confess complete ignorance of the Holy Spirit—an ignorance in which John would never have left his disciples. But it is not likely that the Jews, even if they had not been baptized at all, would have been wholly without any knowledge of the Spirit, who is celebrated in so many passages of Scripture. Their answer, then, that they did not know whether there was a Spirit, must be understood as if they had said that they had not yet heard whether or not the gifts of the Spirit, about which Paul questioned them, were given to the disciples of Christ. I grant that John's baptism was a true baptism, one and the same with the baptism of Christ. But I deny that they were re-baptized. What, then, is meant by the words, “They were baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus”? Some take it to mean that Paul only instructed them in sound doctrine; but I would rather interpret it more simply, that the baptism of the Holy Spirit—that is, the visible gifts of the Holy Spirit—were given by the laying on of hands. These are sometimes designated by the name of baptism. So, on the day of Pentecost, the apostles are said to have remembered the Lord's words concerning the baptism of the Spirit and of fire. And Peter relates that these same words came to his mind when he saw these gifts poured out on Cornelius and his household and kindred. There is nothing in this interpretation that conflicts with what is added afterward, “When Paul had laid his hands upon them, the Holy Spirit came on them” (Acts 19:6). For Luke is not narrating two different things; he is following the form of narrative common among the Hebrews, who first give the substance and then explain it more fully. Anyone can see this from the context itself. For he says, “When they heard this, they were baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus. And when Paul laid his hands upon them, the Holy Spirit came on them.” In this last sentence the nature of that baptism is described. But if ignorance ruins an earlier baptism and requires it to be corrected by a second one, then the apostles should have been the first of all to be re-baptized, since for more than three full years after their baptism they had scarcely received even a small portion of purer doctrine. And then, considering how many acts of ignorance the mercy of God daily corrects in us, what rivers would be enough for so many repeated baptisms?

19¶

The force, dignity, usefulness, and purpose of the sacrament must now, unless I am mistaken, be clear enough. As for the external symbol, I wish the genuine institution of Christ had been kept, for it would have served to restrain the boldness of men. As though to be baptized with water, according to Christ's command, were a contemptible thing, a blessing—or rather an incantation—was devised to defile the true consecration of the water. Afterward the lighted taper and the anointing oil were added, while the breathing meant to drive out the devil was introduced.

As to the mode of baptism, it is a matter of no importance whether we baptize by immersing the one baptized entirely in the water, or only by sprinkling water upon him. According to the differences among countries, this should be left free to the churches. For the sign is set forth in both. Even so, the very word “baptize” means to immerse completely, and it is certain that the practice of thus fully immersing was observed in the ancient Church.

20¶

It is also relevant here to note that it is not proper for private individuals to take upon themselves the administration of baptism. For baptism, like the dispensing of the Supper, is part of the ministerial office. Christ did not give the command to any men or women whatsoever to baptize, but only to those whom he had appointed as apostles. And when, in administering the Supper, he ordered his disciples to do what they had seen him do—he himself having acted the part of a lawful minister—he plainly meant that in this they should follow his example. The practice that has been in use for many ages, and almost from the very beginning of the Church, of allowing laypeople to baptize in danger of death when a minister could not be present in time, cannot, it seems to me, be defended on adequate grounds. Even the early Christians who observed or tolerated this practice were not certain whether it was rightly done. Augustine expresses this doubt when he says, “Although a layman has given baptism when compelled by necessity, I do not know whether anyone can piously say that it ought to be repeated. For if it is done without any necessity compelling it, it is a usurpation of another's office; but if necessity urges it, it is either no fault or a pardonable one.” As for women, it was decreed without exception in the Council of Carthage that they were not to presume to baptize at all. But is there not a danger that someone who is sick may be deprived of the gift of regeneration if he dies without baptism? By no means. Even before our children are born, God declares that he adopts them as his own, when he promises that he will be a God to us and to our descendants after us. In this promise their salvation is included. No one will dare to offer God such an insult as to deny that he is able to make good his promise. Few perceive how much harm has been caused by the doctrine, wrongly taught, that baptism is necessary to salvation, and so they think caution all the less necessary. For when the opinion prevails that all are lost who happen not to be dipped in water, our condition becomes worse than that of God's ancient people—as if his grace were now more restrained than it was under the Law. In that case Christ would be thought to have come not to fulfill the promises but to abolish them, since the promise, which was then effective in itself to confer salvation before the eighth day, would now not be effective without the help of a sign.

21¶

What the custom was before Augustine's day we gather, first, from Tertullian, who says that a woman is not permitted to speak in the Church, nor to teach, nor to baptize, nor to make an offering, so that she may not claim for herself any office belonging to the man—not to mention that of the priest. We have a sufficient witness to the same thing in Epiphanius, when he rebukes Marcian for giving women permission to baptize. I am not unaware of the answer given by those who hold the opposite view—namely, that ordinary practice is very different from an extraordinary remedy used under the pressure of extreme necessity. But since Epiphanius declares it a mockery to let women baptize, and makes no exception, it is plain enough that the corruption is condemned as inexcusable under any pretext. And in his Third Book, when he says that this was not even permitted to the holy mother of Christ, he makes no reservation.

22¶

The example of Zipporah (Exod. 4:25) is quoted to no purpose. Because the angel of God was appeased after she took a stone and circumcised her son, some wrongly infer that her act was approved by God. If that were so, we would have to say that God was pleased with the worship which the Gentiles brought from Assyria and set up in Samaria. For by the same reasoning it would follow that the mongrel worship established in Samaria by those who came from the East was pleasing to God, seeing that afterward they were no longer troubled by wild beasts.

Baptism, Calvin shows, seals three things upon faith: the once-for-all forgiveness of sins in Christ's blood, our ingrafting into his death and resurrection for mortification and new life, and our union with him in all his benefits—a pledge so sure that its power is not undone by later sin nor by the unworthiness of the one who administered it, for it bears not a man's name but the name of the triune God. Because original corruption remains even in the baptized, the Christian life is a daily warfare, never a complacent rest. For families and churches in 2026, this is both comfort and summons. When sin accuses, do not run to some new remedy—run back to your baptism, to the perpetual washing it signifies, and press on in the long mortification of the flesh that is begun, not finished, at the font. Teach your children whose name was set upon them and what they are called to become. The water has passed; the promise stands; fight on until the Lord himself completes the work.
