Book 3 · Chapter 21 · USMC modern English

Of the Eternal Election, by Which God Has Predestinated Some to Salvation, and Others to Destruction

The divisions of this chapter are,—I. The necessity and utility of the doctrine of eternal Election explained. Excessive curiosity restrained, sec. l, 2. II. Explanation to those who through false modesty shun the doctrine of Predestination, sec. 3, 4. III. The orthodox doctrine expounded. l. The doctrine of Election and Predestination. It is useful, necessary, and most sweet. Ignorance of it impairs the glory of God, plucks up humility by the roots, begets and fosters pride. The doctrine establishes the certainty of salvation, peace of conscience, and the true origin of the Church. Answer to two classes of men: 1. The curious. 2. A sentiment of Augustine confirmed by an admonition of our Savior and a passage of Solomon. 3. An answer to a second class—viz. those who are unwilling that the doctrine should be adverted to. An objection founded on a passage of Solomon, solved by the words of Moses. 4. A second objection—viz. That this doctrine is a stumbling-block to the profane. Answer 1. The same may be said of many other heads of doctrine. 2. The truth of God will always defend itself. Third objection—viz. That this doctrine is dangerous even to believers. Answer 1. The same objection made to Augustine. 2. We must not despise anything that God has revealed. Arrogance and blasphemy of such objections. 5. Certain cavils against the doctrine. 1. Prescience regarded as the cause of predestination. Prescience and predestination explained. Not prescience, but the good pleasure of God the cause of predestination. This apparent from the gratuitous election of the posterity of Abraham and the rejection of all others. 6. Even of the posterity of Abraham some elected and others rejected by special grace. 7. The Apostle shows that the same thing has been done in regard to individuals under the Christian dispensation.

1¶

The covenant of life is not preached to everyone in the same way, and even among those who do hear it preached, it is not always received in the same way. This difference reveals the unsearchable depth of God's judgment, and without any doubt it serves his purpose of eternal election. But if it is clearly owing to nothing but God's good pleasure that salvation is freely offered to some while others are given no access to it, then great and difficult questions arise at once. These questions cannot be answered unless we hold sound views about election and predestination. To many people the subject seems troubling, because they think it utterly wrong that out of the whole mass of humanity some should be predestined to salvation and others to destruction. As we go on, it will become clear how endlessly such people entangle themselves. Yet we may add this: in the very obscurity that frightens them, we can see not only how useful this doctrine is, but also how sweet its fruits are. We will never be persuaded, as we ought to be, that our salvation flows from God's free mercy as its fountain until we come to know his eternal election. The grace of God is set in sharp relief by the contrast — namely, that he does not adopt everyone alike to the hope of salvation, but gives to some what he denies to others. It is plain how greatly ignorance of this principle steals from the glory of God and weakens true humility. But although this truth must be known, Paul declares that it cannot be known unless God sets works entirely aside and elects those whom he has predestined. His words are: "Even so then, at this present time also, there is a remnant according to the election of grace. And if it is by grace, then it is no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace. But if it is of works, then it is no longer grace; otherwise work is no longer work" (Rom. 11:6). If, in order to show that our salvation flows entirely from God's good mercy, we must be carried back to the source of election, then those who would do away with election do everything in their power to obscure what they ought instead to proclaim as loudly as possible — and they pull humility up by the very roots. Paul plainly declares that only when the salvation of a remnant is credited to free election do we come to know that God saves whomever he wills out of his pure good pleasure, and that he is not paying a debt — a debt that can never be owed. Those who shut the door, and would not let anyone get even a taste of this doctrine, are unjust to God and to people alike, for there is no other way to humble us as we ought to be humbled, or to make us feel how much we owe to him. Nor, in fact, do we have any sure ground of confidence anywhere else. We say this on the authority of Christ, who, to free us from all fear and to make us unconquerable in the midst of our many dangers, snares, and deadly conflicts, promises safety to all whom the Father has taken under his protection (John 10:26). From this we conclude that all who do not know they are God's own special people must be miserable, trembling without end. So those who overlook the three benefits we have noted, and would tear out the very foundation of our safety, give poor counsel to themselves and to all the faithful. And what is this but the very origin of the Church? As Bernard rightly teaches, the Church could not be found or recognized among the creatures, because it lies hidden — wonderfully hidden in both cases — within the lap of blessed predestination and the mass of wretched condemnation.

But before I take up the subject, I have some remarks to make to two kinds of people. Predestination is in itself a subject of considerable difficulty, and it is made far more tangled, and therefore far more dangerous, by human curiosity, which cannot be kept from wandering into forbidden paths and climbing into the clouds, determined, if it can, that none of God's secrets shall go unexplored. When we see so many — some of them in other respects not bad people — everywhere rushing into this boldness and wickedness, we must remind them of where their duty lies in this matter. First, then, when they inquire into predestination, let them remember that they are pressing into the inner chambers of the divine wisdom. Anyone who rushes in there confidently and without restraint, instead of satisfying his curiosity, will enter a maze with no way out. So it was that Eck boasted he had written about predestination just to give his youthful spirit some exercise.

2¶

"We have entered the way of faith," says Augustine; "let us hold firmly to it. It leads to the chambers of the King, in which are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge. For our Lord Jesus Christ was not begrudging his great and most chosen disciples when he said, 'I have yet many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now' (John 16:12). We must walk, advance, and grow, so that our hearts may be able to take in the things they cannot grasp now. But if the last day finds us still making progress, we will learn there what we could not learn here." If we give due weight to the truth that the word of the Lord is the only road that can lead us to investigate whatever we are permitted to hold about him, and the only light that can let us see what we ought to see about him, this will curb and restrain all presumption. For it will show us that the moment we go beyond the bounds of his word, we are off the road, in the dark, and bound to stumble, wander, and fall again and again. So let our first principle be this: to want any other knowledge of predestination than the knowledge his word sets forth is no less foolish than to walk where there is no path, or to look for light in the dark. Let us not be ashamed to be ignorant in a matter where ignorance is itself a kind of learning. Rather, let us gladly hold back from chasing knowledge that is foolish, dangerous, and even fatal to reach for. If an unbridled imagination drives us on, our proper course is to oppose it with these words: "It is not good to eat much honey; so for people to search out their own glory is not glory" (Prov. 25:27). There is good reason to dread a presumption that can only plunge us headlong into ruin.

3¶

There are others who, in trying to cure this disease, advise that predestination should hardly ever be mentioned, and tell us to avoid every question about it as we would avoid a rock at sea. Their restraint is rightly to be praised, since they think such mysteries should be handled with care; but because they keep too far inside the proper limits, they have little influence over the human mind, which does not easily allow itself to be reined in. So in order to keep to the right course in this matter, we must go back to the word of God, where we are given the correct rule for understanding. For Scripture is the school of the Holy Spirit, in which nothing useful and necessary to know has been left out, and nothing is taught except what is worth knowing. Therefore, whatever Scripture sets down on the subject of predestination, we must be careful not to keep from the faithful, or else we will seem either to rob them spitefully of God's blessing, or to accuse and mock the Spirit, as though he had published what should have been suppressed at all costs. Let the Christian, I say, open his mind and ears to all the words of God that are addressed to him, provided he does so with this restraint: that whenever the Lord closes his holy mouth, the Christian also stops inquiring. The best rule of sober humility is not only to follow wherever God leads in learning, but also, when he makes an end of teaching, to stop wishing to be wise. The danger such people fear is not so great that we should, on account of it, turn our minds away from the words of God. There is a famous saying of Solomon: "It is the glory of God to conceal a matter" (Prov. 25:2). But since both godliness and common sense tell us that this is not meant of everything, we must look for a distinction, so that under the pretense of modesty and sobriety we do not settle for an animal-like ignorance. Moses expresses this clearly in a few words: "The secret things belong to the Lord our God, but the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever" (Deut. 29:29). We see how he urges the people to study the teaching of the law in keeping with God's own decree, because God was pleased to make it known; yet at the same time he keeps them within these boundaries, for the simple reason that people are not permitted to pry into the secret things of God.

4¶

I admit that godless people lay hold of predestination so they can sneer, quibble, snap, and scoff. But if their insolence frightens us, then we will have to hide all the chief articles of the faith, because they and their kind leave hardly one of them unattacked by blasphemy. A rebellious spirit will rage just as insolently when it hears that there are three persons in the divine essence as when it hears that God, in creating man, foresaw everything that would happen to him. Nor will they hold back their jeers when they are told that little more than five thousand years have passed since the creation of the world. For they will ask, why did the power of God lie idle for so long? In short, nothing can be stated that they will not assail with mockery. To silence their blasphemies, must we say nothing about the divinity of the Son and the Spirit? Must the creation of the world be passed over without a word? Not at all! The truth of God is too powerful, here and everywhere, to fear the slanders of the ungodly, as Augustine forcefully argues in his treatise On the Gift of Perseverance. For we see that the false apostles, by defaming and accusing the true teaching of Paul, could not make him ashamed of it. There is nothing to the claim that this whole subject is dangerous to godly minds, as if it tended to destroy exhortation, shake faith, and disturb and dishearten the heart. Augustine does not hide the fact that on these very grounds he was often charged with preaching predestination too freely; but, as it was easy for him to do, he thoroughly answers the charge. Since a great variety of absurd objections are raised here, we have thought it best to deal with each of them in its proper place (see chapter 23). I only ask that this be accepted as a general rule: the secret things of God are not to be searched out, and the things he has revealed are not to be ignored — so that we are not charged on the one hand with curiosity, and on the other with ingratitude. For Augustine shrewdly observed that we can safely follow Scripture, which walks gently, like a mother adjusting her step to our weakness. But those who are so cautious and timid that they would bury all mention of predestination so it will not trouble weak minds — with what excuse, I ask, will they cloak their arrogance, when in effect they charge God with a lack of proper foresight, as if he had failed to see a danger that they imagine they are wisely guarding against? Therefore, whoever heaps reproach on the doctrine of predestination openly brings a charge against God, as though he had thoughtlessly let something slip from him that is harmful to the Church.

5¶

No one who wishes to be thought godly dares simply to deny the predestination by which God adopts some to the hope of life and assigns others to eternal death; but it is greatly quibbled over, especially by those who make foreknowledge its cause. We do, indeed, attribute both foreknowledge and predestination to God; but we say it is absurd to make predestination depend on foreknowledge (see chapter 22, section 1). When we attribute foreknowledge to God, we mean that all things always were, and forever remain, under his eye; that to his knowledge there is no past or future, but all things are present — and so present that he does not merely hold the idea of them before him, as we hold things in our memory, but truly sees and beholds them as actually before his eyes. This foreknowledge reaches over the whole circuit of the world and over all creatures. By predestination we mean the eternal decree of God, by which he settled within himself what he willed to become of each and every person. For all are not created on equal terms; rather, some are foreordained to eternal life, others to eternal damnation. And so, depending on which end each person was created for, we say that he has been predestined to life or to death. God has shown this not only in the case of individuals; he has also given a sample of it in the whole posterity of Abraham, to make it plain that the future condition of each nation lies entirely at his disposal: "When the Most High divided to the nations their inheritance, when he separated the sons of Adam, he set the boundaries of the peoples according to the number of the children of Israel. For the Lord's portion is his people; Jacob is the lot of his inheritance" (Deut. 32:8, 9). The separation is plain for all to see. In the person of Abraham, as in a withered stock, one people is specially chosen while the others are rejected; and the reason does not appear, except that Moses, to take from later generations any handle for boasting, tells them that their privilege was owing entirely to God's free love. The reason he gives for their deliverance is, "Because he loved your fathers, therefore he chose their descendants after them" (Deut. 4:37); or more plainly in another chapter, "The Lord did not set his love on you, nor choose you, because you were more in number than any people, for you were the fewest of all peoples; but because the Lord loved you" (Deut. 7:7, 8). He makes the same point again and again: "Behold, heaven and the heaven of heavens belong to the Lord your God, the earth also, with all that is in it. Yet the Lord delighted in your fathers, to love them, and he chose their descendants after them" (Deut. 10:14, 15). Again, in another passage, holiness is commanded of them, because they had been chosen to be a special people; and in still another, love is declared to be the cause of their protection (Deut. 23:5). Believers proclaim this with one voice: "He will choose our inheritance for us, the excellency of Jacob, whom he loved" (Ps. 47:4). The gifts with which God had adorned them, they all credit to free love — not only because they knew they had not earned them by any merit, but because not even the holy patriarch himself possessed any virtue that could win such outstanding honor for himself and his descendants. And to crush their pride more completely, God reproaches them for having earned nothing of the kind, since they were a rebellious and stiff-necked people (Deut. 9:6). Often, too, the prophets remind the Jews of this election by way of rebuke and shame, because they had disgracefully turned away from it. However that may be, let those who would credit God's election to human worth or merit step forward. When they see that one nation is preferred above all others, when they hear that it was no regard for their merit that moved God to show greater favor to a small and lowly people — indeed, to a wicked and rebellious people — will they bring a complaint against him for choosing to give such a display of mercy? But neither will their noisy words hinder his work, nor will their insults, like stones thrown against heaven, strike or harm his righteousness; rather, they will fall back on their own heads. To this principle of a free covenant, moreover, the Israelites are called back whenever thanks are to be given to God, or their hopes for the future are to be revived. "The Lord, he is God," says the Psalmist; "it is he who has made us, and not we ourselves; we are his people, and the sheep of his pasture" (Ps. 100:3; 95:7). The added denial, "not we ourselves," is not pointless; it teaches us that God is not only the author of all the good qualities in which people excel, but that those qualities have their source in him, since there is nothing in the people themselves deserving of such honor. In the following words, too, they are commanded to rest content with God's mere good pleasure: "O seed of Abraham his servant, you children of Jacob his chosen ones" (Ps. 105:6). And after listing God's continual mercies as the fruits of election, the conclusion is that he acted so kindly because he remembered his covenant. The song of the whole Church agrees with this teaching: "They did not gain the land by their own sword, nor did their own arm save them; but your right hand, and your arm, and the light of your countenance, because you favored them" (Ps. 44:3). It is worth noting that when the land is mentioned, it stands as a visible symbol of the secret election in which adoption is included. David elsewhere calls the people to the same gratitude: "Blessed is the nation whose God is the Lord, and the people whom he has chosen for his own inheritance" (Ps. 33:12). Samuel revives their hopes in this way: "The Lord will not forsake his people, for his great name's sake, because it has pleased the Lord to make you his people" (1 Sam. 12:22). And when David's faith is under attack, how does he arm himself for the battle? "Blessed is the man whom you choose, and cause to draw near to you, that he may dwell in your courts" (Ps. 65:4). But since God's hidden election was confirmed by a first and a second election, and by other mercies in between, Isaiah applies the terms this way: "The Lord will have mercy on Jacob, and will yet choose Israel" (Isa. 14:1). Looking forward to a future time — the gathering of the scattered people, who seemed to have been abandoned — he says it will be a sign of a firm and steady election, despite their apparent abandonment. And when it is said elsewhere, "I have chosen you, and not cast you away" (Isa. 41:9), the unbroken course of his great generosity is credited to fatherly kindness. This is stated still more plainly in Zechariah by the angel: the Lord "shall choose Jerusalem again," as though the severity of his chastisements had amounted to rejection, or the captivity had been a break in his election — which, however, remains unbroken, even though its signs do not always appear.

6¶

We must add a second stage of a more limited kind, one in which the grace of God was shown in a more special form: namely, when out of the same family of Abraham God rejected some, and by keeping others within his Church showed that he held on to them as his sons. At first Ishmael had held the same rank as his brother Isaac, because the spiritual covenant was equally sealed in him by the sign of circumcision. He is the first to be cut off; then Esau; and at last a countless multitude — almost the whole of Israel. In Isaac the seed was called. The same calling held good in the case of Jacob. God gave a similar example in the rejection of Saul. This too is celebrated in the psalm: "Moreover he refused the tent of Joseph, and did not choose the tribe of Ephraim, but chose the tribe of Judah" (Ps. 78:67, 68). Sacred history repeats this from time to time, so that the secret grace of God may be displayed more wonderfully in such a change. I grant that it was by their own fault that Ishmael, Esau, and others fell from their adoption; for the condition attached was that they should faithfully keep the covenant of God, whereas they treacherously broke it. The remarkable kindness of God lay in this: that he had been pleased to prefer them above other nations, as it is said in the psalm, "He has not dealt so with any nation; and as for his judgments, they have not known them" (Ps. 147:20). But I had good reason for saying that two stages are to be observed here. For in the election of the whole nation, God had already shown that in exercising his pure generosity he was bound by no law, but was free — so that he was by no means required to divide his grace equally, and the very unevenness of it proves that it was free. Accordingly, Malachi dwells on Israel's ingratitude: that being not only chosen out of the whole human race, but set apart in particular from a sacred household, they treacherously and impiously spurn God, their generous parent. "Was not Esau Jacob's brother?" says the Lord; "yet I loved Jacob, and I hated Esau" (Mal. 1:2, 3). For God takes it as a given that, since both were the sons of a holy father, and heirs of the covenant — in short, branches from a sacred root — the sons of Jacob were under no ordinary obligation for having been admitted to that honor. But when, by the rejection of Esau the firstborn, their ancestor, though lower in birth, was made the heir, God charges them with a double ingratitude, since they were not held back even by a double bond.

7¶

Although it is now plain enough that God, by his secret counsel, chooses whomever he wills while he rejects others, his free election has been only partly explained until we come to the case of individual persons — to whom God not only offers salvation, but assigns it in such a way that the certainty of the outcome is neither doubtful nor left hanging. On predestination, see the godly and very learned observations of Luther: volume 1, page 86 and following, and page 87 and following; volume 3, on Psalm 22:8; volume 5, on John, page 117; also his Preface to the Epistle to the Romans, and his work against Erasmus, On the Bondage of the Will, pages 429 and following, 452, and 463; and also on Psalm 139.

Calvin opens the doctrine of eternal election with a paradox the modern heart resists: that our salvation flows wholly from God's free mercy, given to some and withheld from others, and that this very truth is the deepest ground of humility, certainty, and the praise of God. In a generation that treats every soul as the captain of its own destiny, this teaching cuts against the grain — and that is precisely why your family needs it. Teach your children that they were not loved because they were lovely, but loved before they drew breath, before they could earn or forfeit anything — and watch how that uproots both pride and despair. Yet heed Calvin's warning: do not pry into the secret counsel of God where Scripture falls silent; bury your curiosity and rest in what he has revealed. The believer who knows he is the Lord's peculiar people walks unshaken through snares and mortal conflicts. Build your house on that rock, and the storms of a faithless age will not move it.
