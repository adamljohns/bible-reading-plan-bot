Book 1 · Chapter 17 · USMC modern English

Use to Be Made of the Doctrine of Providence

This chapter may be conveniently divided into two parts:—I. A general explanation is given of the doctrine of Divine Providence, in so far as conducive to the solid instruction and consolation of the godly, sect. 1, and specially sect. 2-12. First, however, those are refuted who deny that the world is governed by the secret and incomprehensible counsel of God; those also who throw the blame of all wickedness upon God, and absurdly pretend that exercises of piety are useless, sect. 2-5. Thereafter is added a holy meditation on Divine Providence, which, in the case of prosperity, is painted to the life, sect. 6-11. II. A solution of two objections from passages of Scripture, which attribute repentance to God, and speak of something like an abrogation of his decrees.

Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. Summary of the doctrine of Divine Providence. 1. It embraces the future and the past. 2. It works by means, without means, and against means. 3. Mankind, and particularly the Church, the object of special care. 4. The mode of administration usually secret, but always just. This last point more fully considered.
  2. 2. The profane denial that the world is governed by the secret counsel of God, refuted by passages of Scripture. Salutary counsel.
  3. 3. This doctrine, as to the secret counsel of God in the government of the world, gives no countenance either to the impiety of those who throw the blame of their wickedness upon God, the petulance of those who reject means, or the error of those who neglect the duties of religion.
  4. 4. As regards future events, the doctrine of Divine Providence not inconsistent with deliberation on the part of man.
  5. 5. In regard to past events, it is absurd to argue that crimes ought not to be punished, because they are in accordance with the divine decrees. 1. The wicked resist the declared will of God. 2. They are condemned by conscience. 3. The essence and guilt of the crime is in themselves, though God uses them as instruments.
  6. 6. A holy meditation on Divine Providence. 1. All events happen by the ordination of God. 2. All things contribute to the advantage of the godly. 3. The hearts of men and all their endeavours are in the hand of God. 4. Providence watches for the safety of the righteous. 5. God has a special care of his elect.
  7. 7. Meditation on Providence continued. 6. God in various ways curbs and defeats the enemies of the Church. 7. He overrules all creatures, even Satan himself, for the good of his people.
  8. 8. Meditation on Providence continued. 8. He trains the godly to patience and moderation. Examples. Joseph, Job, and David. 9. He shakes off their lethargy, and urges them to repentance.
  9. 9. Meditation continued. 10. The right use of inferior causes explained. 11. When the godly become negligent or imprudent in the discharge of duty, Providence reminds them of their fault. 12. It condemns the iniquities of the wicked. 13. It produces a right consideration of the future, rendering the servants of God prudent, diligent, and active. 14. It causes them to resign themselves to the wisdom and omnipotence of God, and, at the same time, makes them diligent in their calling.
  10. 10. Meditation continued. 15. Though human life is beset with innumerable evils, the righteous, trusting to Divine Providence, feel perfectly secure.
  11. 11. The use of the foregoing meditation.
  12. 12. The second part of the chapter, disposing of two objections. 1. That Scripture represents God as changing his purpose, or repenting, and that, therefore, his Providence is not fixed. Answer to this first objection. Proof from Scripture that God cannot repent.
  13. 13. Why repentance attributed to God.
  14. 14. Second objection, that Scripture speaks of an annulment of the divine decrees. Objection answered. Answer confirmed by an example.
1¶

Moreover, the human mind is so prone to indulge in empty subtleties that it is almost impossible for those who do not see the sound and proper use of this doctrine to avoid entangling themselves in perplexing difficulties. It will therefore be well here to point to the end that Scripture has in view when it teaches that all things are ordained by God. And it must be observed, first, that the providence of God is to be considered with reference both to the past and to the future; and, second, that in overruling all things it works at one time through means, at another without means, and at another against means. Lastly, the design of God is to show that he takes care of the whole human race, but is especially watchful in governing the Church, which he favors with a closer oversight. We must add, moreover, that although the fatherly favor and kindness of God, as well as his judicial severity, are often plain to see in the whole course of his providence, yet at times, because the causes of events are hidden, the thought is apt to arise that human affairs are whirled about by the blind impulse of Fortune; or our carnal nature inclines us to speak as if God were amusing himself by tossing men up and down like balls. It is true, indeed, that if we were willing to learn with settled and quiet minds, the outcome would at length make it plain that the counsel of God accorded with the highest reason: that his purpose was either to train his people in patience, correct their depraved affections, tame their wantonness, accustom them to self-denial, and rouse them from sluggishness; or, on the other hand, to cast down the proud, defeat the craftiness of the ungodly, and frustrate all their schemes. However much the causes may escape our notice, we must be assured that they are stored up with him, and so exclaim with David, "Many, O Lord my God, are your wonderful works which you have done, and your thoughts which are toward us: if I would declare and speak of them, they are more than can be numbered" (Psalm 40:5). For while our adversities ought always to remind us of our sins, so that the punishment may incline us to repentance, we see, moreover, how Christ declares that there is something more in the secret counsel of his Father than to chastise everyone as he deserves. For he says of the man who was born blind, "Neither has this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him" (John 9:3). Here, where calamity comes even before birth, our carnal sense murmurs as if God were unmerciful in afflicting in this way those who have not offended. But Christ declares that, if only our eyes were clear enough, we would perceive that in this spectacle the glory of his Father is brightly displayed. We must use modesty, not as though compelling God to give an account of himself, but so revering his hidden judgments as to count his will the best of all reasons. Here the words of Cicero apply admirably: Nec si ego quod tu sis sequutus, non perspicio, idcirco minus existimo te nihil sine summa ratione fecisse.

2¶

No one, therefore, will rightly and profitably ponder the providence of God except the one who remembers that he has to do with his own Maker, the Maker of the world, and who, with the humility that becomes him, shows both fear and reverence. This is why so many dogs in our day tear at this doctrine with poisoned teeth, or at least assail it with their barking, refusing to allow God more liberty than their own reason grants to themselves. With what insolence, too, are we attacked for not being content with the precepts of the Law, in which the will of God is contained, and for maintaining that the world is governed by his secret counsels! As if our doctrine were a figment of our own brain, and were not plainly declared by the Spirit and repeated in countless forms of expression! Since some feeling of shame keeps them from daring to belch out their blasphemies against heaven, they pretend to pick a quarrel with us, so that they may give freer vent to their rage. But if they refuse to admit that every event that happens in the world is governed by the incomprehensible counsel of God, let them explain to what purpose Scripture declares that "his judgments are a great deep" (Psalm 36:7). For when Moses exclaims that the will of God "is not in heaven, that you should say, Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us? Neither is it beyond the sea, that you should say, Who shall go over the sea and bring it unto us?" (Deuteronomy 30:12, 13), because it was plainly set forth in the law, it follows that there must be another, hidden will, which is compared to "a great deep." It is of this will that Paul exclaims, "O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out! For who has known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor?" (Romans 11:33, 34). It is true, indeed, that the law and the gospel contain mysteries that far surpass the measure of our understanding; but since God enlightens the minds of his people with a spirit of understanding, so that they may grasp the mysteries he has been pleased to reveal in his word, these are no longer a deep to them, but a path on which they can walk safely, a lamp to guide their feet, a light of life, a school of clear and certain truth. But the wonderful manner of his governing the world is rightly called a deep, because, while it lies hidden from us, it is to be reverently adored. Moses has beautifully expressed both truths in a few words: "The secret things," he says, "belong unto the Lord our God, but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever" (Deuteronomy 29:29). We see how he commands us not only to meditate diligently on the law, but also to look up with reverence to the secret providence of God. The Book of Job likewise, in order to keep our minds humble, contains a description of this lofty theme. The author of the book, after surveying the universe at length and discoursing magnificently on the works of God, finally adds, "Lo, these are parts of his ways: but how little a portion is heard of him?" (Job 26:14). For this reason, in another passage he distinguishes between the wisdom that dwells in God and the measure of wisdom he has assigned to man (Job 28:21, 28). After discoursing on the secrets of nature, he says that wisdom "is hid from the eyes of all living," and that "God understands the way thereof." Shortly afterward he adds that it has been made known so that it might be searched out; for "unto man he said, Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom." To this the words of Augustine refer: "Since we do not know all the things that God does concerning us in the best order, we ought, with good intention, to act according to the Law, and in some things to be acted upon according to the Law, his providence being an unchangeable Law" (Augustine, Eighty-Three Questions, question 27). Therefore, since God claims for himself the right of governing the world, a right unknown to us, let it be our law of modesty and sobriety to rest in his supreme authority, regarding his will as our only rule of justice and the most perfect cause of all things. I do not mean that absolute will the sophists prate about, when by a profane and impious divorce they separate his justice from his power, but that universal overruling providence from which nothing flows that is not right, though the reasons for it may be hidden. See Salvian, Treatise on the True Judgment and Providence of God. See also Bernard, On the Interior House, chapter 25. See also Luther, Letter to the Brethren at Antwerp.

3¶

Those who have learned this modesty will neither murmur against God for the adversities of time past, nor lay on him the blame for their own wickedness, as Homer's Agamemnon does: ̔Εγω δ᾽ ούκ ἁίτιός ειμι, ἀλλὰ Ζεὺς και μοι̑ρα. "Blame not me, but Jupiter and fate." Nor, on the other hand, will they destroy themselves in despair like the youth in Plautus, as if they were swept away by the Fates: "Unstable is the condition of affairs; instead of doing as they please, men only fulfill their fate. I will take myself to a rock, and there end my fortune with my life." Nor will they, after the example of another, use the name of God as a cloak for their crimes. For in another comedy Lyconides speaks this way: "God was the one who drove me on; I believe the gods wished it. If they had not wished it, it would not have been done, I know." They will rather inquire and learn from Scripture what pleases God, and then, under the guidance of the Spirit, strive to attain it. Ready to follow wherever God may call, they will show by their example that nothing is more useful than the knowledge of this doctrine, which perverse men undeservedly assail because it is sometimes wickedly abused. The profane make such a bluster with their foolish childishness that they almost, as the saying goes, confound heaven and earth. If the Lord has marked the moment of our death, they say, it cannot be escaped; it is useless to toil and take precautions. Therefore, when one man does not venture to travel on a road that he hears is infested with robbers; when another calls in the physician and troubles himself with medicines for the sake of his health; when a third abstains from coarser food so as not to injure a sickly constitution; and a fourth is afraid to live in a ruinous house; when all, in short, devise and, with great eagerness of mind, strike out paths by which they may attain what they desire, then either these are all vain remedies seized upon to correct the will of God, or his certain decree does not fix the limits of life and death, health and sickness, peace and war, and the other things that men, as they desire or hate them, labor by their own industry to secure or avoid. Indeed, these triflers even conclude that the prayers of the faithful must be perverse, not to say superfluous, since they ask the Lord to provide for things he has decreed from eternity. And then, attributing whatever happens to the providence of God, they excuse the man who is known to have deliberately planned it. Has an assassin slain an honest citizen? He has, they say, carried out the counsel of God. Has someone committed theft or adultery? Since the deed was foreseen and ordained by the Lord, he is the minister of his providence. Has a son waited indifferently for the death of his parent without trying any remedy? He could not oppose God, who had so determined from eternity. In this way all crimes receive the name of virtues, as being in accordance with divine ordination.

4¶

As regards future events, Solomon easily reconciles human deliberation with divine providence. For while he ridicules the folly of those who presume to undertake anything without God, as if they were not ruled by his hand, elsewhere he expresses himself this way: "A man's heart devises his way, but the Lord directs his steps" (Proverbs 16:9); meaning that the eternal decrees of God in no way prevent us from going forward, under his will, to provide for ourselves and arrange all our affairs. And the reason for this is clear. For he who has fixed the boundaries of our life has at the same time entrusted us with the care of it, provided us with the means of preserving it, warned us in advance of the dangers to which we are exposed, and supplied cautions and remedies, so that we may not be overwhelmed unawares. Now our duty is clear, namely: since the Lord has committed to us the defense of our life, to defend it; since he offers help, to use it; since he warns us of danger, not to rush on heedlessly; since he supplies remedies, not to neglect them. But it is said that a danger that is not fatal will not hurt us, and one that is fatal cannot be resisted by any precaution. But what if dangers are not fatal simply because the Lord has furnished you with the means of warding them off and overcoming them? See how far your reasoning agrees with the order of divine procedure. You infer that danger is not to be guarded against because, if it is not fatal, you will escape without precaution; whereas the Lord commands you to guard against it precisely because he wills it not to be fatal. Cicero, On Fate: "Recte Chrysippus, tam futile est medicum adhibere, quam convalescere." See Luther on Genesis 3:7, against those who abuse the doctrine of predestination in this way.

5¶

The same sort of people wrongly and thoughtlessly refer past events to bare providence. Since all contingent things whatever depend on it, they say, neither thefts, nor adulteries, nor murders are committed without the intervention of the divine will. Why, then, they ask, should the thief be punished for robbing the man whom the Lord chose to chastise with poverty? Why should the murderer be punished for slaying the man whose life the Lord had ended? If all such persons serve the will of God, why should they be punished? I deny that they serve the will of God. For we cannot say that one who is carried away by a wicked mind performs service at the command of God, when he is only following his own malignant desires. He obeys God who, being instructed in his will, hastens in the direction in which God calls him. But how are we so instructed except by his word? The will declared in his word, therefore, is what we must keep in view in acting; God requires of us nothing but what he commands. If we plan anything contrary to his precept, it is not obedience but defiance and transgression. But, they say, if he did not will it, we could not do it. I admit this. But do we act wickedly for the purpose of obeying him? This he certainly does not command. On the contrary, we rush on, not thinking of what he wishes, but so inflamed by our own passionate lust that we strive against him with set purpose. And in this way, while acting wickedly, we serve his righteous ordination, since in his boundless wisdom he knows well how to use bad instruments for good purposes. And see how absurd this way of arguing is. They will have it that crimes ought not to be punished in those who commit them, because they are not committed without the dispensation of God. I grant more: that thieves and murderers and other evildoers are instruments of divine providence, employed by the Lord himself to carry out the judgments he has resolved to inflict. But I deny that this provides any excuse for their misdeeds. For how could it? Will they involve God in the same iniquity with themselves, or will they cloak their depravity with his righteousness? They cannot clear themselves, for their own conscience condemns them; they cannot accuse God, since they perceive the whole wickedness in themselves, and nothing in him but the lawful use of their wickedness. But it is said that he works through them. And where, I ask, does the foul stench of a dead body come from, when it has been left unburied and has rotted in the heat of the sun? All see that it is raised by the rays of the sun, yet no one therefore says that the stench is in the rays. In the same way, since the matter and the guilt of wickedness belong to the wicked man, why should anyone think that God contracts any impurity by using it at his pleasure as his instrument? Have done, then, with that doglike insolence, which may indeed bark from a distance at the justice of God, but cannot reach it!

6¶

These slanders, or rather frenzied dreams, will easily be dispelled by a pure and holy meditation on divine providence, such meditation as godliness requires, so that we may draw from it the best and sweetest fruit. The Christian, then, being most fully persuaded that all things come to pass by the dispensation of God, and that nothing happens by chance, will always direct his eye to him as the principal cause of events, while at the same time paying due regard to secondary causes in their own place. Next, he will have no doubt that a special providence is awake for his preservation, and that it will not allow anything to happen that will not turn to his good and safety. But since its business is first with men and then with the other creatures, he will be assured that the providence of God reigns over both. As regards men, good as well as bad, he will acknowledge that their counsels, wishes, aims, and faculties are so under God's hand that he has full power to turn them in whatever direction he pleases, and to restrain them as often as he pleases. That a special providence watches over the safety of believers is attested by a great number of the clearest promises: Psalm 55:23; 1 Peter 5:7; Psalm 91:1; Zechariah 2:8; Isaiah 26:1; 29:15.

7¶

The servant of God, confirmed by these promises and examples, will add the passages that teach that all men are under God's power, whether to win their minds to us or to curb their wickedness and keep it from doing harm. For it is the Lord who gives us favor, not only with those who wish us well, but also in the eyes of the Egyptians (Exodus 3:21), defeating the malice of our enemies in various ways. Sometimes he deprives them of all presence of mind, so that they cannot undertake anything soundly or soberly. In this way he sends Satan to be a lie in the mouths of all the prophets in order to deceive Ahab (1 Kings 22:22); by the counsel of the young men he so infatuates Rehoboam that his folly robs him of his kingdom (1 Kings 12:10, 15). Sometimes, when he leaves them in possession of their understanding, he so fills them with terror and dismay that they can neither will nor plan the carrying out of what they had intended. Sometimes, too, after allowing them to attempt what lust and rage suggested, he interrupts them at the right moment in their course and does not let them finish what they had begun. So the counsel of Ahithophel, which would have been fatal to David, was defeated before its time (2 Samuel 17:7, 14). So, for the good and safety of his people, he overrules all the creatures, even the devil himself, who, as we see, dared not attempt anything against Job without his permission and command. This knowledge is necessarily followed by gratitude in prosperity, patience in adversity, and an incredible security for the time to come. Everything, therefore, that turns out prosperously and as he wished, the Christian will ascribe entirely to God, whether he has experienced his kindness through the agency of men or has been helped by inanimate creatures. For he will reason this way with himself: Certainly it was the Lord who inclined the minds of these people in my favor, attaching them to me so as to make them the instruments of his kindness. In an abundant harvest he will think that it is the Lord who hears the heavens, so that the heavens may hear the earth, and the earth in turn her own offspring. In other cases he will have no doubt that he owes all his prosperity to the divine blessing, and, admonished by so many circumstances, he will find it impossible to be ungrateful.

8¶

If anything adverse happens to him, he will at once raise his mind to God, whose hand is most effective in impressing on us patience and a calm moderation of mind. Had Joseph kept his thoughts fixed on the treachery of his brothers, he could never have recovered brotherly affection for them. But turning toward the Lord, he forgot the injury, and was so inclined to gentleness and mercy that he even comforts his brothers of his own accord, telling them, "Be not grieved, nor angry with yourselves, that you sold me here; for God did send me before you to preserve life." "As for you, you thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good" (Genesis 45:5; 50:20). Had Job turned his thoughts to the Chaldeans who plundered him, he would instantly have been inflamed with revenge; but recognizing the work of the Lord, he comforts himself with this most beautiful sentiment: "The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord" (Job 1:21). So when David was assailed by Shimei with stones and curses, had he fixed his eyes on the man at once, he would have urged his people to repay the injury; but perceiving that Shimei does not act without an impulse from the Lord, he calms them instead. "So let him curse," he says, "because the Lord has said unto him, Curse David." With the same bridle he elsewhere curbs the excess of his grief: "I was dumb, I opened not my mouth, because you did it" (Psalm 39:9). If there is no more effective remedy for anger and impatience, then he has certainly made no small progress who has learned so to meditate on divine providence that he can always bring his mind to this: The Lord willed it; therefore it must be borne. And this not only because it is unlawful to strive with him, but because he wills nothing that is not just and fitting. The whole comes to this. When we are unjustly attacked by men, let us pass over their malice, which could only aggravate our grief and sharpen our minds for vengeance, and remember to rise up to God, and learn to hold it for certain that whatever an enemy has wickedly done against us was permitted and sent by his righteous dispensation. Paul, in order to suppress our desire to repay injuries, wisely reminds us that we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against our spiritual enemy the devil, so that we may prepare ourselves for the contest (Ephesians 6:12). But to calm all the impulses of passion, the most useful consideration is that God arms the devil, as well as all the wicked, for the conflict, and sits as umpire, so that he may exercise our patience. But if the disasters and miseries that press upon us happen without the agency of men, let us call to mind the teaching of the Law (Deuteronomy 28:1), that all prosperity has its source in the blessing of God, and that all adversity is his curse. And let us tremble at the dreadful warning, "And if you will not be reformed by these things, but will walk contrary unto me; then will I also walk contrary unto you" (Leviticus 26:23, 24). These words condemn our sluggishness when, following our carnal sense and thinking that whatever happens comes about in some way by chance, we are neither moved by the kindness of God to worship him nor stirred by his scourge to repentance. It is for this reason that Jeremiah (Lamentations 3:38) and Amos (Amos 3:6) remonstrated bitterly with the Jews for not believing that good as well as evil came about by the command of God. To the same effect are the words in Isaiah: "I form the light and create darkness: I make peace and create evil. I the Lord do all these things" (Isaiah 45:7).

9¶

At the same time, the Christian will not overlook secondary causes. For while he regards those who benefit him as ministers of the divine goodness, he will not therefore pass them by as if their kindness deserved no gratitude; but, feeling sincerely obliged to them, he will gladly acknowledge the obligation and try, as far as he is able, to repay it. In short, in the blessings he receives he will revere and praise God as their principal author, but he will also honor men as God's ministers, and he will perceive, as is the truth, that by the will of God he is under obligation to those by whose hand God has been pleased to show him kindness. If he suffers any loss through negligence or imprudence, he will indeed believe that it was the Lord's will that it should be so, but at the same time he will impute it to himself. If someone whom it was his duty to care for, but whom he treated with neglect, is carried off by disease, then although he knows that the person had reached a limit beyond which it was impossible to pass, he will not therefore make light of his fault; rather, because he neglected to do his duty faithfully toward him, he will feel as though the man had perished through his own guilty negligence. Far less, where fraud and premeditated malice have been present, as in the case of theft or murder, will he excuse the crime under the pretext of divine providence; rather, in the same crime he will distinctly recognize the justice of God and the iniquity of man, as each is plainly shown in its own place. But it is especially in future events that he will take account of such secondary causes. If he is not left destitute of human help that he can use for his safety, he will count it a divine blessing; but he will not on that account be careless in taking measures, or slow to use the help of those he sees have the means of assisting him. Regarding all the aid the creatures can give him as hands held out to him by the Lord, he will make use of them as the lawful instruments of divine providence. And since he is uncertain what the outcome of any business he undertakes will be (except that he knows that in all things the Lord will provide for his good), he will zealously aim at what he judges best, so far as his abilities allow. In making his plans, he will not be carried away by his own impressions, but will commit and yield himself to the wisdom of God, so that under his guidance he may be led into the right path. Yet his confidence in outward help will not be such that its presence makes him feel secure, or its absence fills him with dismay, as if he were destitute. His mind will always be fixed on the providence of God alone, and he will not allow any consideration of present circumstances to draw him away from the steady contemplation of it. So Joab, while he acknowledges that the outcome of the battle is entirely in the hand of God, does not therefore become idle, but vigorously goes about what belongs to his proper calling: "Be of good courage," he says, "and let us play the men for our people, and for the cities of our God: and the Lord do that which seems him good" (2 Samuel 10:12). The same conviction, keeping us free from rashness and false confidence, will spur us on to constant prayer; and at the same time, by filling our minds with good hope, it will enable us to feel secure and to defy all the dangers that surround us.

10¶

Here we are forcibly reminded of the priceless happiness of a godly mind. Countless are the ills that beset human life and present death in as many different forms. Not to go beyond ourselves: since the body is a vessel, indeed the nurse, of a thousand diseases, a man cannot move without carrying many forms of destruction along with him. His life is, in a manner, interwoven with death. For what else can be said where heat and cold bring equal danger? Then, in whatever direction you turn, all the things around you not only may do harm, but almost openly threaten you and seem to present immediate death. Go on board a ship, and you are only a plank's breadth from death. Mount a horse, and the stumbling of a foot endangers your life. Walk along the streets, and every tile on the roofs is a source of danger. If a sharp instrument is in your own hand or in a friend's, the possible harm is plain. All the savage beasts you see are so many creatures armed for your destruction. Even within a high-walled garden, where everything ministers to delight, a serpent will sometimes lurk. Your house, constantly exposed to fire, threatens you with poverty by day and with destruction by night. Your fields, subject to hail, mildew, drought, and other injuries, threaten barrenness, and with it famine. I say nothing of poison, treachery, and robbery, some of which beset us at home while others follow us abroad. Amid these perils, must not man be most miserable, as one who, more dead than alive, draws an anxious and feeble breath with difficulty, just as if a drawn sword were constantly hanging over his neck? It may be said that these things happen seldom, or at least not always, nor to everyone, and certainly never all at once. I admit it; but since we are reminded by the example of others that they may also happen to us, and that our life is no more exempt than theirs, it is impossible not to fear and dread them as if they were about to befall us. What could you imagine more grievous than such trembling? Add to this that it is something like an insult to God to say that man, the noblest of the creatures, stands exposed to every blind and random stroke of fortune. Here, however, we were referring only to the misery man would feel if he were placed under the dominion of chance.

11¶

But once the light of divine providence has shone on the believer's soul, he is relieved and set free, not only from the extreme fear and anxiety that formerly oppressed him, but from all care. For just as he rightly shudders at the idea of chance, so he can confidently commit himself to God. This, I say, is his comfort: that his heavenly Father so holds all things under his power, so governs them at will by his nod, and so regulates them by his wisdom, that nothing takes place except by his appointment; and that, having been received into his favor and entrusted to the care of his angels, he can be harmed by neither fire, nor water, nor sword, except so far as God their master is pleased to permit. For so the Psalm sings: "Surely he shall deliver you from the snare of the fowler, and from the noisome pestilence. He shall cover you with his feathers, and under his wings shall you trust; his truth shall be your shield and buckler. You shall not be afraid for the terror by night; nor for the arrow that flies by day; nor for the pestilence that walks in darkness; nor for the destruction that wastes at noonday," and so on (Psalm 91:2-6). Hence the exultant confidence of the saints: "The Lord is on my side; I will not fear: what can man do unto me? The Lord takes my part with them that help me." "Though a host should encamp against me, my heart shall not fear." "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil" (Psalm 118:6; 27:3; 23:4).

How is it, I ask, that their confidence never fails, except that, while the world seems to revolve at random, they know that God is everywhere at work, and are assured that his work will be their safety? When they are assailed by the devil and by wicked men, they would of necessity lose heart at once, were they not strengthened by remembering and meditating on providence. But they call to mind that the devil and the whole company of the ungodly are held in on every side by the hand of God as with a bridle, so that they can neither conceive any mischief, nor plan what they have conceived, nor, however much they may have planned, move a single finger to carry it out, except so far as he permits, indeed, except so far as he commands; and that they are not only bound by his fetters, but are even compelled to do him service. When the godly think of all these things, they have abundant sources of consolation. For as it belongs to the Lord to arm the fury of such foes and to turn and direct it as he pleases, so it is his also to fix its measure and its end, so as to keep them from breaking loose and running riot as they please. Upheld by this conviction, Paul, who had said in one place that his journey was hindered by Satan (1 Thessalonians 2:18), in another resolves to undertake it if God permits (1 Corinthians 16:7). If he had only said that Satan was the obstacle, he might have seemed to give him too much power, as if he were able even to overturn the counsels of God; but now, when he makes God the disposer, on whose permission all journeys depend, he shows that however Satan may scheme, he can accomplish nothing except so far as God is pleased to give the word. For the same reason David, considering the various turns that human life undergoes as it rolls along and, in a manner, whirls around, takes refuge in this sanctuary: "My times are in your hand" (Psalm 31:15). He might have spoken of the course of life, or of time, in the singular; but by "times" he meant to express that, however unstable the condition of man may be, the changes that are constantly taking place are under divine regulation. Hence Rezin and the king of Israel, after they had joined their forces for the destruction of Israel, and seemed like torches kindled to destroy and consume the land, are called by the prophet "smoking firebrands." They could only give off a little smoke (Isaiah 7:4). So Pharaoh, when he was a terror to all by his wealth and strength and the multitude of his troops, is compared to the largest of beasts, while his troops are compared to fishes; and God declares that he will take both leader and army with his hooks and drag them wherever he pleases (Ezekiel 29:4). In a word, not to dwell on this any longer, pay attention, and you will at once perceive that ignorance of providence is the greatest of all miseries, and the knowledge of it the highest happiness.

12¶

Enough would have been said on the providence of God, so far as it serves the solid instruction and consolation of believers (for satisfying the curiosity of foolish men is something that cannot be done and ought not to be attempted), were it not that a few passages remain that seem to suggest, contrary to the view we have set forth, that the counsel of God is not firm and stable, but varies with the changes of earthly affairs. First, with reference to the providence of God, it is said that he repented of having made man (Genesis 6:6) and of having raised Saul to the kingdom (1 Samuel 15:11), and that he will repent of the evil he had resolved to inflict on his people as soon as he perceives some amendment in them (Jeremiah 18:8). Second, his decrees are sometimes said to be annulled. He had proclaimed to the Ninevites through Jonah, "Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown," but immediately upon their repentance he inclined to a more merciful sentence (Jonah 3:4-10). After he had announced his death to Hezekiah by the mouth of Isaiah, he was moved by his tears and prayers to postpone it (Isaiah 38:15; 2 Kings 20:15). Hence many argue that God has not fixed human affairs by an eternal decree, but disposes of each single year, day, and hour according to the merits of each individual, and as he judges right and just. As to repentance, we must hold that it can no more exist in God than ignorance, or error, or weakness. If no one knowingly or willingly brings himself under the necessity of repenting, we cannot attribute repentance to God without saying either that he does not know what is going to happen, or that he cannot avoid it, or that he rushes headlong and thoughtlessly into a resolution and then at once regrets it. But this is so far from the meaning of the Holy Spirit that, in the very mention of repentance, he declares that God is not moved by any feeling of regret, that he is not a man that he should repent. And it should be observed that in the same chapter both things are so joined together that a comparison of the passages admirably removes the appearance of contradiction. When it is said that God repented of having made Saul king, the term change is used figuratively. Shortly afterward it is added, "The Strength of Israel will not lie nor repent; for he is not a man, that he should repent" (1 Samuel 15:29). In these words his unchangeableness is plainly asserted without any figure. It is certain, therefore, that in the administration of human affairs the ordination of God is perpetual and above anything like repentance. That there might be no doubt of his constancy, even his enemies are compelled to bear witness to it. For Balaam, even against his will, could not help breaking out in this exclamation: "God is not a man, that he should lie; neither the son of man, that he should repent: has he said, and shall he not do it? or has he spoken, and shall he not make it good?" (Numbers 23:19).

13¶

What, then, is meant by the term repentance? The very same thing that is meant by the other forms of expression by which God is described to us in human terms. Because our weakness cannot reach his height, any description of him that we receive must be brought down to our capacity in order to be understood. And the way of bringing it down is to represent him, not as he really is, but as we conceive of him. Though he is incapable of any feeling of disturbance, he declares that he is angry with the wicked. Therefore, just as when we hear that God is angry we ought not to imagine that there is any emotion in him, but ought rather to consider that the manner of speaking is accommodated to our understanding, since God appears to us like one inflamed and provoked whenever he executes judgment, so we ought not to imagine anything more under the term repentance than a change of action, since men are accustomed to show their displeasure by such a change. Hence, because every change whatever among men is meant as a correction of what displeases, and the correction proceeds from repentance, the same term applied to God simply means that his course of action is changed. In the meantime there is no reversal of his counsel or will, no change in his affection. What he had foreseen, approved, and decreed from eternity, he carries out with unvarying consistency, however sudden the variation may seem to the eye of man.

14¶

Nor does the sacred history, when it relates that the destruction proclaimed to the Ninevites was remitted, and that the life of Hezekiah was prolonged after he had been told of his death, imply that the decrees of God were annulled. Those who think so are deluded about the meaning of threatenings, which, though they are stated simply, nevertheless contain an unspoken condition that depends on the outcome. Why did the Lord send Jonah to the Ninevites to foretell the overthrow of their city? Why did he announce his death to Hezekiah through Isaiah? He could have destroyed both them and him without a message announcing the disaster. He had something else in view than to give them a warning of death that would let them see it from a distance before it came. It was because he did not wish them destroyed, but reformed, and in this way saved from destruction. When Jonah prophesies that in forty days Nineveh will be overthrown, he does it in order to prevent the overthrow. When Hezekiah is forbidden to hope for longer life, it is so that he may obtain longer life. Who does not now see that by threatening of this kind God wished to rouse to repentance those whom he terrified, so that they might escape the judgment their sins deserved? If this is so, the very nature of the case obliges us to supply an unspoken condition in a simple pronouncement. This is confirmed even by similar cases. The Lord, rebuking King Abimelech for having taken away the wife of Abraham, uses these words: "Behold, you are but a dead man, for the woman which you have taken; for she is a man's wife." But after Abimelech's excuse, he speaks this way: "Restore the man his wife, for he is a prophet, and he shall pray for you, and you shall live; and if you restore her not, know that you shall surely die, you and all that are yours" (Genesis 20:3, 7). You see that by the first announcement he makes a deep impression on his mind, so as to make him eager to give satisfaction, and that by the second he clearly explains his will. Since the other passages may be explained in a similar way, you must not infer from them that the Lord departed in any respect from his former counsel because he recalled what he had proclaimed. When, by announcing punishment, he admonishes to repentance those whom he wishes to spare, he is preparing the way for his eternal decree, not altering it one whit either in will or in language. The only difference is that he does not state in so many syllables what is easily understood. The words of Isaiah must remain true: "The Lord of hosts has purposed, and who shall disannul it? And his hand is stretched out, and who shall turn it back?" (Isaiah 14:27).

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

The doctrine of providence is given not to satisfy our curiosity about the secret counsel of God but to instruct and console us — and Calvin presses both the comfort and the duty. It does not abolish foresight, so plan your household, lock your doors, buy the insurance, see the physician; means are God's appointed instruments, not affronts to his sovereignty. Yet when the plan fails and the loss comes anyway, the same doctrine forbids despair, for nothing reached you that did not pass first through a Father's hand. In 2026, with so much manufactured outrage and so many invitations to bitterness, let this be the family's quiet ballast: God curbs the malice of our enemies, trains the godly to patience, and bends every event toward the good of those who love him. Steadiness here is not passivity but worship — we labor diligently and we rest fully, refusing both the laziness that tempts fate and the fear that forgets the Lord.
