Regeneration by Faith — Of Repentance

1¶

Although we have already shown in some measure how faith possesses Christ and gives us the enjoyment of his benefits, the subject would still remain obscure if we did not add an explanation of the effects that flow from it. Not without good reason is the sum of the Gospel made to consist in repentance and the forgiveness of sins; and therefore, where these two heads are left out, any discussion of faith will be thin and defective, and indeed almost useless. Now, since Christ confers on us, and we obtain by faith, both free reconciliation and newness of life, reason and order require that I should here begin to treat of both. The shortest transition, however, will be from faith to repentance; for once repentance is properly understood, it will appear more clearly how a man is justified freely by faith alone, and yet how holiness of life, real holiness, as it is called, cannot be separated from the free imputation of righteousness. The French adds by way of explanation, "C'est à dire, que cela s'accorde bien, que nous ne soyons pas sans bonnes œuvres, et toutesfois que nous soyons reputés justes sans bonnes œuvres;" that is to say, that the two propositions agree perfectly well, namely, that we are not without good works, and yet that we are accounted righteous without works.

2¶

Christ and John, it is said, in their preaching first exhort the people to repentance, and then add that the kingdom of heaven is at hand (Matthew 3:2; 4:17). Such too is the message the Apostles received, and such the course Paul followed, as Luke relates (Acts 20:21). But clinging superstitiously to the order in which the syllables stand, they pay no attention to the connection of meaning in the words. For when our Lord and John begin their preaching thus, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (Matthew 3:2), do they not draw repentance as a consequence from the offer of grace and the promise of salvation? The force of the words, therefore, is the same as if it were said: Because the kingdom of heaven is at hand, for that reason repent. For Matthew, after relating that John preached in this way, says that in this was fulfilled the prophecy of the voice of one crying in the desert, "Prepare ye the way of the Lord, make straight in the desert a highway for our God" (Isaiah 40:3). But in the Prophet that voice is commanded to begin with consolation and glad tidings. Still, when we trace the origin of repentance to faith, we do not imagine some period of time in which faith is to give birth to it; we only mean to show that a man cannot seriously apply himself to repentance unless he knows that he is of God. But no man is truly persuaded that he is of God until he has embraced the favor God offers him. These things will be explained more clearly as we proceed. Some are perhaps misled by the fact that not a few are subdued by terror of conscience, or brought to obedience, before they have been instructed in a knowledge of the divine favor, indeed before they have had any taste of it (see Calvin on Acts 20:21). This is that initial fear. Latin, "Initialis timor," which the French paraphrases thus: "Et c'est une erainte comme on la voit aux petits enfans, qui ne sont point gouvernés par arison;" and it is a fear such as we see in little children, who are not governed by reason.

3¶

Certain learned men who lived long before our own day, and who desired to speak simply and sincerely according to the rule of Scripture, held that repentance consists of two parts, mortification and quickening. By mortification they mean grief of soul and terror, produced by a conviction of sin and a sense of the divine judgment. For when a man is brought to a true knowledge of sin, he begins truly to hate and loathe sin. He is also sincerely displeased with himself, confesses that he is lost and undone, and wishes he were other than he is. Moreover, when he is touched with some sense of the divine justice (for the one conviction follows immediately on the other), he lies terror-struck and stunned, humbled and cast down; he loses heart and despairs. This, which they regarded as the first part of repentance, they usually called contrition. By quickening they mean the comfort that is produced by faith, as when a man laid low by a consciousness of sin, and struck with the fear of God, afterward looks upon his goodness, and upon the mercy, grace, and salvation obtained through Christ, and so lifts up his head, begins to breathe, takes courage, and passes, as it were, from death to life. I admit that these terms, when rightly interpreted, express the power of repentance aptly enough; only I cannot agree with their use of the term quickening for the joy the soul feels after it has been calmed from agitation and fear. It more properly means that desire for a godly and holy life which springs from the new birth; as if it were said that a man dies to himself so that he may begin to live to God.

4¶

Others, seeing that the term is used in Scripture in different senses, have set down two forms of repentance, and in order to distinguish them have called the one Legal repentance, namely, that by which the sinner, stung with a sense of his sin and overwhelmed with fear of the divine anger, remains in that state of turmoil, unable to escape from it. The other they call Evangelical repentance, namely, that by which the sinner, though grievously downcast in himself, yet looks up and sees in Christ the cure for his wound, the comfort for his terror, and the haven of rest from his misery. They give Cain, Saul, and Judas, Genesis 4:13; 1 Samuel 15:30; Matthew 27:3, 4.

2 Kings 20:2; Isaiah 37:2; Jonah 3:5; 2 Samuel 24:10; 12:13, 16; Acts 2:37; Matthew 26:75; Luke 22:62

5¶

Though all this is true, yet the term repentance (so far as I can learn from Scripture) must be taken differently. For in including faith under repentance, they are at odds with what Paul says in the Acts about "testifying both to the Jews and also to the Greeks, repentance toward God, and faith toward our Lord Jesus Christ" (Acts 20:21). Here he names faith and repentance as two different things. What then? Can true repentance exist without faith? By no means. But although they cannot be separated, they ought to be distinguished. As there is no faith without hope, and yet faith and hope are different, so repentance and faith, though constantly bound together, are only to be joined, not confused. I am not unaware that the term repentance takes in the whole work of turning to God, of which faith is not the least important part; but in what sense this is done will be perfectly plain once its nature and power have been explained. The term repentance is derived in Hebrew from conversion, or turning back, and in Greek from a change of mind and purpose; and the thing meant suits both derivations well, for in substance it is this: that withdrawing from ourselves we turn to God, and laying aside the old mind, put on a new one. Therefore it seems to me that repentance may fittingly be defined thus: A real conversion of our life to God, proceeding from a sincere and serious fear of God, and consisting in the mortification of our flesh and of the old man, and in the quickening of the Spirit. In this sense are to be understood all those addresses in which first the prophets, and afterward the apostles, exhorted the people of their time to repentance. The great aim of their labor was to fill them with shame for their sins and dread of the divine judgment, so that they might fall down and humble themselves before him whom they had offended, and, with true repentance, return to the right path. Accordingly, they use the expressions turning, or returning, to the Lord, repenting, and doing repentance interchangeably in the same sense. Matthew 3:2; 1 Samuel 7:8; Luke 3:8; Romans 6:4; Acts 26:20.

6¶

But before going further, it will be proper to explain more clearly the definition we have adopted. There are, then, three things chiefly to be considered in it. First, in the conversion of the life to God, we require a transformation not only in outward works but in the soul itself, which only after it has put off its old habits is able to bring forth fruits that agree with its renewal. The prophet, intending to express this, commands those whom he calls to repentance to make themselves "a new heart and a new spirit" (Ezekiel 18:31). Hence Moses, on several occasions, when he would show how the Israelites were to repent and turn to the Lord, tells them that it must be done with the whole heart and the whole soul (a form of expression that recurs often in the prophets), and by calling it the circumcision of the heart he points to the inward affections. But no passage is better fitted to teach us the true nature of repentance than this: "If thou wilt return, O Israel, saith the Lord, return unto me." "Break up your fallow ground, and sow not among thorns. Circumcise yourselves to the Lord, and take away the foreskins of your heart" (Jeremiah 4:1-4). See how he declares to them that it will be of no use to begin the pursuit of righteousness unless ungodliness has first been rooted out of their inmost heart. And to make the deeper impression, he reminds them that they have to do with God and can gain nothing by deceit, because he hates a double heart. For this reason Isaiah ridicules the perverse attempts of hypocrites, who zealously aimed at an outward repentance by observing ceremonies, but meanwhile did not care "to loose the bands of wickedness, to undo the heavy burdens, and to let the oppressed go free" (Isaiah 58:6). In these words he shows admirably in what the acts of unfeigned repentance consist.

7¶

The second part of our definition is that repentance proceeds from a sincere fear of God. Before the mind of the sinner can be inclined to repentance, he must be awakened by the thought of divine judgment; but once the thought that God will one day ascend his judgment seat to take account of all words and deeds has taken hold of his mind, it will not let him rest or have one moment's peace, but will continually urge him to adopt a different plan of life, so that he may be able to stand securely at that judgment seat. Hence Scripture, when it exhorts to repentance, often brings in the subject of judgment, as in Jeremiah: "Lest my fury come forth like fire, and burn that none can quench it, because of the evil of your doings" (Jeremiah 4:4). Paul, in his address to the Athenians, says, "The times of this ignorance God winked at; but now commandeth all men every where to repent: because he has appointed a day in the which he will judge the world in righteousness" (Acts 17:30, 31). The same thing is repeated in several other passages. Sometimes God is declared to be a judge from the punishments already inflicted, so that sinners are led to reflect that worse awaits them if they do not quickly repent. There is an example of this in the 29th chapter of Deuteronomy. As repentance begins with dread and hatred of sin, the Apostle sets down godly sorrow as one of its causes (2 Corinthians 7:10). By godly sorrow he means that we not only tremble at the punishment, but hate and abhor the sin, because we know that it displeases God. It is no wonder that this should be so, for unless we are stung to the quick, the sluggishness of our carnal nature cannot be corrected; indeed, no degree of sharpness would be enough for our dullness and sloth if God did not lift the rod and strike deeper. There is, moreover, a rebellious spirit which must be broken as with hammers. The stern threats God uses are wrung from him by our depraved dispositions. For while we are asleep it would be useless to coax us with soothing measures. Passages to this effect are found everywhere, and I need not quote them. But there is another reason why the fear of God lies at the root of repentance, namely, that though a man's life were furnished with every kind of virtue, still, if those virtues have no reference to God, however much they may be praised in the world, they are mere abomination in heaven; for the principal part of righteousness is to render to God the service and honor of which he is impiously robbed whenever it is not our express purpose to submit to his authority.

8¶

We must now explain the third part of the definition, and show what is meant when we say that repentance consists of two parts, namely, the mortification of the flesh and the quickening of the Spirit. The prophets, accommodating themselves to a carnal people, express this in simple and homely terms, but clearly, when they say, "Depart from evil, and do good" (Psalm 34:14); "Wash you, make you clean, put away the evil of your doings from before mine eyes; cease to do evil; learn to do well; seek judgment; relieve the oppressed," and so on (Isaiah 1:16, 17). In calling us away from wickedness they demand the entire destruction of the flesh, which is full of perversity and malice. It is a most difficult and arduous achievement to renounce ourselves and lay aside our natural disposition. For the flesh must not be thought destroyed unless everything we have of our own is abolished. But seeing that all the desires of the flesh are enmity against God (Romans 8:7), the first step toward obedience to his law is the renouncing of our own nature. Renewal is afterward shown by the fruits it produces, namely, justice, judgment, and mercy. Since it would not be enough to perform such acts outwardly unless the mind and heart were first endowed with a sense of justice, judgment, and mercy, this is done when the Holy Spirit, instilling his holiness into our souls, so inspires them with new thoughts and affections that they may rightly be regarded as new. And indeed, since we are by nature turned away from God, unless self-denial comes first we will never move toward what is right. Hence we are so often commanded to put off the old man, to renounce the world and the flesh, to forsake our lusts, and to be renewed in the spirit of our mind. Moreover, the very name mortification reminds us how difficult it is to forget our former nature; for we infer from it that we cannot be trained in the fear of God, and learn the first principles of godliness, unless we are violently struck with the sword of the Spirit and brought to nothing, as if God were declaring that to be counted among his sons there must be a destruction of our ordinary nature.

9¶

Both of these we obtain by union with Christ. For if we have true fellowship in his death, our old man is crucified by its power, and the body of sin becomes dead, so that the corruption of our original nature is never again in full vigor (Romans 6:5, 6). If we are partakers in his resurrection, we are raised up by it to newness of life, which conforms us to the righteousness of God. In one word, then, by repentance I understand regeneration. French, "une regeneration spirituelle;" a spiritual regeneration.

2 Corinthians 3:18; Ephesians 4:23, 24; Colossians 3:10; 2 Corinthians 4:16.

10¶

By regeneration the children of God are set free from the bondage of sin, yet not as if they had already obtained full possession of freedom and no longer felt any trouble from the flesh. Material for an unceasing contest remains, so that they may be exercised, and not only exercised, but may better understand their weakness. All writers of sound judgment agree on this, that in the regenerate man there is still a spring of evil which continually sends forth desires that allure and provoke him to sin. They also acknowledge that the saints are still so subject to the disease of concupiscence that, though they resist it, they cannot avoid being every now and then prompted and incited to lust, avarice, ambition, or other vices. There is no need to spend much time examining the opinions of ancient writers. Augustine alone may suffice, since he has collected all their opinions with great care and faithfulness. See Augustine, To Boniface, book 4, and Against Julian, books 1 and 2. See also Sermon 6, On the Words of the Apostle. See also Calvin, Against Pighius, and Calvin, On the Council of Trent.

11¶

When it is said that God cleanses his Church so that it may be "holy and without blemish" (Ephesians 5:26, 27), that he promises this cleansing by means of baptism, and performs it in his elect, I understand the reference to be to the guilt rather than to the matter of sin. In regenerating his people God does indeed accomplish this much for them: he destroys the dominion of sin. Latin, "Reatus," the imputation of sin.

12¶

If anyone thinks it absurd to condemn in this way all the desires by which man is naturally affected, seeing that they were implanted by God, the author of nature, we answer that we by no means condemn those appetites which God so implanted in the mind of man at his first creation that they cannot be rooted out without destroying human nature itself, but only the violent and lawless movements that war against the order of God. But since, as a result of the corruption of nature, all our faculties are so vitiated and corrupted that a constant disorder and excess appears in all our actions, and since the appetites cannot be separated from this excess, we maintain that they are therefore vicious. Or, to put the substance in fewer words, we hold that all human desires are evil, and we charge them with sin, not insofar as they are natural, but because they are inordinate; and inordinate because nothing pure and upright can proceed from a corrupt and polluted nature. Nor does Augustine depart from this doctrine so much in reality as in appearance. From an excessive dread of the odious charge with which the Pelagians attacked him, he sometimes refrains from using the term sin in this sense; but when he says (To Boniface) that while the law of sin remains in the saints, the guilt alone is taken away, he shows clearly enough that his view is not very different from ours.

13¶

We will bring forward some other passages to make his opinion more apparent. In his second book against Julian he says, "This law of sin is both remitted in spiritual regeneration and remains in the mortal flesh: remitted, because the guilt is forgiven in the sacrament by which believers are regenerated; and yet it remains, inasmuch as it produces desires against which believers fight." Again, "Therefore the law of sin (which was in the members of this great Apostle also) is forgiven in baptism, not ended." Again, "The law of sin, the guilt of which, though it remains, is forgiven in baptism, Ambrose called iniquity, for it is iniquitous for the flesh to lust against the Spirit." Again, "Sin is dead in the guilt by which it bound us; and until it is cured by the perfection of burial, though dead, it rebels." In the fifth book he speaks still more plainly: "As blindness of heart is the sin by which God is not believed; and the punishment of sin, by which a proud heart is justly punished; and the cause of sin, when through the error of a blinded heart any evil is committed: so the lust of the flesh, against which the good Spirit wars, is also sin, because it is disobedient to the authority of the mind; and the punishment of sin, because it is the recompense rendered for disobedience; and the cause of sin, consenting by revolt or springing up through contamination." Here he calls it sin without any ambiguity, because, the Pelagian heresy having now been refuted and sound doctrine confirmed, he was less afraid of slander. So also in his forty-first Homily on John, where he speaks his own mind without controversy, he says, "If with the flesh you serve the law of sin, do what the Apostle himself says, 'Let not sin, therefore, reign in your mortal body, that ye should obey it in the lusts thereof' (Romans 6:12). He does not say, Let it not be, but Let it not reign. As long as you live there must be sin in your members; but at least let its dominion be destroyed; do not do what it commands." Those who maintain that concupiscence is not sin are accustomed to rest on the passage of James, "Then, when lust has conceived, it bringeth forth sin" (James 1:15). But this is easily refuted: for unless we understand him to be speaking only of wicked works or actual sins, not even a wicked inclination will be counted sin. But from the fact that he calls crimes and wicked deeds the fruits of lust, and also gives them the name of sins, it does not follow that lust itself is not an evil, and in the sight of God deserving of condemnation.

14¶

Some Anabaptists in the present age mistake some indescribable kind of frenzied excess for the regeneration of the Spirit, holding that the children of God are restored to a state of innocence and therefore need give themselves no concern about curbing the lust of the flesh; that they have the Spirit for their guide, and under his direction never err. See Calvin, Against the Libertines, chapter 18.

15¶

The Apostle, in his description of repentance (2 Corinthians 7:2), lists seven causes, effects, or parts belonging to it, and that on the best grounds. These are carefulness, excuse, indignation, fear, desire, zeal, and revenge. It should not cause surprise that I do not venture to decide whether they ought to be regarded as causes or effects: both views can be maintained. They may also be called affections joined with repentance; but since Paul's meaning can be ascertained without entering into any of these questions, we will be content with a simple explanation. He says, then, that godly sorrow produces carefulness. He who is truly displeased with himself for sinning against his God is at the same time stirred to care and attention, so that he may completely disentangle himself from the chains of the devil and keep a better guard against his snares, and so not afterward lose the guidance of the Holy Spirit or be overcome by careless security. Next comes excuse, which in this place means not a defense, in which the sinner, to escape the judgment of God, either denies his fault or makes it lighter, but an apology that relies more on intercession than on the goodness of the cause. So children who are not wholly abandoned, while they acknowledge and confess their errors, still plead for pardon; and to make room for this, they show by every means in their power that they have by no means cast off the reverence they owe their parents. In short, they try by excuse not to prove themselves righteous and innocent, but only to obtain pardon. Next follows indignation, under which the sinner inwardly murmurs, remonstrates, and is offended with himself when he recognizes his perversity and ingratitude toward God. By the term fear is meant that trembling which takes hold of our minds whenever we consider both what we have deserved and the fearful severity of the divine anger against sinners. Accordingly, the extreme disquiet which we must necessarily feel both trains us to humility and makes us more cautious for the future. But if the carefulness or anxiety which he mentioned first is the result of fear, the connection between the two becomes plain. Desire seems to me to be used as equivalent to diligence in duty and eagerness in service, to which the sense of our misdeeds ought to be a powerful spur. To this also belongs zeal, which immediately follows; for it signifies the ardor with which we are inflamed when goads like these are applied to us: "What have I done? Into what abyss would I have fallen if the mercy of God had not come to my aid?" The last of all is revenge; for the stricter we are with ourselves, and the more severe the censure we pass on our sins, the more ground we have to hope for the divine favor and mercy. And certainly when the soul is overwhelmed with dread of the divine judgment, it cannot help acting the part of an avenger in inflicting punishment on itself. Godly men no doubt feel that there is punishment in the shame, confusion, groans, displeasure with themselves, and other feelings produced by a serious review of their sins. Let us remember, however, that moderation must be used, so that we are not overwhelmed with sadness, since there is nothing to which trembling consciences are more prone than to rush into despair. This, too, is one of Satan's devices. Those whom he sees overwhelmed with fear in this way he plunges deeper and deeper into the abyss of sorrow, so that they may never rise again. It is true that the fear which ends in humility without giving up the hope of pardon cannot be excessive. And yet we must always beware, according to the apostolic warning, of giving way to extreme dread, since this tends to make us flee from God while he is calling us to himself by repentance. Therefore the advice of Bernard is good: "Grief for sins is necessary, but it must not be perpetual. My advice is that you turn back at times from sorrow and the anxious remembrance of your ways, and escape to the plain, to a calm review of the divine mercies. Let us mingle honey with wormwood, so that the wholesome bitterness may give health when we drink it tempered with a mixture of sweetness. While you think humbly of yourselves, think also of the goodness of the Lord" (Bernard, On the Song of Songs, Sermon 11).

16¶

We can now understand what the fruits of repentance are, namely, the duties of godliness toward God and of love toward men, together with holiness and purity of life in general. In short, the more earnestly a man strives to conform his life to the standard of the divine law, the surer the signs he gives of his repentance. Accordingly, the Spirit, in exhorting us to repentance, at one time sets before us each separate precept of the law, at another the duties of the second table; although there are also passages in which, after condemning impurity at its fountain in the heart, he afterward comes down to the outward marks by which repentance is proved to be sincere. A portrait of this I will shortly set before the reader's eyes when I come to describe the Christian life (below, chapter 6). I will not here collect the passages from the prophets in which they ridicule the trifling observances of those who labor to appease God with ceremonies, and show that they are mere mockery; or those in which they show that outward integrity of conduct is not the chief part of repentance, seeing that God looks at the heart. Anyone moderately familiar with Scripture will understand by himself, without being reminded by others, that when he has to do with God, nothing is gained unless he begins with the inward affections of the heart. There is a passage of Joel which will do much to help in understanding the others: "Rend your heart, and not your garments" (Joel 2:13). Both are also briefly expressed by James in these words: "Cleanse your hands, ye sinners; and purify your hearts, ye double-minded" (James 4:8). Here, indeed, the secondary thing is set down first; but the source and principle is pointed out afterward, namely, that hidden defilements must be wiped away and an altar raised to God in the very heart. There are, moreover, certain outward exercises which we use in private as remedies to humble us and tame our flesh, and in public to testify to our repentance. These have their origin in that revenge of which Paul speaks (2 Corinthians 7:2); for when the mind is distressed, it naturally expresses itself in sackcloth, groans, and tears, avoids ornament and every kind of display, and gives up all delights. Then he who feels how great an evil the rebellion of the flesh is tries every means of curbing it. Besides, he who rightly considers how grievous a thing it is to have offended the justice of God cannot rest until, in his humility, he has given glory to God. Ancient writers often mention such exercises when they speak of the fruits of repentance. But although they by no means place the power of repentance in them, my readers must pardon me for saying what I think: they certainly seem to insist on them more than is right. Anyone who weighs the matter with judgment will, I trust, agree with me that they went too far in two ways. First, by urging so strongly and praising so extravagantly that bodily discipline, they did succeed in making the people embrace it with greater zeal; but in a manner they obscured what they ought to have regarded as of far more serious importance. Secondly, the penalties they imposed were considerably more rigorous than the gentleness of the Church requires, as will be shown elsewhere.

17¶

But since there are some who, from the frequent mention of sackcloth, fasting, and tears, especially in Joel (2:12), think that these make up the principal part of repentance, we must dispel their delusion. In that passage the proper part of repentance is described by the words, "turn ye even to me with your whole heart"; "rend your heart, and not your garments." The "fastings," "weeping," and "mourning" are brought in not as invariable or necessary effects, but as special circumstances. French, "Circonstances qui convenoyent specialement alore;" circumstances which were then especially suitable.

French, "Fust la coustume de ce temps-la, et ne nous appartienne aujourduhui de rien;" was the custom of that time, and we today have nothing to do with it.

The French adds, "Soit de guerre, de famine, ou de pestilence;" whether of war, famine, or pestilence.

Latin, "Calamitosis temporibus peculiariter destinari." French, "Convient particulierement a ceux qui veulent testifier quils se recognoissant avoir merité l'ire de Dieu, et neantmoins requierent pardon de sa clemence;" is particularly suitable for those who acknowledge that they have deserved the wrath of God, and yet seek pardon from his mercy.

18¶

This much, however, I will add: when the name repentance is applied to the outward profession, it is used improperly, and not in its true sense as I have explained it. For that is not so much a turning to God as the confession of a fault, joined with a plea that the sentence and punishment be turned aside. Thus to repent in sackcloth and ashes (Matthew 11:21; Luke 10:13) is simply to testify our displeasure with ourselves when God is angry with us for having grievously offended him. It is, indeed, a kind of public confession by which, condemning ourselves before angels and the world, we anticipate the judgment of God. For Paul, rebuking the sluggishness of those who indulge themselves in their sins, says, "If we would judge ourselves, we should not be judged" (1 Corinthians 11:31). It is not always necessary, however, to inform others openly and make them witnesses of our repentance; but to confess privately to God is a part of true repentance which cannot be left out. Nothing would be more unfitting than for God to pardon the sins in which we are flattering ourselves, and which we hypocritically cloak so that he may not bring them to light. We must not only confess the sins we commit daily; more grievous falls ought to carry us further, and bring to our remembrance things that seemed long ago buried. David sets an example of this before us in his own person (Psalm 51). Filled with shame for a recent crime, he examines himself, going back to the womb, and acknowledging that even then he was corrupted and defiled. He does this not to make his fault lighter, as many hide themselves in the crowd and grasp at impunity by involving others along with them. David does very differently: he frankly makes it an aggravation of his sin that, being corrupted from his earliest infancy, he did not cease to add iniquity to iniquity. In another passage, too, he surveys his past life and implores God to pardon the errors of his youth (Psalm 25:7). And indeed, we will not prove that we have thoroughly shaken off our dullness until, groaning under the burden and lamenting our sad condition, we seek relief from God. It is, moreover, to be observed that the repentance which we are commanded to practice constantly differs from that which raises, as it were, from death those who had fallen more shamefully, or given themselves up to sin without restraint, or by some kind of open revolt had thrown off the authority of God. For Scripture, in exhorting to repentance, often speaks of it as a passage from death to life, and when it relates that a people had repented, it means that they had abandoned idolatry and other forms of gross wickedness. For this reason Paul pronounces woe on sinners "who have not repented of the uncleanness, and fornication, and lasciviousness which they have committed" (2 Corinthians 12:21). This distinction ought to be carefully observed, so that when we hear of a few individuals having been summoned to repent, we do not sink into careless security, as if we had nothing to do with the mortification of the flesh; whereas, because of the depraved desires that are always enticing us, and the iniquities that keep springing from them, it must claim our unceasing care. The special repentance required of those whom the devil has entangled in deadly snares and drawn away from the fear of God does not abolish that ordinary repentance which the corruption of our nature obliges us to practice throughout the whole course of our lives.

19¶

Moreover, if it is true, and nothing can be more certain, that a complete summary of the Gospel is contained under these two heads, namely, repentance and the forgiveness of sins, do we not see that the Lord justifies his people freely, and at the same time renews them to true holiness by the sanctification of his Spirit? John, the messenger sent before the face of Christ to prepare his ways, proclaimed, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (Matthew 11:10; 3:2). By inviting them to repentance, he urged them to acknowledge that they were sinners, and in every respect condemned before God, so that they might thus be moved earnestly to seek the mortification of the flesh and a new birth in the Spirit. By announcing the kingdom of God he called for faith, since by the kingdom of God which he declared to be at hand he meant forgiveness of sins, salvation, life, and every other blessing we obtain in Christ. Therefore we read in the other Evangelists, "John did baptize in the wilderness, and preach the baptism of repentance for the remission of sins" (Mark 1:4; Luke 3:3). What does this mean, but that, weary and weighed down with the burden of sin, they should turn to the Lord and take hold of the hope of forgiveness and salvation? The French adds, "pource qu'il lui est propre, et comme naturel, de sauver ce que est perdu;" because it is proper to him, and as it were natural, to save what is lost.

20¶

Moreover, as hatred of sin, which is the beginning of repentance, first gives us access to the knowledge of Christ, who reveals himself to none but miserable and afflicted sinners, groaning, laboring, burdened, hungry, and thirsty, wasting away with grief and wretchedness, so, if we would stand in Christ, we must aim at repentance, practice it throughout our whole lives, and continue in it to the end. Christ came to call sinners, but to call them to repentance. He was sent to bless the unworthy, but by "turning away every one" "from his iniquities." Scripture is full of similar passages. Hence, when God offers forgiveness of sins, he usually requires repentance in return, indicating that his mercy should move men to repent. "Keep ye judgment," says he, "and do justice: for my salvation is near to come." Again, "The Redeemer shall come to Zion, and unto them that turn from transgression in Jacob." Again, "Seek ye the Lord while he may be found, call ye upon him while he is near. Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts, and let him return unto the Lord, and he will have mercy upon him." "Repent ye, therefore, and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out." Isaiah 56:1; 59:20; 55:6, 7; Acts 2:38; 3:19.

This is found in various passages of his work, and often in the Phaedo.

21¶

Moreover, that repentance is a special gift of God is, I trust, too well understood from the doctrine above to require any lengthy discussion. Hence the Church. French, "L'Eglise primitive du temps des Apostres;" the early Church of the Apostles' time.

23¶

If you attend carefully, you will see that the Apostle is speaking not of one or two particular falls, but of the universal revolt by which the reprobate renounce salvation. It is no wonder that God should be implacable toward those whom John, in his Epistle, declares were not of the elect, from whom they went out (1 John 2:19). For he is directing his words against those who imagined that they could return to the Christian religion even though they had once revolted from it. To strip them of this false and pernicious opinion, he says, what is most true, that those who had once knowingly and willingly cast off fellowship with Christ had no way of returning to it. It is not, however, cast off by those who merely transgress the word of the Lord through the looseness of their lives, but by those who openly reject his whole doctrine. There is a false reasoning in the expressions casting off and sinning. Casting off, as the Novatians interpret it, is when anyone, though taught by the Law of the Lord not to steal or commit adultery, does not refrain from theft or adultery. On the contrary, I hold that there is an implied antithesis, in which all the things contrary to those that had been said must be understood as repeated, so that what is expressed is not some particular vice, but a universal turning away from God, and (so to speak) the apostasy of the whole man. Therefore, when he speaks of those falling away "who were once enlightened, and have tasted of the heavenly gift, and were made partakers of the Holy Ghost, and have tasted of the good word of God, and the powers of the world to come," we must understand him to refer to those who, with deliberate ungodliness, have quenched the light of the Spirit, tasted the heavenly word and spurned it, estranged themselves from the sanctification of the Spirit, and trampled underfoot the word of God and the powers of a world to come. To show more clearly that this was the kind of ungodliness he intended, he afterward expressly adds the term willfully. For when he says, "If we sin willfully, after that we have received the knowledge of the truth, there remaineth no more sacrifice for sins," he does not deny that Christ is a perpetual victim to atone for the transgressions of the saints (this the whole Epistle plainly proclaims in explaining the priesthood of Christ), but he says that no other sacrifice remains once this one is abandoned. And it is abandoned when the truth of the Gospel is openly renounced.

24¶

To some it seems harsh, and at odds with the divine mercy, to deny forgiveness utterly to any who flee to it. This is easily answered. It is not said that pardon will be refused if they turn to the Lord; rather, it is altogether denied that they can turn to repentance, since for their ingratitude they are struck by the just judgment of God with eternal blindness. There is nothing contrary to this in the use that is afterward made of the example of Esau, who tried in vain, with crying and tears, to recover his lost birthright; nor in the warning of the Prophet, "They cried, and I would not hear." Such expressions do not denote true conversion or calling upon God, but that anxiety by which the wicked, when in calamity, are forced to see what they formerly disregarded in their security, namely, that nothing can help them but the assistance of the Lord. This, however, they do not so much implore as lament having lost. Hence all that the Prophet means by crying, and the apostle by tears, is the dreadful torment that stings and tortures the wicked in despair. It is important to observe this carefully; for otherwise God would be inconsistent with himself when he proclaims through the Prophet that "If the wicked will turn from all his sins that he has committed," "he shall surely live, he shall not die" (Ezekiel 18:21, 22). And (as I have already said) it is certain that the mind of man cannot be changed for the better except by his preventing grace. The promise to those who call upon him will never fail; but the names of conversion and prayer are improperly given to that blind torment by which the reprobate are torn when they see that they must seek God if they would find a remedy for their calamities, and yet shrink from approaching him.

25¶

But since the Apostle declares that God is not appeased by feigned repentance, it is asked how Ahab obtained pardon and turned aside the punishment pronounced against him (1 Kings 21:28, 29), seeing that, as it appears, he was only struck with sudden alarm and afterward continued in his former way of life. He did indeed clothe himself in sackcloth, cover himself with ashes, and lie on the ground, and (as the testimony given of him bears) humbled himself before God. It was a small matter to tear his garments while his heart remained obstinate and swollen with wickedness, and yet we see that God was inclined to mercy. I answer that though hypocrites are sometimes spared in this way for a time, the wrath of God still rests upon them, and that they are spared not so much for their own sake as for a public example. For what did Ahab gain by the lightening of his punishment, except that he did not suffer it while alive on the earth? The curse of God, though hidden, was fixed on his house, and he himself went to eternal destruction. We may see the same thing in Esau (Genesis 27:38, 39). For though he met with a refusal, a temporal blessing was granted to his tears. But since, according to the declaration of God, the spiritual inheritance could be possessed by only one of the brothers, when Jacob was chosen instead of Esau, that event shut him out from the divine mercy; yet there was still given to him, as a man of earthbound nature, this consolation, that he should be filled with the fatness of the earth and the dew of heaven. And this, as I said a moment ago, should be regarded as done for the example of others, so that we may learn to apply our minds, and exert ourselves with greater eagerness, in the way of sincere repentance; for there cannot be the least doubt that God will be ready to pardon those who turn to him truly and from the heart, seeing that his mercy extends even to the unworthy, though they bear the marks of his displeasure. In this way we are also taught how dreadful is the judgment that awaits all the rebellious who, with brazen face and iron heart, make sport of despising and disregarding the divine threatenings. In this way God often stretched out his hand to deliver the Israelites from their calamities, though their cries were feigned and their minds double and faithless, as he himself complains in the Psalms that they immediately returned to their former course (Psalm 78:36, 37). But he intended in this way, by kindness and forbearance, either to bring them to true repentance or to leave them without excuse. And yet by remitting the punishment for a time, he does not place himself under any perpetual obligation. Rather, he sometimes rises up with greater severity against hypocrites and doubles their punishment, so that it may appear how much hypocrisy displeases him. But, as I have observed, he gives some examples of his readiness to pardon, so that the godly may be spurred on by them to amend their lives, and the pride of those who insolently kick against the pricks may be more severely condemned.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Repentance, Calvin shows, is no single tearful hour but the whole turning of the life Godward — a real conversion springing from the fear of God, consisting in the daily mortification of the flesh and the quickening of the Spirit, continued to our last breath. It flows from faith and never abolishes the free imputation of righteousness, yet real holiness is inseparable from it. For the believer in 2026 this is a sober word against the cheap repentance of a culture that confesses lightly and amends nothing, and against the despair Satan loves to deepen in tender consciences. Mingle honey with the wormwood, as Bernard counseled — grieve over sin truly, but turn back at times to a calm review of God's mercy lest you be swallowed in sorrow. In the home, model the long obedience: a heart broken and a heart hoping, fallow ground broken up again each morning. We do not graduate from repentance; we walk in it until we walk through death into life.
