Book 4 · Chapter 3 · USMC modern English

Of the Teachers and Ministers of the Church — Their Election and Office

The three heads of this chapter are,—I. A few preliminary remarks on Church order, on the end, utility, necessity, and dignity of the Christian ministry, sec. 1-3. II. A separate consideration of the persons performing Ecclesiastical functions, sec. 4-10. III. Of the Ordination or calling of the ministers of the Church, sec. 10-16. 1. Summary of the chapter. Reasons why God, in governing the Church, uses the ministry of men. 1. To declare his condescension. 2. To train us to humility and obedience. 3. To bind us to each other in mutual charity. These reasons confirmed by Scripture. 2. This ministry of men most useful to the whole Church. Its advantages enumerated. 3. The honourable terms in which the ministry is spoken of. Its necessity established by numerous examples. 4. Second part of the chapter, treating of Ecclesiastical office-bearers in particular. Some of them, as Apostles, Prophets, and Evangelists, temporary. Others, as Pastors and Teachers, perpetual and indispensable. 5. Considering the office of Evangelist and Apostle as one, we have Pastors corresponding with Apostles, and Teachers with Prophets. Why the name of Apostles specially conferred on the twelve. 6. As to the Apostles so also to Pastors the preaching of the Word and the administration of the sacraments has been committed. How the Word should be preached. 7. Regularly every Pastor should have a separate church assigned to him. This, however, admits of modification, when duly and regularly made by public authority. 8. Bishops, Presbyters, Pastors, and Ministers, are used by the Apostles as one and the same. Some functions, as being temporary, are omitted. Two—namely, those of Elders and Deacons—as pertaining to the ministry of the Word, are retained. 9. Distinction between Deacons. Some employed in distributing alms, others in taking care of the poor. 10. Third part of the chapter, treating of the Ordination or calling of the ministers of the Church. 11. A twofold calling—viz. an external and an internal. Mode in which both are to be viewed. 12. 1. Who are to be appointed ministers? 2. Mode of appointment. 13. 3. By whom the appointment is to be made. Why the Apostles were elected by Christ alone. Of the calling and election of St Paul. 14. Ordinary Pastors are designated by other Pastors. Why certain of the Apostles also were designated by men. 15. The election of Pastors does not belong to one individual. Other Pastors should preside, and the people consent and approve. 16. Form in which the ministers of the Church are to be ordained. No express precept but one. Laying on of hands.

1¶

We are now to speak of the order by which the Lord has willed that his Church should be governed. For although it is right that he alone should rule and reign in the Church, that he should preside over it and be conspicuous in it, and that its government should be exercised and administered by his word alone, yet because he does not dwell among us in visible presence, so as to declare his will to us with his own lips, he uses the ministry of men for this purpose (as we have said), making them, as it were, his substitutes. Latin, "quasi vicariam operam." French, "les faisans comme ses lieutenans"; making them, as it were, his substitutes.

See on this subject Augustine, De Doctrina Christiana, book 1.

2¶

By these words he shows that the ministry of men, which God employs in governing the Church, is a principal bond by which believers are held together in one body. He also indicates that the Church cannot be kept safe unless it is upheld by those safeguards to which the Lord has been pleased to entrust its safety. Christ "ascended up far above all heavens, that he might fill all things" (Ephesians 4:10). The manner of this filling is as follows. Through the ministers to whom he has committed this office, and to whom he has given grace to discharge it, he dispenses and distributes his gifts to the Church, and so shows himself in a manner actually present, by putting forth the power of his Spirit in this his institution, so that it may not be vain or fruitless. In this way the renewal of the saints is accomplished and the body of Christ is built up; in this way we grow up in all things into him who is the Head, and are joined to one another; in this way we are all brought into the unity of Christ, provided that prophecy flourishes among us, that we receive his apostles, and that we do not despise the doctrine that is ministered to us. Whoever, therefore, labors to abolish this order and this kind of government of which we speak, or belittles it as of minor importance, is plotting the devastation, or rather the ruin and destruction, of the Church. For neither the light and heat of the sun, nor food and drink, are so necessary to sustain and nourish the present life as the apostolic and pastoral office is to preserve a Church on the earth.

3¶

Accordingly, I have noted above that God has repeatedly commended the dignity of this office by the titles he has bestowed on it, so that we might hold it in the highest esteem, as among the most excellent of our blessings. He declares that in raising up teachers he confers a special benefit on men, when he bids his prophet cry out, "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace" (Isaiah 52:7), and when he calls the apostles the light of the world and the salt of the earth (Matthew 5:13, 14). Nor could the office be more highly praised than when he said, "He that heareth you heareth me; and he that despiseth you despiseth me" (Luke 10:16). But the most striking passage of all is the one in the Second Epistle to the Corinthians, where Paul treats this question, as it were, deliberately. He contends that there is nothing in the Church more noble and glorious than the ministry of the Gospel, since it is the administration of the Spirit of righteousness and eternal life. These and similar passages ought to keep that method of governing and maintaining the Church by ministers, a method which the Lord has ratified forever, from seeming worthless in our eyes and at length falling into disuse through contempt. How very necessary it is, he has declared not only by words but also by examples. When he was pleased to shed the light of his truth more brightly on Cornelius, he sent an angel from heaven to dispatch Peter to him (Acts 10:3). When he was pleased to call Paul to the knowledge of himself and to graft him into the Church, he does not address him with his own voice, but sends him to a man from whom he may receive both the doctrine of salvation and the sanctification of baptism (Acts 9:6-20). It was not by mere chance that the angel, who is the interpreter of God, refrains from declaring the will of God and orders a man to be called to declare it; nor that Christ, the only Master of believers, commits Paul to the teaching of a man, that same Paul whom he had determined to carry up into the third heaven and to honor with a wondrous revelation of things that could not be spoken (2 Corinthians 12:2). Who, then, will presume to despise or to set aside as superfluous that ministry whose usefulness God has been pleased to attest by such evidence?

4¶

Those who preside over the government of the Church according to the institution of Christ are named by Paul as, first, Apostles; second, Prophets; third, Evangelists; fourth, Pastors; and last, Teachers (Ephesians 4:11). Of these, only the last two hold an ordinary office in the Church. The Lord raised up the other three at the beginning of his kingdom, and still raises them up from time to time when the necessity of the times requires it. The nature of the apostolic function is clear from the command, "Go ye into all the world, and preach the Gospel to every creature" (Mark 16:15). No fixed limits are given them; rather, the whole world is assigned to them to be brought under the obedience of Christ, so that by spreading the Gospel as widely as they could, they might set up his kingdom everywhere. Accordingly, when Paul would prove his apostleship, he does not say that he had won some one city for Christ, but that he had spread the Gospel far and wide, that he had not built on another man's foundation, but had planted churches where the name of his Lord had never been heard. The apostles, therefore, were sent out to bring the world back from its revolt to the true obedience of God, and to establish his kingdom everywhere by the preaching of the Gospel; or, if you prefer, they were like the first architects of the Church, sent to lay its foundations throughout the world. By Prophets he means not all interpreters of the divine will, but those who excelled by special revelation; none such exist now, or they are less evident. By Evangelists I mean those who, while lower in rank than the apostles, were next to them in office, and even acted as their substitutes. Such were Luke, Timothy, Titus, and others like them; perhaps also the seventy disciples whom our Savior appointed in the second place after the apostles (Luke 10:1). According to this interpretation, which seems to me consistent with both the words and the meaning of Paul, those three functions were not instituted in the Church to be perpetual, but only to last as long as churches were to be founded where none had existed before, or at least where churches were to be carried over from Moses to Christ. Yet I do not deny that afterward God has from time to time raised up Apostles, or at least Evangelists in their place, as he has done in our own time. For such men were needed to bring the Church back from the revolt of Antichrist. Nevertheless I call the office extraordinary, because it has no place in churches that are rightly constituted. Next come Pastors and Teachers, whom the Church can never do without, and between whom, I think, there is this difference: teachers do not preside over discipline, or the administration of the sacraments, or admonitions, or exhortations, but over the interpretation of Scripture only, so that pure and sound doctrine may be maintained among believers. But all these functions are included in the pastoral office.

5¶

We now understand which offices in the government of the Church were temporary, and which were instituted to last forever. But if we class evangelists with apostles, we shall have two pairs of offices that in a manner correspond to each other. For the same likeness that our teachers bear to the ancient prophets, pastors bear to the apostles. The prophetic office was more excellent because of the special gift of revelation that accompanied it, but the office of teachers was of almost the same nature and had entirely the same end. In the same way, the twelve whom the Lord chose to publish the new preaching of the Gospel to the world (Luke 6:13) excelled others in rank and dignity. For although, from the nature of the case and the derivation of the word, all officers of the Church may properly be called apostles, because they are all sent by the Lord and are his messengers, yet since it was of great importance that a sure attestation be given to the mission of those who delivered a new and extraordinary message, it was right that the twelve (to whose number Paul was afterward added) should be distinguished from the rest by a title of their own. Paul does indeed give the same name to Andronicus and Junia, who, he says, were "of note among the apostles" (Romans 16:7); but when he speaks strictly, he confines the term to that first order. And this is the common usage of Scripture. Still, pastors (except that each has the government of a particular church assigned to him) have the same function as the apostles. Let us now look still more clearly at the nature of this function.

6¶

When our Lord sent out the apostles, he gave them a commission (as was said a little earlier) to preach the Gospel and to baptize those who believed, for the remission of sins. He had already commanded that they should distribute the sacred symbols of his body and blood after his example (Matthew 28:19; Luke 22:19). Such is the sacred, inviolable, and perpetual law laid upon those who succeed to the place of the apostles: they receive a commission to preach the Gospel and to administer the sacraments. From this we infer that those who neglect both of these falsely lay claim to the office of apostles. But what shall we say of pastors? Paul speaks not of himself only but of all pastors when he says, "Let a man so account of us, as of the ministers of Christ, and stewards of the mysteries of God" (1 Corinthians 4:1). Again, in another passage, he describes a bishop as one "holding fast the faithful word as he hath been taught, that he may be able by sound doctrine both to exhort and convince the gainsayers" (Titus 1:9). From these and similar passages, which occur everywhere, we may infer that the two principal parts of the pastoral office are to preach the Gospel and to administer the sacraments. But the work of teaching does not consist merely in public addresses; it extends also to private admonitions. So Paul calls the Ephesians to witness: "I kept back nothing that was profitable to you, but have showed you, and have taught you publicly, and from house to house, testifying both to the Jews, and also to the Greeks, repentance toward God, and faith toward our Lord Jesus Christ." A little later he says, "Remember, that, for the space of three years, I ceased not to warn every one night and day with tears" (Acts 20:20, 31). Our present purpose, however, is not to list the separate qualities of a good pastor, but only to point out what those profess who call themselves pastors: namely, that in presiding over the Church they do not hold an idle dignity, but must train the people to true godliness by the doctrine of Christ, administer the sacred mysteries, and preserve and exercise right discipline. To those who are set as watchmen in the Church the Lord declares, "When I say unto the wicked, Thou shalt surely die; and thou givest him not warning, nor speakest to warn the wicked from his wicked way, to save his life; the same wicked man shall die in his iniquity; but his blood will I require at thine hand" (Ezekiel 3:18). What Paul says of himself applies to all pastors: "For though I preach the Gospel, I have nothing to glory of: for necessity is laid upon me; yea, woe is unto me if I preach not the Gospel" (1 Corinthians 4:16). In short, what the apostles did for the whole world, every pastor should do for the flock over which he is appointed.

7¶

While we assign a church to each pastor, we do not deny that one who is bound to a single church may assist other churches, whether some disturbance has arisen that requires his presence, or his advice is sought on some doubtful matter. But because this arrangement is necessary to maintain the peace of the Church, each man has his proper duty assigned to him, lest all should fall into disorder, run up and down without any fixed calling, crowd together indiscriminately in one place, and leave the churches empty at their whim, caring more for their own convenience than for the building up of the Church. This arrangement ought, as far as possible, to be generally observed, so that every man, content with his own limits, may not trespass on another's province. Nor is this a human invention. It is an ordinance of God. For we read that Paul and Barnabas appointed presbyters over each of the churches of Lystra, Antioch, and Iconium (Acts 14:23); and Paul himself charges Titus to ordain presbyters in every town (Titus 1:5). In the same way he mentions the bishops of the Philippians, and Archippus, the bishop of the Colossians (Philippians 1:1; Colossians 4:17). And in the Acts we have his celebrated address to the presbyters of the Church at Ephesus (Acts 20:28). Let everyone, then, who takes up the government and care of one church know that he is bound by this law of divine calling. This does not mean that he is tied to the soil (as the lawyers say), that is, enslaved and, as it were, fixed in place, so that he cannot move a foot if the public good requires it and the thing is done properly and in order. But he who has been called to one place ought not to think of leaving it, nor seek to be released whenever he judges it to his own advantage. Again, if it is expedient for anyone to be transferred to another place, he ought not to attempt it on his own private motion, but should wait for public authority.

8¶

In giving the names of bishops, presbyters, and pastors without distinction to those who govern churches, I have done so on the authority of Scripture, which uses these words as synonyms. To all who discharge the ministry of the word it gives the name of bishops. So Paul, after charging Titus to ordain elders in every city, immediately adds, "A bishop must be blameless," and so on (Titus 1:5, 7). So in another place he greets several bishops in one church (Philippians 1:1). And in the Acts, the elders of Ephesus, whom he is said to have called together, he calls bishops in the course of his address (Acts 20:17). Here it should be noted that we have so far listed only those offices which consist in the ministry of the word; nor does Paul mention any others in the passage we quoted from the fourth chapter of the Epistle to the Ephesians. But in the Epistle to the Romans and the First Epistle to the Corinthians he lists other offices, such as powers, gifts of healing, interpretation, government, and care of the poor (Romans 12:7; 1 Corinthians 12:28). Of those that were temporary I say nothing, for it is not worthwhile to dwell on them. But there are two of perpetual duration, namely, government and care of the poor. By these governors I understand elders chosen from the people to join with the bishops in pronouncing censures and exercising discipline. For this is the only meaning that can be given to the passage, "He that ruleth with diligence" (Romans 12:8). From the beginning, therefore, each church had its senate. Latin, "senatum." French, "conseil ou consistoire"; council or consistory.

9¶

The care of the poor was committed to deacons, of whom Paul mentions two classes in the Epistle to the Romans: "He that giveth, let him do it with simplicity"; "he that showeth mercy, with cheerfulness" (Romans 12:8). Since it is certain that he is speaking here of public offices of the Church, there must have been two distinct classes. If I am not mistaken, in the first clause he designates the deacons who administered the alms; in the second, those who had devoted themselves to the care of the poor and the sick. Such were the widows whom he mentions in the Epistle to Timothy (1 Timothy 5:10). For there was no public office that women could discharge except that of devoting themselves to the service of the poor. If we admit this (and it certainly ought to be admitted), there will be two classes of deacons: the one serving the Church by administering the affairs of the poor, the other by caring for the poor themselves. For although the term διακονία has a wider meaning, Scripture gives the name of deacons specifically to those whom the Church appoints to dispense alms and to care for the poor, making them, as it were, stewards of the public treasury of the poor. Their origin, institution, and office are described by Luke (Acts 6:3). When a murmuring arose among the Greeks because their widows were neglected in the ministry to the poor, the apostles, pleading that they could not discharge both offices, to preach the word and to serve tables, asked the multitude to elect seven men of good report, to whom this office might be committed. Such deacons as the Apostolic Church had, it is fitting that we should have after her example.

10¶

Now since in the sacred assembly all things ought to be done decently and in order (1 Corinthians 14:40), there is nothing in which this ought to be more carefully observed than in settling its government, for disorder in any respect is nowhere more dangerous. Therefore, lest restless and turbulent men should presumptuously push themselves forward to teach or to rule (which is exactly what was going to happen), it was expressly provided that no one should take up a public office in the Church without a call (Hebrews 5:4; Jeremiah 17:16). Therefore, if anyone would be regarded as a true minister of the Church, he must first be duly called; and second, he must answer to his calling, that is, take up and carry out the office assigned to him. This may often be observed in Paul, who, when he would prove his apostleship, almost always cites his call, together with his faithfulness in discharging the office. If so great a minister of Christ does not dare to claim for himself the authority to be heard in the Church except as one appointed to it by the command of his Lord and faithfully performing what was entrusted to him, how great is the effrontery of any man who lacks one or both of these and yet demands such honor for himself! But since we have already touched on the necessity of carrying out the office, let us now treat only of the call.

11¶

The subject falls under four heads, namely, who are to be appointed ministers, in what way, by whom, and with what rite or ceremony of initiation. I am speaking of the outward and formal call, which belongs to the public order of the Church. I say nothing of that secret call of which every minister is conscious before God, but of which the Church is not a witness; I mean the good testimony of our heart that we take up the office offered to us not from ambition or greed or any other selfish motive, but from a sincere fear of God and a desire to build up the Church. This, as I have said, is indeed necessary for every one of us if we would have our ministry approved by God. Still, a man may have been duly called by the Church even though he accepted with a bad conscience, provided his wickedness is not evident. It is also usual to say that private men are called to the ministry when they seem fit and suited to discharge it; that is, because learning joined with godliness and the other endowments of a good pastor is a kind of preparation for the office. For those whom the Lord has destined for this great office he first supplies with the armor required for discharging it, so that they may not come to it empty and unprepared. Hence Paul, in the First Epistle to the Corinthians, when treating of the offices, first lists the gifts in which those who held the offices ought to excel. But since this is the first of the four heads I mentioned, let us now proceed to it.

12¶

What kind of persons should be elected bishops Paul treats at length in two passages (Titus 1:7; 1 Timothy 3:1). The substance is that none are to be chosen except those who are sound in doctrine and holy in life, and not notorious for any fault that might destroy their authority and bring disgrace on the ministry. The description of deacons and elders is entirely similar (see chapter 4, sections 10-13). We must always take care that they are not unfit for, or unequal to, the burden laid upon them; in other words, that they are furnished with the means they will need to fulfill their office. So our Savior, when about to send out his apostles, furnished them with the arms and instruments that were indispensably required. Luke 21:15; 24:49; Mark 6:15; Acts 1 8; 1 Timothy 5:22.

13¶

The third division we have adopted is by whom ministers are to be chosen. No fixed rule on this point can be drawn from the appointment of the apostles, which was somewhat different from the common call of others. Since theirs was an extraordinary ministry, it was necessary, in order to make it conspicuous by some more distinguished mark, that those who were to discharge it should be called and appointed by the mouth of the Lord himself. It was not, therefore, by any human election, but at the sole command of God and Christ, that they prepared themselves for the work. Hence, when the apostles wished to put another in the place of Judas, they did not venture to name anyone with certainty, but put forward two, so that the Lord might declare by lot which of them he wished to succeed (Acts 1:23). It is in this way that we ought to understand Paul's declaration that he was made an apostle "not of men, neither by man, but by Jesus Christ, and God the Father" (Galatians 1:1). The first part, namely, not of men, he had in common with all godly ministers of the word, for no one could rightly perform the office unless called by God. The other was proper and peculiar to him. And when he glories in it, he boasts not only that he had what belongs to a true and lawful pastor, but he also brings forward the insignia of his apostleship. For there were some among the Galatians who, seeking to belittle his authority, represented him as some ordinary disciple put in the place of the original apostles. In order to keep unimpaired the dignity of his ministry, against which he knew these attempts were aimed, he felt it necessary to show that he was in no respect inferior to the other apostles. Accordingly, he affirms that he was not chosen by the judgment of men, like some ordinary bishop, but by the mouth and plain oracle of the Lord himself.

14¶

But no sober person will deny that the regular mode of lawful calling is that bishops should be designated by men, since there are numerous passages of Scripture to this effect. Nor, as has been said, is there anything contrary to this in Paul's protest that he was not sent either of man or by man, since he is not speaking there of the ordinary election of ministers, but claiming for himself what was peculiar to the apostles. And yet the Lord, in choosing Paul in this way by special privilege, still subjected him meanwhile to the discipline of a call by the Church. For Luke relates, "As they ministered to the Lord, and fasted, the Holy Ghost said, Separate me Barnabas and Saul for the work whereunto I have called them" (Acts 13:2). Why this setting apart and laying on of hands after the Holy Spirit had attested their election, unless it was so that the discipline of the Church might be preserved in the appointing of ministers by men? God could give no more illustrious proof of his approval of this order than by causing Paul to be set apart by the Church after he had already declared that he had appointed him to be the Apostle of the Gentiles. We may see the same thing in the election of Matthias. Since the apostolic office was of such importance that they did not venture to appoint anyone to it by their own judgment, they put forward two, on one of whom the lot might fall, so that the election might have a sure testimony from heaven, and at the same time the order of the Church might not be disregarded.

15¶

The next question is whether a minister should be chosen by the whole Church, or only by his colleagues and the elders who have charge of discipline, or whether ministers may be appointed by the authority of one individual. See chapter 4, sections 10, 11; chapter 5, sections 2, 3. Also Calvin on Acts 6:3, and Luther, volume 2, page 374.

16¶

It remains to consider the form of ordination, to which we have assigned the last place in the call (see chapter 4, sections 14, 15). It is certain that when the apostles appointed anyone to the ministry, they used no other ceremony than the laying on of hands. This form was derived, I think, from the custom of the Jews, who by the laying on of hands in a manner presented to God whatever they wished to be blessed and consecrated. So Jacob, when about to bless Ephraim and Manasseh, placed his hands on their heads (Genesis 48:14). Our Lord did the same when he prayed over the little children (Matthew 19:15). With the same intent (as I suppose) the Jews, according to the command of the law, laid hands on their sacrifices. Therefore the apostles, by the laying on of hands, signified that they were making an offering to God of the man whom they admitted to the ministry; though they also did the same over those on whom they conferred the visible gifts of the Spirit (Acts 8:17; 19:6). However that may be, it was the regular form whenever they called anyone to the sacred ministry. In this way they consecrated pastors and teachers; in this way they consecrated deacons. But although there is no fixed precept concerning the laying on of hands, yet since we see that it was uniformly observed by the apostles, their careful observance of it ought to be regarded by us as a precept (see chapter 14, section 20; chapter 19, section 31). And it is certainly useful that by such a symbol the dignity of the ministry should be commended to the people, and that he who is ordained should be reminded that he is no longer his own, but is bound in service to God and the Church. Besides, it will not be an empty sign if it is restored to its genuine origin. For if the Spirit of God has instituted nothing in the Church in vain, we shall find that this ceremony of his appointment is not useless, provided it is not superstitiously abused. Lastly, it is to be observed that it was not the whole people, but only the pastors, who laid hands on ministers, though it is uncertain whether several always laid on their hands. It is certain that in the case of the deacons it was done by Paul and Barnabas and a few others (Acts 6:6; 13:3). But in another place Paul mentions that he himself, without any others, laid hands on Timothy: "Wherefore I put thee in remembrance, that thou stir up the gift of God which is in thee, by the putting on of my hands" (2 Timothy 1:6). For what is said in the First Epistle of the laying on of the hands of the presbytery I do not understand as if Paul were speaking of the college of elders. By the expression I understand the ordination itself, as if he had said, Act in such a way that the gift you received by the laying on of hands, when I made you a presbyter, may not be in vain.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin teaches that the ministry of teachers and pastors is no human contrivance but the chosen instrument by which Christ, ascended and reigning, feeds and builds his Church — he condescends to govern us by the mouths of men, and to despise the lawful minister is to despise him. A word for 2026: in an age that prizes the self-taught feed and the disembodied stream, the household of faith must recover its reverence for the called, examined, ordained man who stands in the pulpit not by ambition but by Christ's appointment. Test those who teach you against the Word, yes — Calvin would have it no other way — but do not let suspicion harden into the contempt that imagines every soul its own pastor. Sit, as a family, under faithful preaching; honor the office your Lord himself ordained; pray for the men who keep watch over your souls, that the means of grace he has fixed would not be despised in the very season we need them most.
