Book 3 · Chapter 6 · USMC modern English

The Life of a Christian Man — Scriptural Arguments Exhorting to It

This and the four following chapters treat of the Life of the Christian, and are so arranged as to admit of being classed under two principal heads. First, it must be held to be an universally acknowledged point, that no man is a Christian who does not feel some special love for righteousness, chap. 6. Secondly, in regard to the standard by which every man ought to regulate his life, although it seems to be considered in chap. 7 only, yet the three following chapters also refer to it. For it shows that the Christian has two duties to perform. First, the observance being so arduous, he needs the greatest patience. Hence chap. 8 treats professedly of the utility of the cross, and chap. 9 invites to meditation on the future life. Lastly, chap. 10 clearly shows, as in no small degree conducive to this end, how we are to use this life and its comforts without abusing them. This sixth chapter consists of two parts,—I. Connection between this treatise on the Christian Life and the doctrine of Regeneration and Repentance. Arrangement of the treatise, sec. 1-3. II. Extremes to be avoided; 1. False Christians denying Christ by their works condemned, sec. 4. 2. Christians should not despair, though they have not attained perfection, provided they make daily progress in piety and righteousness. 1. Connection between this chapter and the doctrine of Regeneration. Necessity of the doctrine concerning the Christian Life. The brevity of this treatise. The method of it. Plainness and unadorned simplicity of the Scripture system of morals. 2. Two divisions. First, Personal holiness. 1. Because God is holy. 2. Because of our communion with his saints. 3. Second division, relating to our Redemption. Admirable moral system of Scripture. Five special inducements or exhortations to a Christian Life. 4. False Christians who are opposed to this life censured 1. They have not truly learned Christ. 2. The Gospel not the guide of their words or actions. 3. They do not imitate Christ the Master. 4. They would separate the Spirit from his word. 5. Christians ought not to despond: Provided 1. They take the word of God for their guide. 2. Sincerely cultivate righteousness. 3. Walk, according to their capacity, in the ways of the Lord. 4. Make some progress. 5. Persevere.

1¶

We have said that the purpose of regeneration is to bring the life of believers into agreement and harmony with the righteousness of God, and so to confirm the adoption by which they have been received as sons. But although the law contains within it that new life by which the image of God is restored in us, yet, since our sluggishness stands in great need of both helps and incentives, it will be useful to gather out of Scripture a true account of this reformation, so that none who have a heartfelt desire for repentance may go astray in their zeal. Moreover, I am not unaware that in undertaking to describe the life of the Christian I am entering on a large and wide subject, one which, when fully considered in all its parts, is enough to fill a large volume. We see to what length the Fathers extend their exhortations in treating of individual virtues. This they do not from mere talkativeness; for whatever virtue you undertake to recommend, your pen is led of its own accord by the abundance of the material to enlarge on it, so that you seem not to have discussed it properly unless you have done so at length. My intention, however, in the plan of life which I now propose to give, is not to extend it so far as to treat each virtue separately and enlarge on it in exhortation. This must be sought in the writings of others, and particularly in the Homilies of the Fathers. The French adds, "C'est a dire, sermons populaires;" that is to say, popular sermons.

The passage in brackets is omitted in the French.

2¶

The Scriptural system of which we speak aims chiefly at two things. The first is that the love of righteousness, to which we are by no means naturally inclined, may be instilled and implanted in our minds. The second (see chapter 7) is to prescribe a rule that will keep us from going astray while we pursue righteousness. It has many admirable ways of commending righteousness. The French begins the sentence thus, "Quant est du premier poinct;" as to the first point.

3¶

To arouse us the more, it sets before us God the Father, who, as he has reconciled us to himself in his Anointed, has stamped his image upon us, to which he would have us conformed (Romans 5:4). Come, then, and let them show me a more excellent system among the philosophers, who think that they alone have a moral philosophy properly and orderly arranged. They, when they would give excellent exhortations to virtue, can only tell us to live in agreement with nature. Scripture draws its exhortations from the true source. Malachi 1:6; Ephesians 5:1; 1 John 3:1, 3; Ephesians 5:26; Romans 6:1-4; 1 Corinthians 6:11; 1 Peter 1:15, 19; 1 Corinthians 6:15; John 15:3; Ephesians 5:2, 3; Colossians 3:1, 2; 1 Corinthians 3:16, 5:17; 2 Corinthians 6:16; 1 Thessalonians 5:23

4¶

This is the place to address those who, having nothing of Christ but the name and the sign, would still be called Christians. How dare they boast of this sacred name? None have fellowship with Christ but those who have gained the true knowledge of him from the Gospel. The Apostle denies that any man has truly learned Christ who has not learned to put off "the old man, which is corrupt according to the deceitful lusts, and put on Christ" (Ephesians 4:22). They stand convicted, therefore, of falsely and unjustly claiming a knowledge of Christ, however fluently and eloquently they can talk about the Gospel. Doctrine is not a matter of the tongue but of the life; it is not grasped by the intellect and memory alone, like other branches of learning, but is received only when it takes possession of the whole soul and finds its seat and dwelling in the inmost recesses of the heart. Let them, therefore, either stop insulting God by boasting that they are what they are not, or let them show themselves not unworthy disciples of their divine Master. To doctrine, in which our religion is contained, we have given the first place, since our salvation begins with it; but it must be poured into the breast and pass into our conduct, and so transform us into itself that it does not prove unfruitful. If philosophers are rightly offended, and banish from their company in disgrace those who, while professing an art that ought to govern their conduct, turn it into mere talkative sophistry, with how much better reason shall we detest those shallow sophists who are content to let the Gospel play upon their lips, when by its power it ought to penetrate the inmost affections of the heart, fix its seat in the soul, and pervade the whole man a hundred times more than the cold discourses of philosophers?

5¶

I do not insist that the life of the Christian shall breathe nothing but the perfect Gospel, though this is to be desired and ought to be attempted. I do not insist so strictly on evangelical perfection as to refuse to acknowledge as a Christian any man who has not attained it. In that way all would be shut out of the Church, since there is no man who is not far removed from this perfection, while many who have made but little progress would be undeservedly rejected. What then? Let us set this before our eyes as the end at which we ought constantly to aim. Let it be regarded as the goal toward which we are to run. For you cannot divide the matter with God, taking up part of what his word commands and leaving out part at your pleasure. For, in the first place, God consistently commends integrity as the principal part of his worship, meaning by integrity a real singleness of mind, free from pretense and fiction, to which a double mind is opposed; as if it had been said that the spiritual beginning of a good life is when the inward affections are sincerely devoted to God in the pursuit of holiness and justice. But seeing that in this earthly prison of the body no man is supplied with strength enough to hasten on his course with due eagerness, while most are so weighed down with weakness that, hesitating and limping and even crawling on the ground, they make little progress, let each one of us go as far as his humble ability allows, and press on with the journey once begun. No one will travel so badly as not to make some degree of progress daily. This, therefore, let us never cease to do, so that we may advance daily in the way of the Lord; and let us not despair because of the slender measure of our success. However little our success may answer to our wish, our labor is not lost when today is better than yesterday, provided that with true singleness of mind we keep our aim and strive toward the goal, not speaking flattering things to ourselves, nor indulging our vices, but making it our constant effort to become better, until we attain to goodness itself. If throughout the whole course of our life we seek and follow it, we shall at length attain it, when, set free from the weakness of the flesh, we are admitted to full fellowship with God.

Calvin's burden here is plain and searching: a Christian is not made by the name, the talk, or the volubility of gospel speech, but by a heart that has learned to love righteousness and to put off the old man — doctrine transfused into the breast until it transforms the whole life. In 2026, where the name "Christian" costs nothing and a fluent post can mimic faith without a single mortified affection, this falls upon us with weight. Let it search the home first: it is not family devotions performed for show but the slow conforming of parent and child to the image of the Father that proves the seed is living. And take Calvin's mercy with his rigor — he does not demand perfection before he will own you as a brother, only that you set the goal before your eye and refuse to flatter your vices, making today better than yesterday. So press on, however small the measure. The believer who crawls toward holiness and never quits is nearer the kingdom than the eloquent man who stands still.
