A Summary of the Christian Life — Of Self-Denial

The divisions of the chapter are,—I. The rule which permits us not to go astray in the study of righteousness, requires two things—viz. that man, abandoning his own will, devote himself entirely to the service of God; whence it follows, that we must seek not our own things, but the things of God, sec. 1, 2. II. A description of this renovation or Christian life taken from the Epistle to Titus, and accurately explained under certain special heads, sec. 3 to end. 1. Consideration of the second general division in regard to the Christian life. Its beginning and sum. A twofold respect. 1. We are not our own. Respect to both the fruit and the use. Unknown to philosophers, who have placed reason on the throne of the Holy Spirit. 2. Since we are not our own, we must seek the glory of God, and obey his will. Self-denial recommended to the disciples of Christ. He who neglects it, deceived either by pride or hypocrisy, rushes on destruction. 3. Three things to be followed, and two to be shunned in life. Impiety and worldly lusts to be shunned. Sobriety, justice, and piety, to be followed. An inducement to right conduct. 4. Self-denial the sum of Paul’s doctrine. Its difficulty. Qualities in us which make it difficult. Cures for these qualities. 1. Ambition to be suppressed. 2. Humility to be embraced. 3. Candour to be esteemed. 4. Mutual charity to be preserved. 5. Modesty to be sincerely cultivated. 5. The advantage of our neighbour to be promoted. Here self-denial most necessary, and yet most difficult. Here a double remedy. 1. The benefits bestowed upon us are for the common benefit of the Church. 2. We ought to do all we can for our neighbour. This illustrated by analogy from the members of the human body. This duty of charity founded on the divine command. 6. Charity ought to have for its attendants patience and kindness. We should consider the image of God in our neighbours, and especially in those who are of the household of faith. Hence a fourfold consideration which refutes all objections. A common objection refuted. 7. Christian life cannot exist without charity. Remedies for the vices opposed to charity. 1. Mercy. 2. Humility. 3. Modesty. 4. Diligence. 5. Perseverance. 8. Self-denial, in respect of God, should lead to equanimity and tolerance. 1. We are always subject to God. 2. We should shun avarice and ambition. 3. We should expect all prosperity from the blessing of God, and entirely depend on him. 9. We ought not to desire wealth or honours without the divine blessing, nor follow the arts of the wicked. We ought to cast all our care upon God, and never envy the prosperity of others. 10. We ought to commit ourselves entirely to God. The necessity of this doctrine. Various uses of affliction. Heathen abuse and corruption.

1¶

Although the Law of God contains a perfect rule of conduct, admirably arranged, it has pleased our divine Master to train his people by a more exact method to the rule that the Law enjoins; and the leading principle of that method is this: it is the duty of believers to present their "bodies a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable unto God, which is their reasonable service" (Romans 12:1). From this he draws the exhortation: "Be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind, that ye may prove what is that good, and acceptable, and perfect will of God." The great point, then, is this: we are consecrated and dedicated to God, and therefore from now on we should not think, speak, plan, or act without a view to his glory. What he has made sacred cannot, without signal insult to him, be turned to profane use. But if we are not our own but the Lord's, it is plain both what error we must shun and to what end the actions of our life ought to be directed. We are not our own; therefore neither our own reason nor our own will is to rule our acts and counsels. We are not our own; therefore let us not make it our end to seek what may please our carnal nature. We are not our own; therefore, as far as possible, let us forget ourselves and all that is ours. On the other hand, we are God's; let us therefore live and die to him (Romans 14:8). We are God's; therefore let his wisdom and will preside over all our actions. We are God's; to him, then, as the only lawful end, let every part of our life be directed. Oh, how great is the progress of the man who, taught that he is not his own, has taken the rule and government of himself away from his own reason in order to give them to God! For as the surest road to ruin for men is to obey themselves, so the only haven of safety is to have no other will and no other wisdom than to follow the Lord wherever he leads. Let this, then, be the first step: to abandon ourselves, and to devote the whole energy of our minds to the service of God. By service I mean not only that which consists in obedience in word, but that by which the mind, stripped of its own carnal feelings, obeys the call of the Spirit of God without reserve. This transformation (which Paul calls the renewing of the mind, Romans 12:2; Ephesians 4:23), though it is the first entrance into life, was unknown to all the philosophers. They give the government of man to reason alone, thinking that she alone is to be heard; in short, they assign to her the sole direction of conduct. But Christian philosophy bids her give way and yield complete submission to the Holy Spirit, so that the man himself no longer lives, but Christ lives and reigns in him (Galatians 2:20).

2¶

From this follows the second principle: we are not to seek our own will but the Lord's, and to act with a view to advancing his glory. Great is our progress when, almost forgetting ourselves, and certainly setting our own reason aside, we faithfully make it our study to obey God and his commandments. For when Scripture commands us to lay aside private regard for ourselves, it not only strips our minds of an excessive longing for wealth, or power, or human favor, but roots out all ambition and thirst for worldly glory, and other more hidden plagues. The Christian ought indeed to be so trained and disposed as to consider that throughout his whole life he has to do with God. For this reason, as he will submit all things to the disposal and judgment of God, so he will devoutly direct his whole mind to him. For he who has learned to look to God in everything he does is at the same time turned away from all vain thoughts. This is that self-denial which Christ so strongly presses on his disciples from the very outset (Matthew 16:24), and which, as soon as it takes hold of the mind, leaves no room, first, for pride, show, and ostentation, or, second, for avarice, lust, luxury, effeminacy, or the other vices that self-love breeds. On the contrary, wherever it does not reign, the foulest vices are indulged without shame; or, if there is some appearance of virtue, it is corrupted by a depraved craving for applause. Show me, if you can, a single man who, unless he has renounced himself in obedience to the Lord's command, is disposed to do good for its own sake. Those who have not so renounced themselves have pursued virtue at least for the sake of praise. The philosophers who argued most strongly that virtue is to be desired for her own sake were so puffed up with arrogance as to make it plain that they sought virtue for no other reason than as an occasion for indulging their pride. So far, then, is God from being pleased with these hunters after popular applause, with their swollen breasts, that he declares they have received their reward in this world (Matthew 6:2), and that harlots and tax collectors are nearer the kingdom of heaven than they (Matthew 21:31). We have not yet sufficiently explained how great and how many are the obstacles that hinder a man in the pursuit of uprightness so long as he has not renounced himself. The old saying is true: there is a world of iniquity stored up in the human soul. Nor can you find any other remedy for this than to deny yourself, renounce your own reason, and direct your whole mind to the pursuit of those things which the Lord requires of you, and which you are to seek only because they are pleasing to him.

3¶

In another passage Paul gives a brief, but more distinct, account of each of the parts of a well-ordered life: "The grace of God that bringeth salvation hath appeared to all men, teaching us that, denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly, in this present world; looking for that blessed hope, and the glorious appearance of the great God and our Savior Jesus Christ; who gave himself for us, that he might redeem us from all iniquity, and purify to himself a peculiar people, zealous of good works" (Titus 2:11-14). After holding out the grace of God to stir us up and to prepare the way for his true worship, he removes the two greatest obstacles that stand in the way — namely, ungodliness, to which we are by nature too prone, and worldly lusts, which reach still farther. Under ungodliness he includes not merely superstition but everything at odds with the true fear of God. Worldly lusts are the same as the lusts of the flesh. So he commands us, with regard to both tables of the Law, to lay aside our own mind and renounce whatever our own reason and will dictate. Then he reduces all the actions of our life to three branches: sobriety, righteousness, and godliness. Sobriety undoubtedly denotes chastity and temperance as well as the pure and frugal use of temporal goods and the patient endurance of want. Righteousness takes in all the duties of equity, giving to everyone his due. Next follows godliness, which separates us from the pollutions of the world and joins us to God in true holiness. These, when bound together by an unbreakable chain, make up complete perfection. But since nothing is more difficult than to bid farewell to the will of the flesh, to subdue, indeed to forswear, our lusts, to devote ourselves to God and our brothers, and to lead an angelic life amid the pollutions of the world, Paul, to set our minds free from every entanglement, calls us back to the hope of a blessed immortality. He rightly urges us to strive, because as Christ has once appeared as our Redeemer, so at his final coming he will bring to full effect the salvation he obtained. In this way he scatters all the allurements that cloud our path and keep us from aspiring as we ought to heavenly glory; indeed, he tells us that we must be pilgrims in the world, so that we may not fail to obtain the heavenly inheritance.

4¶

Moreover, we see from these words that self-denial has respect partly to men and partly (and more especially) to God (sections 8-10). For when Scripture commands us, with regard to our fellow men, to prefer them in honor above ourselves and sincerely to labor to promote their good (Romans 12:10; Philippians 2:3), it gives us commands which our mind is utterly incapable of obeying until its natural feelings are subdued. For we all rush so blindly in the direction of self-love that everyone thinks he has good reason to exalt himself and to despise all others by comparison. If God has bestowed on us something we have no cause to regret, we immediately trust in it and grow elated, and not only swell but almost burst with pride. The vices with which we abound we both carefully hide from others and flatteringly picture to ourselves as small and trivial; indeed, we sometimes embrace them as virtues. When the same qualities we admire in ourselves appear in others, even if theirs are superior, we maliciously belittle and carp at them, so that we may not be forced to yield to them; in the same way, with their vices, we are not content with severe and sharp censure but take pains to exaggerate them. Hence the insolence with which each man, as if exempt from the common lot, seeks to exalt himself above his neighbor, confidently and proudly despising others, or at least looking down on them as his inferiors. The poor man yields to the rich, the commoner to the noble, the servant to the master, the unlearned to the learned, and yet everyone inwardly cherishes some notion of his own superiority. So each, flattering himself, sets up a kind of kingdom in his own breast; the arrogant, to please themselves, pass judgment on the minds and manners of other men, and when strife arises, the full venom shows itself. Many carry about with them some measure of gentleness so long as everything goes smoothly and pleasantly for them; but how few there are who, when stung and provoked, keep the same even temper! For this there is no other remedy than to pull up by the roots those most poisonous plagues, self-love and the love of victory (φιλονεικία καὶ φιλαυτία). This the teaching of Scripture does. For it teaches us to remember that the endowments God has bestowed on us are not our own but his free gifts, and that those who pride themselves on them betray their ingratitude. "Who maketh thee to differ," says Paul, "and what hast thou that thou didst not receive? now if thou didst receive it, why dost thou glory, as if thou hadst not received it?" (1 Corinthians 4:7). Then, by a diligent examination of our faults, let us keep ourselves humble. In this way, while nothing will remain to swell our pride, there will be much to subdue it. Again, we are commanded, whenever we see the gifts of God in others, so to revere and respect the gifts as also to honor those in whom they reside. Since God has been pleased to bestow honor on them, it would ill become us to strip them of it. Then we are told to overlook their faults — not, indeed, to encourage them by flattery, but not, on account of them, to insult those whom we ought to regard with honor and goodwill. Calvin, De Sacerdotiis Eccles. Papal., at the end.

5¶

How difficult it is to carry out the duty of seeking our neighbor's good! Unless you give up all thought of yourself and in a sense cease to be yourself, you will never accomplish it. How can you show those works of love which Paul describes unless you renounce yourself and become wholly devoted to others? "Charity (he says, 1 Corinthians 13:4) suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up, doth not behave itself unseemly, seeketh not her own, is not easily provoked," and so on. If this were the only thing required of us — to seek not our own — nature would not have the least power to comply. She so inclines us to love ourselves alone that she will not easily allow us to pass by ourselves and our own interests in order to watch over the interests of others, still less to yield our own rights of our own accord and resign them to another. But Scripture, to lead us to this, reminds us that whatever we obtain from the Lord is granted on the condition that we use it for the common good of the Church, and that, therefore, the lawful use of all our gifts is a kind and generous sharing of them with others. There can be no surer rule, nor a stronger exhortation to keep it, than when we are taught that all the endowments we possess are divine deposits entrusted to us for the very purpose of being distributed for the good of our neighbor. But Scripture goes still farther when it likens these endowments to the different members of the body (1 Corinthians 12:12). No member has its function for itself or applies it to its own private use, but passes it on to its fellow members; nor does it derive any advantage from it other than what it receives in common with the whole body. So whatever the godly man can do, he is bound to do for his brothers, consulting his own interest in no other way than by striving earnestly for the common edification of the Church. Let this, then, be our method of showing goodwill and kindness: to consider that, in regard to everything God has bestowed on us by which we can help our neighbor, we are his stewards and are bound to give account of our stewardship; and, moreover, that the only right way of administering it is the way governed by love. In this way we shall not only join the pursuit of our neighbor's good to a regard for our own, but make the latter subordinate to the former. And lest we should fail to see that this is the law for rightly administering every gift we receive from God, he applied that law long ago to the smallest tokens of his own kindness. He commanded the firstfruits to be offered to him, as a testimony from the people that it was impious to reap any benefit from goods not first consecrated to him (Exodus 22:29; 23:19). But if the gifts of God are not sanctified to us until we have with our own hand dedicated them to the Giver, it must be a gross abuse that gives no sign of such dedication. It is useless to object that you cannot enrich the Lord by your offerings. Though, as the Psalmist says, "Thou art my Lord: my goodness extendeth not unto thee," yet you can extend it "to the saints that are in the earth" (Psalm 16:2, 3); and so a comparison is drawn between sacred offerings and alms, as now corresponding to the offerings under the Law.

6¶

Moreover, so that we may not grow weary in well-doing (as would otherwise happen at once and without fail), we must add the other quality in the Apostle's list: "Charity suffereth long, and is kind, is not easily provoked" (1 Corinthians 13:4). The Lord commands us to do good to all without exception, though the greater part of them, if judged by their own merit, are most unworthy of it. But Scripture adds a most excellent reason when it tells us that we are not to look at what men deserve in themselves, but to consider the image of God, which exists in all, and to which we owe all honor and love. But in those who are of the household of faith the same rule is to be observed still more carefully, inasmuch as that image is renewed and restored in them by the Spirit of Christ. Therefore, whoever the man may be who is presented to you as needing your help, you have no ground for refusing it to him. Say he is a stranger. The Lord has put a mark on him that ought to be familiar to you; for this reason he forbids you to despise your own flesh (Galatians 6:10). Say he is lowly and of no account. The Lord points him out as one whom he has distinguished with the luster of his own image (Isaiah 58:7). Say that you are bound to him by no ties of duty. The Lord has, as it were, put him in his own place, so that in him you may recognize the many great obligations by which the Lord has bound you to himself. Say that he is unworthy of your least effort on his behalf; yet the image of God, by which he is commended to you, is worthy of you and of all your efforts. But if he not only deserves no good, but has provoked you with injury and harm, even this is no good reason why you should not embrace him in love and show him the offices of love. He has deserved very differently from me, you will say. But what has the Lord deserved? The French reads, "Car si nous disons qu'il n'a merité que mal de nous; Dieu nous pourra demander quel mal il nous a fait, lui dont nous tenons tout notre bien" — For if we say that he has deserved nothing of us but evil, God may ask us what evil he has done us, he of whom we hold our every blessing.

Matthew 5:44; 6:14; 18:35; Luke 17:3.

7¶

We shall succeed in mortifying ourselves in this way if we fulfill all the duties of love. Those duties, however, are not fulfilled by merely discharging them, even if none is left out, unless this is done from a pure feeling of love. For it can happen that a man performs every one of these offices, so far as the outward act is concerned, and yet is far from performing them rightly. For you see some who want to be thought very generous, and yet attach an insult to everything they give, by the haughtiness of their looks or the violence of their words. And to such a sorry state have we come in this unhappy age that most men almost never give alms without contempt. Such conduct ought not to have been tolerated even among the heathen; but from Christians something more is required than to wear cheerfulness on their faces and to make the discharge of their duties pleasant with courteous words. First, they should put themselves in the place of the one they see in need of their help, and pity his misfortune as if they felt and bore it themselves, so that a feeling of pity and humanity may move them to help him just as they would help themselves. He who is so minded will go and give help to his brothers, and will not only keep his acts free from any taint of arrogance or reproach, but will neither look down on the brother to whom he does a kindness as one who needed his help, nor keep him in subjection as one under obligation to him; just as we do not insult a diseased member when the rest of the body labors for its recovery, nor think it under special obligation to the other members because it has required more effort than it has returned. An exchange of offices between members is not regarded as in any way a free gift, but rather as the payment of what is due by the law of nature, and which it would be monstrous to deny. For this reason, he who has performed one kind of duty will not think himself thereby released, as usually happens when a rich man, after contributing something from his substance, hands over the remaining burdens to others as if he had nothing to do with them. Everyone should rather consider that, however great he is, he owes himself to his neighbors, and that the only limit to his generosity is the failure of his means. The extent of these should govern the extent of his charity.

8¶

Let us now consider more fully the principal part of self-denial, which, as we have said, has reference to God. Many things have already been said about it which it would be superfluous to repeat; and therefore it will be enough to view it as it trains us to equanimity and endurance. First, then, in seeking the comfort or tranquility of the present life, Scripture calls us to resign ourselves and all we have to the disposal of the Lord, and to give up to him the affections of our heart, so that he may tame and subdue them. We have a frenzied desire, an infinite eagerness, to pursue wealth and honor, to scheme for power, to pile up riches, and to collect all those trifles that seem to serve luxury and splendor. On the other hand, we have a remarkable dread, a remarkable hatred, of poverty, lowly birth, and a humble condition, and feel the strongest desire to guard against them. Hence, in those who shape their life by their own counsel, we see how restless they are in mind, how many schemes they try, to what labors they submit, in order to gain what avarice or ambition desires, or, on the other hand, to escape poverty and lowliness. To avoid such entanglements, the course that Christian men must follow is this. First, they must not long for, or hope for, or think of any kind of prosperity apart from the blessing of God; on it they must cast themselves, and there rest safely and confidently. For however much the carnal mind may seem sufficient for itself when, in the pursuit of honor or wealth, it relies on its own industry and zeal or is helped by the favor of men, it is certain that all this is nothing, and that neither intellect nor labor will be of the least use except so far as the Lord prospers both. On the contrary, his blessing alone makes a way through all obstacles and brings everything to a joyful and favorable outcome. Second, though without this blessing we may be able to acquire some measure of fame and wealth (as we see wicked men every day loaded with honors and riches), yet since those on whom the curse of God rests do not enjoy the least particle of true happiness, whatever we obtain without his blessing must turn out badly. But surely men ought not to desire what adds to their misery.

9¶

Therefore, if we believe that all prosperous and desirable success depends entirely on the blessing of God, and that when it is lacking every kind of misery and calamity awaits us, it follows that we should not strive eagerly for riches and honors, trusting in our own skill and diligence, or leaning on the favor of men, or relying on some empty notion of fortune; but should always look to the Lord, so that under his guidance we may be led to whatever lot he has provided for us. The first result will be that, instead of rushing on without regard to right and wrong, by tricks and wicked arts and with injury to our neighbors, to snatch at wealth and seize on honors, we will follow only such fortune as we may enjoy in innocence. Who can hope for the help of the divine blessing amid fraud, robbery, and other wicked arts? As this blessing attends only the one who thinks purely and acts uprightly, so it calls away all who long for it from crooked designs and evil deeds. Second, a curb will be put on us, restraining an overeager desire to become rich, or an ambitious striving after honor. How can anyone have the effrontery to expect that God will help him accomplish desires at odds with his word? What God pronounces cursed with his own lips can never be pursued with his blessing. Lastly, if our success does not match our wish and hope, we shall nevertheless be kept from impatience and from loathing our condition, whatever it may be, knowing that to feel so would be to murmur against God, at whose pleasure riches and poverty, contempt and honors, are dealt out. In short, he who leans on the divine blessing in the way described will not, in pursuing the things men usually desire most eagerly, use wicked arts which he knows would gain him nothing; nor, when anything prosperous comes to him, will he credit it to himself and his own diligence, or industry, or fortune, instead of ascribing it to God as its author. If, while the affairs of others flourish, his make little progress or even go backward, he will bear his humble lot with greater equanimity and moderation than any irreligious man bears a moderate success that merely falls short of what he wished; for he has a comfort in which he can rest more peacefully than at the very summit of wealth or power, because he considers that his affairs are ordered by the Lord in the way that best serves his salvation. This, we see, is how David was disposed, who, while he follows God and gives himself up to his guidance, declares, "Neither do I exercise myself in great matters, or in things too high for me. Surely I have behaved and quieted myself as a child that is weaned of his mother" (Psalm 131:1, 2).

10¶

Nor is it in this respect only that godly minds ought to show this tranquility and endurance; it must be extended to all the accidents to which this present life is exposed. He alone, therefore, has truly denied himself who has resigned himself entirely to the Lord, placing the whole course of his life at his disposal. Whatever happens, he whose mind is so composed will neither count himself wretched nor murmur against God because of his lot. How necessary this disposition is will be clear if you consider the many accidents to which we are exposed. Various diseases attack us again and again: at one time plague rages; at another we are caught up in all the calamities of war. Frost and hail, destroying the promise of the year, bring barrenness, which reduces us to poverty; wife, parents, children, and relatives are carried off by death; our house is destroyed by fire. These are the events that make men curse their life, loathe the day of their birth, execrate the light of heaven, even find fault with God, and (as they are eloquent in blasphemy) charge him with cruelty and injustice. The believer must in these things also contemplate the mercy and truly fatherly kindness of God. Accordingly, should he see his house reduced to solitude by the loss of his kindred, even then he will not cease to bless the Lord; his thought will be, Still the grace of the Lord, which dwells within my house, will not leave it desolate. If his crops are blasted, mildewed, or cut off by frost, or struck down by hail — the French reads, "Soit que ses bleds et vignes soyent gastées et destruites par gelée, gresle, ou autre tempeste" — whether his corn and vines are hurt and destroyed by frost, hail, or other tempest.

The whole Christian life, Calvin says, turns on three words: we are not our own. We are God's — bought, consecrated, no longer the rulers of our own reason or will — and from that flows self-denial: the surrender of ambition, the giving of our gifts as stewards for the common good of the church, the seeing of God's image even in the neighbor who has wronged us. In an age that catechizes our children from infancy to be their own, to follow their heart and curate their own glory, this is the counter-word the home most needs. Teach the young that the self enthroned is the surest road to ruin, and that the only haven is to have no will but the Lord's. Practice it where it bites hardest — preferring others in honor, bearing the irritating brother, casting our cares on God rather than scheming for wealth and standing. The watchful household does not chase what God has not blessed; it rests, weaned and quiet, in the lot he assigns.
