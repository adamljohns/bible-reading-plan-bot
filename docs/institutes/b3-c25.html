Of the Last Resurrection

There are four principal heads in this chapter,—I. The utility, necessity, truth, and irrefragable evidence of the orthodox doctrine of a final resurrection—a doctrine unknown to philosophers, sec. 1-4. II. Refutation of the objections to this doctrine by Atheists, Sadducees, Chiliasts, and other fanatics, sec. 5-7. III. The nature of the final resurrection explained, sec. 8, 9. IV. Of the eternal felicity of the elect, and the everlasting misery of the reprobate. 1. For invincible perseverance in our calling, it is necessary to be animated with the blessed hope of our Savior’s final advent. 2. The perfect happiness reserved for the elect at the final resurrection unknown to philosophers. 3. The truth and necessity of this doctrine of a final resurrection. To confirm our belief in it we have, 1. The example of Christ; and, 2. The omnipotence of God. There is an inseparable connection between us and our risen Savior. The bodies of the elect must be conformed to the body of their Head. It is now in heaven. Therefore, our bodies also must rise, and, reanimated by their souls, reign with Christ in heaven. The resurrection of Christ a pledge of ours. 4. As God is omnipotent, he can raise the dead. Resurrection explained by a natural process. The vision of dry bones. 5. Second part of the chapter, refuting objections to the doctrine of resurrection. 1. Atheists. 2. Sadducees. 3. Chiliasts. Their evasion. Various answers. 4. Universalists. Answer. 6. Objections continued. 5. Some speculators who imagine that death destroys the whole man. Refutation. The condition and abode of souls from death till the last day. What meant by the bosom of Abraham. 7. Refutation of some weak men and Manichees, pretending that new bodies are to be given. Refutation confirmed by various arguments and passages of Scripture. 8. Refutation of the fiction of new bodies continued. 9. Shall the wicked rise again? Answer in the affirmative. Why the wicked shall rise again. Why resurrection promised to the elect only. 10. The last part of the chapter, treating of eternal felicity; 1 Its excellence transcends our capacity. Rules to be observed. The glory of all the saints will not be equal. 11. Without rewarding questions which merely puzzle, an answer given to some which are not without use. 12. As the happiness of the elect, so the misery of the reprobate, will be without measure, and without end.

1¶

Christ, the Sun of righteousness, shining on us through the gospel, has, as Paul declares, conquered death and given us the light of life; and so, on believing, we are said to have passed "from death unto life." We are no longer strangers and pilgrims, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God, who has made us sit with his only begotten Son in heavenly places, so that nothing is lacking to our complete happiness. Yet, so that we may not find it grievous to be exercised in a hard warfare, as if the victory won by Christ had produced no fruit, we must attend to what is taught elsewhere concerning the nature of hope. For since we hope for what we do not see, and faith, as another passage says, is "the evidence of things not seen," we are absent from the Lord as long as we are imprisoned in the body. For this reason Paul says, "Ye are dead, and your life is hid with Christ in God. When Christ, who is our life, shall appear, then shall ye also appear with him in glory." Our present condition, therefore, requires us to "live soberly, righteously, and godly," "looking for that blessed hope, and the glorious appearing of the great God and our Savior Jesus Christ." Here we need no ordinary patience, so that we do not grow worn out with fatigue and either turn back or abandon our post. Accordingly, all that has been said so far about our salvation calls on us to lift our minds toward heaven, so that, as Peter urges, though we do not now see Christ, "yet believing," we may "rejoice with joy unspeakable and full of glory," receiving the end of our faith, even the salvation of our souls. 2 Timothy 1:10; John 5:24; Ephesians 2:6, 19; Romans 7:16-18; Hebrews 11:1; 2 Corinthians 5:6; Colossians 3:3; Titus 2:12.

2¶

In ancient times the philosophers discoursed, and even debated with one another, about the chief good; yet none of them except Plato acknowledged that it consisted in union with God. Yet he could not form even an imperfect idea of its true nature, and this is no wonder, since he had learned nothing of the sacred bond of that union. We, even in this earthly pilgrimage of ours, know in what our perfect and only happiness consists — a happiness which, while we long for it, sets our hearts more and more on fire each day, until we come to its full enjoyment. That is why I said that none share in the benefits of Christ except those who lift their minds to the resurrection. This, accordingly, is the mark that Paul sets before believers, and at which he says they are to aim, forgetting everything else until they reach it (Philippians 3:8). All the more strenuously, then, must we strive for it, lest, if the world absorbs us, we be severely punished for our sloth. French, "nous recevions un povre salaire de nostre lascheté et paresse"; that is, we receive a poor wage for our carelessness and sloth.

3¶

The very importance of the subject ought to increase our zeal. Paul rightly contends that if Christ is not risen, the whole gospel is a delusion and empty (1 Corinthians 15:13-17); for our condition would be more miserable than that of other mortals, since we are exposed to much hatred and insult and face danger every hour — indeed, we are like sheep appointed for slaughter. And so the authority of the gospel would fail, not in one part only, but in its very essence, including both our adoption and the completion of our salvation. Let us, therefore, give heed to a matter that is the most serious of all, so that no length of time may produce weariness in us. I have put off to this place the brief treatment I am to give it, so that my readers, having received Christ, the author of perfect salvation, may learn to rise higher and to know that he is clothed with heavenly immortality and glory in order that the whole body may be made conformable to the Head. For in this way the Holy Spirit is constantly setting before us in his person a pattern of the resurrection. It is difficult to believe that after our bodies have been consumed with rottenness, they will rise again at their appointed time. And so, while many of the philosophers maintained the immortality of the soul, few of them assented to the resurrection of the body. Although they were inexcusable in this, we are reminded by it that the subject is too difficult for human understanding to reach. To enable faith to overcome this great difficulty, Scripture supplies two supporting proofs: the one, the likeness of Christ's resurrection; the other, the omnipotence of God. Therefore, whenever the subject of the resurrection is considered, let us think of the case of our Savior, who, having completed his mortal course in the nature he had assumed, obtained immortality, and is now the pledge of our future resurrection. For in the miseries that beset us, we always bear "about in the body the dying of the Lord Jesus, that the life also of Jesus might be made manifest in our mortal flesh" (2 Corinthians 4:10). It is not lawful, it is not even possible, to separate him from us without tearing him apart. Hence Paul's argument, "If there be no resurrection of the dead, then is Christ not risen" (1 Corinthians 15:13); for he takes it as an acknowledged principle that when Christ was subjected to death, and by rising won a victory over death, it was not on his own account, but that in the Head was begun what must necessarily be fulfilled in all the members, according to the rank and order of each. For it would not be fitting for us to be made equal to him in every respect. It is said in the psalm, "Neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption" (Psalm 16:10). Although a portion of this confidence belongs to us according to the measure given to us, yet the full effect appeared only in Christ, who, free from all corruption, took up again a spotless body. Then, so that there may be no doubt about our fellowship with Christ in a blessed resurrection, and so that we may be content with this pledge, Paul plainly affirms that he sits in the heavens and will come as judge on the last day for the express purpose of changing our vile body, "that it may be fashioned like unto his glorious body" (Philippians 3:21). For he says elsewhere that God did not raise his Son from death to give a single isolated display of his mighty power, but that the Spirit exerts the same power in those who believe; and accordingly he says that the Spirit, when he dwells in us, is life, because the end for which he was given is to quicken our mortal body (Romans 8:10, 11; Colossians 3:4). I only touch briefly on subjects that might be treated more fully and that deserve to be adorned more splendidly; and yet in the little I have said I trust that godly readers will find enough material for building up their faith. Christ rose again so that he might have us as partakers with him of the life to come. He was raised up by the Father, inasmuch as he was the Head of the Church, from which he cannot possibly be severed. He was raised up by the power of the Spirit, who performs the work of quickening in us as well. In short, he was raised up to be the resurrection and the life. But just as we have said that in this mirror we behold a living image of the resurrection, so it provides sure evidence to support our minds, provided we do not faint or grow weary at the long delay; for it is not ours to measure out the periods of time at our own pleasure, but to wait patiently until God in his own time renews his kingdom. To this Paul refers when he says, "But every man in his own order: Christ the first-fruits; afterward they that are Christ's at his coming" (1 Corinthians 15:23).

But so that no question may be raised about the resurrection of Christ, on which ours is founded, we see how often and in how many ways he has borne witness to it. Scoffers will deride the narrative given by the Evangelist as a childish fable. For what weight will they give to a message that timid women bring, and that disciples almost dead with fear afterward confirm? Why does Christ not rather set up the illustrious trophies of his victory in the middle of the temple and the marketplace? Why does he not come forth and strike terror into Pilate in his very presence? Why does he not show himself alive again to the priests and to all Jerusalem? Profane men will scarcely admit that the witnesses he chooses are well qualified. I answer that, although at the beginning their weakness was contemptible, the whole was directed by the wonderful providence of God, so that, partly from love to Christ and religious zeal, partly from unbelief, those who had just been overcome with fear now hurry to the tomb — not only that they might be eyewitnesses of the fact, but that they might hear angels announce what they actually saw. How can we question the truthfulness of those who regarded what the women told them as a fable until they saw the reality? It is not strange that the whole people, and the governor as well, after they had been furnished with evidence sufficient to convince them, were not allowed to see Christ or the other signs (Matthew 27:66; 28:11). The tomb is sealed, sentinels keep watch, and on the third day the body is not found. The soldiers are bribed to spread the report that his disciples had stolen the body — as if the disciples had had the means of disarming a band of soldiers, or had been supplied with weapons, or had been trained for so daring an attempt. But if the soldiers did not have courage enough to drive them off, why did they not pursue them and seize some of them with the help of the populace? Pilate, therefore, in effect set his seal to the resurrection of Christ, and the guards who were posted at the tomb, by their silence or their falsehood, also became heralds of his resurrection. Meanwhile the voice of angels was heard: "He is not here, but is risen" (Luke 24:6). The heavenly splendor plainly shows that they were not men but angels. Afterward, if any doubt still remained, Christ himself removed it. The disciples saw him often; they even touched his hands and his feet, and their unbelief does no small service in confirming our faith. He spoke with them about the mysteries of the kingdom of God, and at length, while they watched, he ascended to heaven. This sight was shown not to the eleven apostles only, but was seen by more than five hundred brethren at once (1 Corinthians 15:6). Then, by sending the Holy Spirit, he gave proof not only of life but also of supreme power, as he had foretold: "It is expedient for you that I go away: for if I go not away, the Comforter will not come unto you" (John 16:7). Paul was not thrown down on the road by the power of a dead man, but felt that he whom he was opposing possessed sovereign authority. To Stephen he appeared for another purpose — namely, that he might overcome the fear of death by the certainty of life. To refuse assent to these many and authentic proofs is not hesitancy, but depraved and therefore infatuated obstinacy.

4¶

We have said that in proving the resurrection our thoughts must be directed to the immense power of God. Paul teaches this briefly when he says that the Lord Jesus Christ "shall change our vile body, that it may be fashioned like unto his glorious body, according to the working of that mighty power whereby he is able even to subdue all things unto himself" (Philippians 3:21). Therefore nothing could be more out of place than to look here at what can be done by natural means, when the subject set before us is a miracle beyond all reckoning, which by its very greatness overwhelms our senses. Paul, however, by bringing forward a proof from nature, refutes the senselessness of those who deny the resurrection: "Thou fool, that which thou sowest is not quickened except it die," and so on (1 Corinthians 15:36). He says that in seed there is a kind of resurrection, because the crop is produced out of corruption. Nor would the thing be so hard to believe if we were as attentive as we ought to be to the wonders that meet our eyes in every quarter of the world. But let us remember that no one is truly persuaded of the future resurrection except the one who, carried away with wonder, gives God the glory.

Lifted up by this conviction, Isaiah exclaims, "Thy dead men shall live, together with my dead body shall they arise. Awake and sing, ye that dwell in dust" (Isaiah 26:19). In desperate circumstances he rises to God, the author of life, in whose hand are "the issues from death" (Psalm 68:20). Job too, when he was more like a corpse than a living man, trusting in the power of God, does not hesitate, as if in full vigor, to rise up to that day: "I know that my Redeemer liveth, and that he will stand at the latter day upon the earth" (that is, that he will there put forth his power); "and though after my skin worms destroy this body, yet in my flesh shall I see God; whom I shall see for myself, and mine eyes shall behold, and not another" (Job 19:25-27). For though some resort to a more subtle interpretation, by which they twist these passages as if they were not to be understood of the resurrection, they only confirm what they are eager to overthrow; for holy men, in seeking comfort in their misfortunes, turn for relief merely to the likeness of a resurrection. This is better learned from a passage in Ezekiel. When the Jews scorned the promise of return, and objected that it was no more probable than that the dead should come forth from the tomb, there is shown to the prophet in a vision a field covered with dry bones, which at the command of God recover sinews and flesh. Though under that figure he encourages the people to hope for their return, yet the ground of hope is taken from the resurrection, since it is the chief type of all the deliverances that believers experience in this world. So also Christ declares that the voice of the Gospel gives life; but because the Jews did not receive it, he immediately adds, "Marvel not at this; for the hour is coming in which all that are in the grave shall hear his voice, and shall come forth" (John 5:28, 29). Therefore, amid all our conflicts let us exult after the example of Paul that he who has promised us the life to come "is able to keep that" which "is committed unto him," and so let us glory that there is laid up for us "a crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, shall give" (2 Timothy 1:12; 4:8). In this way all the hardships we may endure will be a demonstration of our future life, "seeing it is a righteous thing with God to recompense tribulation to them that trouble you; and to you who are troubled rest with us, when the Lord Jesus shall be revealed from heaven with his mighty angels, in flaming fire" (2 Thessalonians 1:6-8). But we must attend to what he adds shortly after — namely, that he "shall come to be glorified in his saints, and to be admired in all them that believe," by receiving the Gospel.

5¶

Although the minds of men ought to be continually occupied with this pursuit, yet, as if they had deliberately resolved to banish all memory of the resurrection, they have called death the end of all things, the extinction of man. For Solomon certainly expresses the commonly received opinion when he says, "A living dog is better than a dead lion" (Ecclesiastes 9:4). And again, "Who knoweth the spirit of man that goes upward, and the spirit of the beast that goes downward to the earth?" Calvin translates, "Quis scit an hominis anima ascendit sursum?" — Who knows whether the soul of man goes upward? and so on.

101 D101 Chiliasm (from a Greek word meaning "a thousand") arose very early in the history of theology. Some of the early Church Fathers distinguished between a first and a second resurrection, and held that there would be an intervening millennial kingdom in which Christ would reign with His saints upon the earth. This view may be found in Papias, Irenaeus, Barnabas, Hermas, Justin Martyr, and Tertullian (all from the second century). But by the time of Luther and Calvin, the leading theologians (both Roman Catholic and Protestant) had rejected the doctrine of an earthly millennium. Calvin calls it a "fiction," and says that it is "too puerile to need or to deserve refutation."

102 D102 Calvin's chief objection to "Chiliasm" appears to be the alleged limitation, to a period of one thousand years, of the reign of Christ with His saints. A secondary objection arises out of his interpretation of Revelation 20:2-7 (in which the term "thousand years" appears six times) as referring to the Church militant in this world. These objections against "Chiliasm" would today apply only to that view of the last things called Premillennialism. However, if a person held (1) that Christ's kingdom is spiritually present, as He rules in the hearts of His elect; (2) that Christ's kingdom shall have a future, earthly manifestation, when He shall reign with His saints upon the earth; and (3) that Christ's kingdom will not cease at the close of the thousand years, but will merge into eternity; then it would appear that Calvin's chief objection to this view would be removed. Of course, his secondary objection would remain as a difference of hermeneutical (interpretive) approach and method.

6¶

Besides these, two other dreams have been invented by men who indulge a wicked curiosity. Some, supposing that the whole man perishes, have thought that the soul will rise again with the body; while others, admitting that spirits are immortal, hold that they will be clothed with new bodies, and so deny the resurrection of the flesh. Since I have already touched on the former point in speaking of the creation of man, it will be enough to remind the reader again how groveling an error it is to turn a spirit, formed after the image of God, into a passing breath that animates the body only during this fading life, and to reduce the temple of the Holy Spirit to nothing; in short, to rob of the badge of immortality that part of ourselves in which the divinity shines most brightly and the marks of immortality are most conspicuous, so as to make the condition of the body better and more excellent than that of the soul. Very different is the course Scripture takes, for it compares the body to a tabernacle, out of which it describes us as moving when we die, because it judges us by that part which distinguishes us from the lower animals. So Peter, referring to his approaching death, says, "Knowing that shortly I must put off this my tabernacle" (2 Peter 1:14). Paul again, speaking of believers, after saying, "If our earthly house of this tabernacle were dissolved, we have a building of God," adds, "Whilst we are at home in the body, we are absent from the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:1, 6). If the soul did not survive the body, how could it be present with the Lord on being separated from the body? But an apostle removes all doubt when he says that we go "to the spirits of just men made perfect" (Hebrews 12:23); by these words he means that we are joined in fellowship with the holy patriarchs, who, even in death, cultivate the same godliness, so that we cannot be members of Christ unless we are united with them. And if the soul, when unclothed of the body, did not retain its essence and were not capable of blessed glory, our Savior would not have said to the thief, "Today shalt thou be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43). Trusting in these clear proofs, let us not hesitate, after the example of our Savior, to commend our spirits to God when we come to die, or, after the example of Stephen, to commit ourselves to the protection of Christ, who with good reason is called "the Shepherd and Bishop" of our souls (Acts 7:59; 1 Peter 2:25). Moreover, to pry curiously into their intermediate state is neither lawful nor profitable (see Calvin, Psychopannychia). Many torment themselves greatly by debating what place the souls occupy, and whether or not they already enjoy heavenly glory. It is foolish and rash to inquire into hidden things further than God permits us to know. Scripture, after telling us that Christ is present with them and receives them into paradise (John 12:32), and that they are comforted, while the souls of the reprobate suffer the torments they have deserved, goes no further. What teacher or doctor will reveal to us what God has concealed? As to the place of their abode, the question is no less futile and pointless, since we know that the soul does not have the same dimensions as the body. French, "La question quant au lieu est bien frivole et sotte: veu que nous savons que l'ame n'a pas ses mesures de long et de large, comme le corps"; that is, the question as to place is very frivolous and foolish, seeing that we know the soul has no measures of length and breadth like the body.

7¶

Equally monstrous is the error of those who imagine that the soul, instead of taking up again the body with which it is now clothed, will receive a new and different body. Nothing could be more futile than the reason the Manichees give — namely, that it would be unfitting for impure flesh to rise again: as if there were no impurity in the soul, and yet this does not shut it out from the hope of heavenly life. It is just as if they were to say that what is infected by the taint of sin cannot be purified by God; for I say nothing now of the raving dream that flesh is impure by nature because it was created by the devil. I only maintain that nothing in us at present that is unworthy of heaven is any obstacle to the resurrection. But, first, Paul commands believers to cleanse themselves from "all filthiness of the flesh and spirit" (2 Corinthians 7:1) — the judgment which is to follow, that everyone shall "receive the things done in his body, according to that he has done, whether it be good or bad" (2 Corinthians 5:10). With this agrees what he says to the Corinthians: "That the life also of Jesus might be made manifest in our body" (2 Corinthians 4:10). For this reason he says elsewhere, "I pray God your whole spirit and soul and body be preserved blameless unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Thessalonians 5:23). He says "body" as well as "spirit and soul," and no wonder; for it would be utterly absurd that bodies which God has dedicated to himself as temples should fall into corruption without hope of resurrection. What? Are they not also members of Christ? Does he not pray that God would sanctify every part of them, and does he not command them to praise his name with their tongues, to lift up pure hands, and to offer sacrifices? That part of man, then, which the heavenly Judge honors so highly — what madness is it for any mortal man to reduce it to dust without hope of revival? In the same way, when Paul urges, "glorify God in your body, and in your spirit, which are God's," he certainly does not allow that what he claims for God as sacred is to be handed over to eternal corruption. Nor, indeed, does Scripture give a clearer explanation on any subject than on the resurrection of our flesh. "This corruptible," says Paul, "must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality" (1 Corinthians 15:53). If God formed new bodies, where would this change of quality be? If it were said only that we must be renewed, the ambiguity of the expression might perhaps leave room for quibbling; but here, pointing with his finger to the bodies with which we are clothed, and promising that they will be incorruptible, he very plainly affirms that no new bodies are to be manufactured. "Indeed," as Tertullian says, "he could not have spoken more expressly if he had held his skin in his hands" (Tertullian, On the Resurrection of the Flesh). Nor can any quibble enable them to escape the force of another passage in which, saying that Christ will be the Judge of the world, he quotes from Isaiah, "As I live, saith the Lord, every knee shall bow to me" (Romans 14:11; Isaiah 45:23); for he openly declares that those whom he was addressing will have to give an account of their lives. This could not be true if new bodies were to be brought before the judgment seat. Moreover, there is no ambiguity in the words of Daniel: "Many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (Daniel 12:2); for he does not bring new matter from the four elements to compose men, but calls the dead forth from their graves. And the reason that dictates this is plain. For if death, which had its origin in the fall of man, is accidental to our nature, the renewal produced by Christ must take place in the same body that began to be mortal. And certainly, since the Athenians mocked Paul for asserting the resurrection (Acts 17:32), we may infer what his preaching was; their derision has no small force to confirm our faith. The saying of our Savior is also worth noting: "Fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell" (Matthew 10:28). Here there would be no ground for fear if the body we now have were not liable to punishment. Nor is another saying of our Savior any less clear: "The hour is coming, in the which all that are in the graves shall hear his voice, and shall come forth; they that have done good, unto the resurrection of life; and they that have done evil, unto the resurrection of damnation" (John 5:28, 29). Shall we say that the soul rests in the grave so that it may hear the voice of Christ there, and not rather that the body will, at his command, recover the vigor it had lost? Moreover, if we are to receive new bodies, where will the conformity of the Head and the members be? Christ rose again. Was it by forming a new body for himself? No; he had foretold, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (John 2:19). The mortal body he had carried before, he received again; for it would not have profited us much if a new body had been substituted, and the one that had been offered in expiatory sacrifice had been destroyed. We must, therefore, attend to the connection that the Apostle celebrates, that we rise because Christ rose (1 Corinthians 15:12); for nothing is less likely than that the flesh in which we bear about the dying of Christ will have no share in the resurrection of Christ. This was even shown by a striking example when, at the resurrection of Christ, many bodies of the saints came forth from their graves. For it cannot be denied that this was a prelude, or rather a pledge, of the final resurrection for which we hope, such as had already appeared in Enoch and Elijah, whom Tertullian calls candidates for resurrection, because, exempted from corruption in both body and soul, they were received into the keeping of God.

8¶

I am ashamed to spend so many words on so clear a matter; but my readers will kindly bear with the annoyance, so that perverse and presumptuous minds may not be able to seize on any flaw to deceive the simple. The flighty spirits with whom I am now disputing put forward a fiction of their own brain, that in the resurrection there will be a creation of new bodies. Their only reason for thinking so is that it seems incredible to them that a dead body, long wasted by corruption, should return to its former state. Mere unbelief, therefore, is the parent of their opinion. The Spirit of God, on the contrary, consistently urges us in Scripture to hope for the resurrection of our flesh. For this reason Baptism is, according to Paul, a seal of our future resurrection; and in the same way the holy Supper invites us to expect it with confidence, when with our mouths we receive the symbols of spiritual grace. And certainly the whole exhortation of Paul, "Yield ye your members as instruments of righteousness unto God" (Romans 6:13), would be cold, if he did not add, as he does in another passage, "He that raised up Christ from the dead shall also quicken your mortal bodies" (Romans 8:11). For what would it profit to devote feet, hands, eyes, and tongues to the service of God, if these did not afterward share in the benefit and the reward? Paul expressly confirms this when he says, "The body is not for fornication, but for the Lord; and the Lord for the body. And God has both raised up the Lord, and will also raise up us by his own power" (1 Corinthians 6:13, 14). The words that follow are clearer still: "Know ye not that your bodies are the members of Christ?" "Know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost?" (1 Corinthians 6:15, 19). Meanwhile we see how he connects the resurrection with chastity and holiness, just as shortly afterward he includes our bodies in the purchase of redemption. It would be contrary to reason that the body in which Paul bore the marks of his Savior, and in which he magnificently exalted him (Galatians 6:17), should lose the reward of the crown. So he glories in this way: "Our conversation is in heaven; from whence also we look for the Saviour, the Lord Jesus Christ: Who shall change our vile body, that it may be fashioned like unto his glorious body" (Philippians 3:20, 21). As it is true "that we must through much tribulation enter into the kingdom of God" (Acts 14:22), so it would be unreasonable that this entrance should be denied to the bodies that God trains under the banner of the cross and adorns with the palm of victory.

Accordingly, the saints never had any doubt that they would one day be the companions of Christ, who takes upon his own person all the afflictions by which we are tried, so that he may show their quickening power. Latin, "ut vivificas esse doceat"; French, "pour monstrer quells nous meinent à vie"; that is, to show that they lead us to life.

It remains to make a passing remark on the manner of the resurrection. I speak this way because Paul, by calling it a mystery, urges us to sobriety, so that he may restrain a licentious indulgence in free and subtle speculation. First, we must hold, as has already been observed, that the body in which we shall rise will be the same as at present with respect to substance, but that its quality will be different; just as the body of Christ that was raised up was the same as the one that had been offered in sacrifice, and yet excelled in other qualities, as if it had been altogether different. Paul declares this by familiar examples (1 Corinthians 15:39). For as the flesh of man and of beasts is the same in substance but not in quality, and as all the stars are made of the same matter but have different degrees of brightness, so he shows that though we shall keep the substance of the body, there will be a change by which its condition will become far more excellent. The corruptible body, therefore, in order that we may be raised, will not perish or vanish away, but, stripped of corruption, will be clothed with incorruption. Since God has all the elements at his disposal, no difficulty can prevent him from commanding the earth, the fire, and the water to give up what they seem to have destroyed. Isaiah also testifies to this, though not without a figure: "Behold, the Lord comes out of his place to punish the inhabitants of the earth for their iniquity: the earth also shall disclose her blood, and shall no more cover her slain" (Isaiah 26:21). But a distinction must be made between those who died long ago and those who will be found alive on that day. For as Paul declares, "We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed" (1 Corinthians 15:51); that is, there will be no need for a period of time to pass between death and the beginning of the second life, for in a moment of time, in the twinkling of an eye, the trumpet will sound, raising up the dead incorruptible and, by a sudden change, fitting those who are alive for the same glory. So in another passage he comforts believers who were to undergo death, telling them that those who are alive at that time will not go before the dead, because those who have fallen asleep in Christ will rise first (1 Thessalonians 4:15). Should anyone press the Apostle's declaration, "It is appointed unto all men once to die" (Hebrews 9:27), the answer is easy: when the natural state is changed, there is an appearance of death, which is fitly called by that name; and therefore there is no inconsistency between these two things — namely, that all, when stripped of their mortal body, will be renewed by death, and yet that where the change is sudden, there will be no necessary separation between the soul and the body.

9¶

But a more difficult question arises here: How can the resurrection, which is a special benefit of Christ, be common to the ungodly, who lie under the curse of God? We know that in Adam all died. Christ has come to be the resurrection and the life (John 11:25). Is it to revive the whole human race without distinction? But what could be more out of place than that the ungodly, in their obstinate blindness, should obtain what the godly worshipers of God receive by faith alone? It is certain, therefore, that there will be one resurrection to judgment and another to life, and that Christ will come to separate the kids from the goats (Matthew 25:32). I observe that this ought not to seem very strange, since something resembling it happens every day. We know that in Adam we were deprived of the inheritance of the whole world, and that the same reason that shuts us out from eating of the tree of life shuts us out also from common food. How is it, then, that God not only makes his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, but that, with respect to the uses of the present life, his boundless generosity is constantly flowing forth in rich abundance? From this we certainly perceive that things which belong properly to Christ and his members overflow to the wicked also; not that their possession of them is lawful, but that they may thus be rendered the more inexcusable. So the wicked often experience the kindness of God, not in ordinary measure, but in such measure as sometimes throws all the blessings of the godly into the shade, though in the end these lead to their greater damnation. Should it be objected that the resurrection is not properly compared with fading and earthly blessings, I answer again that when the devils were first estranged from God, the fountain of life, they deserved to be utterly destroyed; yet, by the wonderful counsel of God, an intermediate state was prepared for them, in which they might live in death without life. It ought not to seem in any way more absurd that there is to be an accidental resurrection of the ungodly, which will drag them against their will before the judgment seat of Christ, whom they now refuse to receive as their master and teacher. To be consumed by death would be a light punishment if they were not, in punishment of their rebellion, to be brought before the Judge whom they have provoked to a vengeance without measure and without end. But although we are to hold, as already observed, and as is contained in Paul's celebrated confession before Felix, "That there shall be a resurrection of the dead, both of the just and unjust" (Acts 24:15), yet Scripture more often presents the resurrection, together with heavenly glory, as intended for the children of God alone; because, properly speaking, Christ comes not for the destruction but for the salvation of the world. And therefore in the Creed only the life of blessedness is mentioned.

10¶

But since the prophecy that death shall be swallowed up in victory (Hosea 13:14) will only then be fulfilled, let us always remember that the end of the resurrection is eternal happiness, of whose excellence scarcely the smallest part can be described by all that human tongues can say. For though we are truly told that the kingdom of God will be full of light, and gladness, and happiness, and glory, yet the things meant by these words remain far removed from our senses and, as it were, wrapped in a riddle, until the day comes on which he will show us his glory face to face (1 Corinthians 15:54). "Now," says John, "are we the sons of God; and it does not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2). Hence, since the prophets were unable to describe that spiritual blessedness in words, they usually portrayed it by bodily things. On the other hand, because the fervor of our desire must be kindled by some taste of its sweetness, let us especially dwell on this thought: if God contains in himself, as an inexhaustible fountain, all fullness of blessing, those who aspire to the supreme good and to perfect happiness must not long for anything beyond him. This we are taught in several passages: "Fear not, Abraham; I am thy shield, and thy exceeding great reward" (Genesis 15:1). With this agrees David's sentiment: "The Lord is the portion of mine inheritance, and of my cup: thou maintainest my lot. The lines are fallen unto me in pleasant places" (Psalm 16:5, 6). Again, "I shall be satisfied when I awake with thy likeness" (Psalm 17:15). Peter declares that the purpose for which believers are called is that they may be "partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4). How so? Because "he shall come to be glorified in his saints and to be admired in all them that believe" (2 Thessalonians 1:10). If our Lord will share his glory, power, and righteousness with the elect — indeed, will give himself to be enjoyed by them, and, what is better still, will in a certain manner become one with them — let us remember that every kind of happiness is included in this. But when we have made great progress in meditating this way, let us understand that if the conceptions of our minds are set against the sublimity of the mystery, we are still halting at the very entrance. French, "Et encore quand nous aurons bien profité en cette meditation, se nous faut il entendre que nous sommes encore tout au bas et à la premiere entree, et que jamais nous n'approcherons durant cette vie à la hautesse de ce mystere"; that is, and still, when we have profited much by meditating this way, we must understand that we are still far beneath it, at the very threshold, and that never during this life shall we come near the height of this mystery.

11¶

While all the godly will agree on this with one consent, because it is sufficiently attested by the word of God, they will, on the other hand, avoid tangled questions which they feel to be a hindrance in their way, and so keep within the prescribed limits. As for myself, I not only refrain personally from needless investigation of useless matters, but also think myself bound to take care not to encourage the frivolity of others by answering them. Men puffed up with vain knowledge are often asking how great the difference will be between prophets and apostles, and again between apostles and martyrs; by how many degrees virgins will surpass those who are married; in short, they leave no corner of heaven untouched by their speculations. Next it occurs to them to ask to what end the world is to be restored, since the children of God will not need any part of this great and incomparable abundance, but will be like the angels, whose abstinence from food is a symbol of eternal blessedness. I answer that, apart from any use, there will be such pleasantness in the very sight, and such delight in the very knowledge, that this happiness will far surpass all the means of enjoyment now afforded to us. Let us suppose ourselves placed in the richest region of the globe, where no kind of pleasure is lacking: who is there that is not, every now and then, hindered and shut out by disease from enjoying the gifts of God? Who does not often interrupt the course of his enjoyment by intemperance? It follows, then, that enjoyment pure and free from every defect, even though it is of no use to a corruptible life, is the summit of happiness. Others go further and ask whether dross and other impurities in metals will have no existence at the restoration, and are inconsistent with it. Though I should go so far as to concede this to them, yet I expect with Paul a repair of those defects which first began with sin, and on account of which the whole creation groans and travails in pain (Romans 8:22). Others go a step further and ask what better condition can await the human race, since the blessing of offspring will then come to an end. The answer to this difficulty also is easy. When Scripture so highly praises the blessing of offspring, it refers to the progress by which God is constantly urging nature forward to its goal; in perfection itself we know that the case is different. But since such alluring speculations instantly captivate the unwary, who are then led further into the labyrinth until at length, each one being pleased with his own views, there is no limit to disputing, the best and shortest course for us will be to rest content with seeing through a glass darkly until we see face to face. Few out of the vast multitude of mankind are concerned about how they are to get to heaven; all would gladly know before the time what is done in heaven. Almost all, while slow and sluggish in entering the contest, are already picturing to themselves imaginary triumphs.

12¶

Moreover, since language cannot describe the severity of the divine vengeance on the reprobate, their pains and torments are pictured to us by bodily things, such as darkness, weeping and gnashing of teeth, unquenchable fire, the ever-gnawing worm (Matthew 8:12; 22:13; Mark 9:43; Isaiah 66:24). It is certain that by such modes of expression the Holy Spirit meant to strike all our senses with dread, as when it is said, "Tophet is ordained of old; yea, for the king it is prepared: he has made it deep and large; the pile thereof is fire and much wood; the breath of the Lord, like a stream of brimstone, does kindle it" (Isaiah 30:33). As we thus need help to conceive the miserable doom of the reprobate, so the consideration on which we ought chiefly to dwell is the fearful consequence of being cut off from all fellowship with God; and not only so, but of feeling that his majesty is set against us, while we cannot possibly escape from it. For, first, his indignation is like a raging fire, at whose touch all things are devoured and annihilated. Next, all creatures are the instruments of his judgment, so that those to whom the Lord will thus openly display his anger will feel that heaven, and earth, and sea, and all beings, animate and inanimate, are, as it were, inflamed with dire indignation against them and armed for their destruction. Therefore the Apostle made no trivial declaration when he said that unbelievers will be "punished with everlasting destruction from the presence of the Lord, and from the glory of his power" (2 Thessalonians 1:9). And whenever the prophets strike terror by means of bodily figures, although, considering our dull understanding, there is nothing extravagant in their language, yet they give preludes of the future judgment in the sun and the moon and the whole fabric of the world. Hence unhappy consciences find no rest, but are tossed and driven about by a dreadful whirlwind, feeling as if torn by an angry God, pierced through with deadly darts, terrified by his thunderbolts, and crushed by the weight of his hand; so that it would be easier to plunge into abysses and whirlpools than to endure these terrors for a moment. How fearful, then, must it be to be beset in this way throughout eternity! On this subject there is a memorable passage in the ninetieth Psalm: although God by a mere look scatters all mortals and brings them to nothing, yet because his worshipers are more timid in this world, he presses them all the more, so that he may spur them on, while burdened with the cross, to press forward until he himself shall be all in all.

END OF BOOK THREE.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin closes Book Three lifting our eyes from time to the last resurrection — that hope without which, as Paul says, the whole gospel is vain and we are of all men most miserable. Christ rose not as an isolated wonder but as the firstfruits and pledge that our very bodies, the same in substance though glorified in quality, shall be raised conformed to his. This is the steel that holds the believer at his post when the warfare grows long. In a 2026 that calls death the end of all things and treats this fleeting life as the whole account, set this conviction deep in your household: the body laid in the grave is not abandoned but awaited, and every tribulation borne under the banner of the cross is a down payment on glory. Let the immeasurable joy reserved for the elect — and the measureless terror awaiting those estranged from God — drive your family neither to careless ease nor to dread, but to sober, patient watchfulness, pressing onward until he who is our life appears, and we appear with him in glory.
