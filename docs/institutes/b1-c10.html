Book 1 · Chapter 10 · USMC modern English

In Scripture, the True God Opposed, Exclusively, to All the Gods of the Heathen

Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. Explanation of the knowledge of God resumed. God as manifested in Scripture, the same as delineated in his works.
  2. 2. The attributes of God as described by Moses, David, and Jeremiah. Explanation of the attributes. Summary. Uses of this knowledge.
  3. 3. Scripture, in directing us to the true God, excludes the gods of the heathen, who, however, in some sense, held the unity of God.
1¶

We observed earlier that the knowledge of God, which in other respects is set out plainly enough in the structure of the world and in all his creatures, is explained more clearly and more intimately by the word. It is fitting now to show that in Scripture the Lord presents himself in the very same character in which we have already seen him portrayed in his works. A full treatment of this subject would take up a great deal of room. Here, however, it will be enough to supply a kind of index, by attending to which the godly reader may come to understand what knowledge of God he ought above all to seek in Scripture, and may be directed in how to go about the search. I am not now turning to that special covenant by which God set the line of Abraham apart from the rest of the nations. For when, by free adoption, he brought those who were his enemies into the rank of sons, even then he was acting in the character of a Redeemer. At present, however, we are occupied with that knowledge which stops short at the creation of the world and does not rise to Christ the Mediator. But although it will soon be necessary to quote certain passages from the New Testament — for proofs are given there both of the power of God the Creator and of his providence in preserving what he first made — I want the reader to keep my present purpose in mind, so that he does not wander from the subject at hand. In short, then, it will be enough for him for now to understand how God, the Creator of heaven and earth, governs the world that was made by him. In every part of Scripture we come upon descriptions of his fatherly kindness and his readiness to do good, and we also come upon instances of severity which show that he is the just punisher of the wicked, especially when they persist in their stubbornness in spite of all his forbearance.

2¶

There are certain passages which hold more vivid descriptions of the divine character, setting it before us as though his very face were visibly portrayed. Moses indeed seems to have meant to gather up briefly whatever may be known of God by man when he said, "The Lord, The Lord God, merciful and gracious, long-suffering, and abundant in goodness and truth, keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, and that will by no means clear the guilty; visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children, and upon the children's children, unto the third and to the fourth generation" (Exodus 34:6, 7). Here we may notice, first, that his eternity and self-existence are declared by his magnificent name repeated twice; and second, that in this listing of his perfections he is described not as he is in himself, but in his relation to us, so that our acknowledgment of him may be a living and actual impression rather than empty, visionary speculation. What is more, the perfections listed here are precisely those which we saw shining in the heavens and on the earth — compassion, goodness, mercy, justice, judgment, and truth. For power and energy are included under the name Jehovah. The prophets use similar terms when they would declare his holy name in full. Rather than heap up a great many passages, it may be enough for now to point to one Psalm (145), in which a summary of the divine perfections is given so carefully that not one of them seems to have been left out. And yet every perfection set down there may also be contemplated in creation; and so, what we find him to be when experience is our guide, that is just what he declares himself to be by his word. In Jeremiah, where God proclaims the character in which he would have us acknowledge him, the description is not so full, yet in substance it is the same. "Let him that glorieth," he says, "glory in this, that he understandeth and knoweth me, that I am the Lord which exercise loving-kindness, judgment, and righteousness, in the earth" (Jeremiah 9:24). Surely the attributes which it is most necessary for us to know are these three: loving-kindness, on which alone our entire safety depends; judgment, which is exercised daily on the wicked and awaits them in a far severer form, even eternal destruction; and righteousness, by which the faithful are preserved and most kindly cherished. The prophet declares that when you understand these, you are richly furnished with the means of glorying in God. Nor is there any omission here of his truth, or his power, or his holiness, or his goodness. For how could this knowledge of his loving-kindness, judgment, and righteousness exist at all, if it were not founded on his inviolable truth? And again, how could anyone believe that he governs the earth with judgment and righteousness, without presupposing his mighty power? And where does his loving-kindness come from, if not from his goodness? In short, if all his ways are loving-kindness, judgment, and righteousness, then his holiness too is plain to see in them. Furthermore, the knowledge of God which is set before us in the Scriptures is meant for the same purpose as the knowledge that shines in creation — namely, that by it we may learn to worship him with perfect integrity of heart and unfeigned obedience, and also to depend entirely on his goodness.

3¶

Here it may be fitting to give a summary of the general teaching. First, then, let the reader observe that Scripture, in order to direct us to the true God, plainly excludes and rejects all the gods of the heathen, because religion had been corrupted everywhere in nearly every age. It is true, indeed, that the name of one God was known and celebrated everywhere. For those who worshiped a whole host of gods, whenever they spoke the genuine language of nature, simply used the name god, as though they had thought one god enough. Justin Martyr noticed this shrewdly, and to the same effect he wrote a treatise entitled On the Monarchy of God, in which he shows by a great variety of evidence that the unity of God is engraved on the hearts of all. Tertullian also proves the same thing from the common forms of speech, in his book On Idolatry. See also, in Augustine, a letter from one Maximus, a grammarian of Medaura, making sport of his own gods and scoffing at the true religion; and see at the same time Augustine's grave and admirable reply (Epistles 42, 43).

Scripture does not introduce a stranger; the God who thunders in creation is the same God who declares himself in his Word — merciful and gracious, long-suffering, abundant in goodness and truth, yet by no means clearing the guilty. Calvin draws our gaze to those few perfections we most need: loving-kindness on which our whole safety rests, judgment that surely awaits the obstinate, and righteousness that preserves the faithful — and against every rival, Scripture flatly excludes the gods of the heathen. We need that exclusivity now as much as any age, for the old idols simply wear new clothes, and a culture that says all paths climb the same mountain is as adulterated as Calvin's pagans who kept God's name on their lips while bowing to a crowd of substitutes. Teach your children the God who is actually there — not a sentiment, not a force, but the Lord who is kind and just at once. Know him as he reveals himself, worship him with integrity of heart, and depend wholly on his goodness.
