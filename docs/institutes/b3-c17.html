1¶

Let us now consider the other arguments which Satan devises through his agents to destroy or weaken the doctrine of justification by faith. I think we have already put it out of our slanderers' power to treat us as if we were enemies of good works, since justification is denied to works not in order that no good works may be done, or that those which are done may be denied to be good, but only so that we may not trust or glory in them or ascribe salvation to them. Our only confidence and boasting, our only anchor of salvation, is that Christ the Son of God is ours, and that in him we are sons of God and heirs of the heavenly kingdom, called to the hope of eternal blessedness not by our own worth but by the kindness of God. But since, as has been said, they attack us with other engines, let us now proceed to demolish these also. First, they fall back on the legal promises which the Lord proclaimed to those who keep the law, and they ask us whether we hold them to be void or effectual. Since it would be absurd and ridiculous to say they are void, they take it for granted that they have some force. From this they infer that we are not justified by faith alone. For the Lord speaks thus: "Wherefore it shall come to pass, if ye hearken to these judgments, and keep and do them, that the Lord thy God shall keep unto thee the covenant and the mercy which he sware unto thy fathers; and he will love thee, and bless thee, and multiply thee" (Deut. 7:12, 13). Again, "If ye thoroughly amend your ways and your doings; if ye thoroughly execute judgment between a man and his neighbor; if ye oppress not the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow, and shed not innocent blood in this place, neither walk after other gods to your hurt: then will I cause you to dwell in this place, in the land that I gave to your fathers, for ever and ever" (Jer. 7:5-7). It would serve no purpose to quote a thousand similar passages, which, since they do not differ in meaning, are to be explained on the same principle. In substance, Moses declares that in the law are set forth "a blessing and a curse," life and death (Deut. 11:26); and from this they argue either that this blessing has become idle and fruitless, or that justification is not by faith alone. We have already shown, See Book 2, chapter 7, sections 2-8, 15; chapter 8, section 3; chapter 11, section 8; Book 3, chapter 19, section 2.

2¶

For this reason, the promises offered in the law would all be void and ineffectual if God in his goodness did not send the gospel to our aid, since the condition on which they depend, and under which alone they are to be performed, namely, the fulfillment of the law, will never be met. Still, the aid which the Lord gives does not consist in leaving part of justification to be obtained by works and supplying the other part out of his forbearance, but in giving us Christ as in himself alone the fulfillment of righteousness. For the apostle, after first stating that he and the other Jews, knowing that "a man is not justified by the works of the law," had "believed in Jesus Christ," gives as the reason not that they might be helped to make up the sum of righteousness by faith in Christ, but that they "might be justified by the faith of Christ, and not by the works of the law" (Gal. 2:16). If believers withdraw from the law to faith in order to find in faith the justification which they see is not in the law, they certainly renounce justification by the law. Therefore, let whoever wishes enlarge on the rewards said to await the one who keeps the law, provided he understands at the same time that, because of our depravity, we receive no benefit from them until we have obtained another righteousness by faith. So David, after mentioning the reward which the Lord has prepared for his servants (Ps. 25, almost throughout), immediately comes down to an acknowledgment of sins, by which the reward is made void. In Psalm 19 also he loudly extols the benefits of the law, but immediately exclaims, "Who can understand his errors? Cleanse thou me from secret faults" (Ps. 19:12). This passage agrees perfectly with the former one, where, after saying, "All the paths of the Lord are mercy and truth unto such as keep his covenant and his testimonies," he adds, "For thy name's sake, O Lord, pardon mine iniquity; for it is great" (Ps. 25:10, 11). So too we ought to acknowledge that the favor of God is offered to us in the law, provided we can deserve it by our works, but that it never actually reaches us through any such deserving.

3¶

What, then? Were the promises given only to vanish away without fruit? I declared just now that this is not my opinion. I do say that their force does not extend to us as long as they look to the merit of works, and therefore that, considered in themselves, they are in a certain sense abolished. Hence the apostle shows that the celebrated promise, "Ye shall therefore keep my statutes and my judgments: which if a man do, he shall live in them" (Lev. 18:5; Ezek. 20:10), will be of no avail if we stop at it, and will profit us not a bit more than if it had never been given, since it is out of reach even for the holiest servants of God, who are all far from fulfilling the law, being surrounded by many infirmities. But when the gospel promises are put in their place, promises which announce the free pardon of sins, the result is not only that our persons are accepted by God, but that his favor is also shown to our works; and this not only in that the Lord is pleased with them, but also because he visits them with the blessings which were due by covenant to the keeping of his law. I admit, therefore, that the works of the faithful are rewarded with the promises which God gave in his law to those who practice righteousness and holiness; but in this reward we should always attend to the cause which wins favor for works. This cause, then, appears to be threefold. First, God, turning his eye away from the works of his servants, which deserve reproach more than praise, embraces them in Christ, and by the intervention of faith alone reconciles them to himself without the aid of works. Second, he honors the works themselves, which are not valued by their own worth, out of his fatherly kindness and forbearance, so far as to give them some degree of value. Third, he extends his pardon to them, not imputing the imperfection by which they are all polluted and for which they would deserve to be counted vices rather than virtues. Hence it appears how much Sophists French, "Les Sophistes de Sorbonne" — the Sophists of the Sorbonne.

4¶

They quote the saying of Peter as given by Luke in the Acts: "Of a truth I perceive that God is no respecter of persons: but in every nation he that feareth him, and worketh righteousness, is accepted with him" (Acts 10:34, 35). And from this they infer, as something that seems to them beyond doubt, that if man wins the favor of God by right conduct, his obtaining salvation is not entirely the gift of God; and further, that when God in his mercy helps the sinner, he is inclined to mercy by works. There is no way of reconciling the passages of Scripture unless you observe that man's acceptance with God is twofold. As man is by nature, God finds nothing in him which can incline him to mercy, except only his wretchedness. If it is clear, then, that man, when God first intervenes for him, is naked and destitute of all good and, on the other hand, loaded and filled with every kind of evil, for what quality, I ask, shall we say that he is worthy of the heavenly kingdom? Where God so clearly displays free mercy, have done with that empty imagination of merit. Another passage in the same book, namely, where Cornelius hears from the lips of an angel, "Thy prayers and thine alms are come up for a memorial before God" (Acts 10:4), is miserably twisted to prove that man is prepared by the pursuit of good works to receive the favor of God. Cornelius, being endowed with true wisdom, that is, with the fear of God, must have been enlightened by the Spirit of wisdom; and being a keeper of righteousness, he must have been sanctified by the same Spirit, righteousness being, as the apostle testifies, one of the most certain fruits of the Spirit (Gal. 5:5). Therefore all those qualities by which he is said to have pleased God he owed to divine grace, so far was he from preparing himself by his own strength to receive it. Indeed, not a syllable of Scripture can be produced which does not agree with the doctrine that the only reason why God receives man into his favor is that he sees him to be in every respect lost when left to himself, lost unless God displays his mercy in delivering him. We now see that in accepting man in this way God looks not to the righteousness of the individual, but simply displays the divine goodness toward miserable sinners who are altogether undeserving of this great mercy.

5¶

But after the Lord has drawn the sinner out of the abyss of perdition and set him apart for himself by adoption, having begotten him again and formed him to newness of life, he embraces him as a new creature and bestows on him the gifts of his Spirit. This is the acceptance to which Peter refers, and by which believers after their calling are approved by God even in respect of works; for the Lord cannot help loving and delighting in the good qualities which he produces in them by his Spirit. But we must always bear in mind that the only way in which men are accepted by God in respect of works is that, whatever good works he has conferred on those whom he admits to favor, he honors with his acceptance by a further act of generosity. For where do their good works come from, except that the Lord, having chosen them as vessels of honor, is pleased to adorn them with true purity? And how are their actions counted good, as if there were no deficiency in them, except that their merciful Father in his forbearance pardons the spots and blemishes that cling to them? In a word, the only meaning of acceptance in this passage is that God accepts and takes pleasure in his children, in whom he sees the traces and features of his own countenance. We have said elsewhere that regeneration is a renewal of the divine image in us. Since God, therefore, whenever he beholds his own face, rightly loves it and holds it in honor, the life of believers, when formed to holiness and justice, is said not without reason to be pleasing to him. But because believers, while surrounded by mortal flesh, are still sinners, and their good works, being only begun, smell of the corruption of the flesh, God cannot be favorable either to their persons or to their works unless he embraces them in Christ more than in themselves. In this way we are to understand the passages in which God declares that he is gracious and merciful to those who practice righteousness. Moses said to the Israelites, "Know therefore that the Lord thy God, he is God, the faithful God, which keepeth covenant and mercy with them that love him and keep his commandments to a thousand generations." These words afterward became a common form of expression among the people. Thus Solomon, in his prayer at the dedication, says, "Lord God of Israel, there is no God like thee, in heaven above, or on earth beneath, who keepest covenant and mercy with thy servants that walk before thee with all their heart" (1 Kings 8:23). The same words are repeated by Nehemiah (Neh. 1:5). As the Lord, in all covenants of mercy, stipulates on his part for integrity and holiness of life in his servants (Deut. 29:18), lest his goodness should be held in derision, or anyone, puffed up with exultation in it, should flatter his own soul while walking in the depravity of his heart, so he is pleased that those whom he admits to communion in the covenant should in this way be kept to their duty. Still, the covenant was free at the first, and so it always remains. Accordingly, while David declares, "according to the cleanness of my hands hath he recompensed me," he does not omit the fountain to which I have referred: "he delivered me, because he delighted in me" (2 Sam. 22:20, 21). In commending the goodness of his cause, he takes nothing away from the free mercy which goes before all the gifts of which it is the source.

6¶

Here, in passing, it is important to observe how these forms of expression differ from legal promises. By legal promises I mean not those which lie scattered through the books of Moses (for many evangelical promises occur there), but those which properly belong to the legal dispensation. All such promises, by whatever name they may be called, are made on the condition that the reward is to be paid when the things commanded are done. But when it is said that the Lord keeps a covenant of mercy with those who love him, the words show what kind of servants those are who have sincerely entered into the covenant, rather than express the reason why the Lord blesses them. The nature of this description is as follows. As the end for which God bestows on us the gift of eternal life is that he may be loved, feared, and worshiped by us, so the end of all the promises of mercy contained in Scripture is rightly that we may revere and serve their author. Therefore, whenever we hear that he does good to those who keep his law, let us remember that the sons of God are described by the duty which they ought constantly to observe, and that his reason for adopting us is that we may revere him as a father. Hence, if we would not deprive ourselves of the privilege of adoption, we must always strive in the direction of our calling. On the other hand, however, let us remember that the completion of the divine mercy does not depend on the works of believers, but that God himself fulfills the promise of salvation to those who answer to their calling by right conduct, because he recognizes the true marks of sons only in those who are directed to good by his Spirit. To this we may refer what is said of the members of the Church: "Lord, who shall abide in thy tabernacle? who shall dwell in thy holy hill? He that walketh uprightly, and worketh righteousness, and speaketh the truth in his heart," and so on (Ps. 15:1, 2). Again, in Isaiah: "Who among us shall dwell with the devouring fire? who among us shall dwell with everlasting burnings? He that walketh righteously," and so on (Isa. 33:14, 15). For what is described is not the strength by which believers can stand before the Lord, but the way in which our most merciful Father brings them into his fellowship and defends and confirms them in it. For as he hates sin and loves righteousness, so he purifies by his Spirit those whom he unites to himself, that he may make them conformable to himself and to his kingdom. Therefore, if it is asked what the first cause is which gives the saints free access to the kingdom of God and a firm and lasting footing in it, the answer is easy: the Lord in his mercy once adopted them and always defends them. But if the question concerns the manner, we must come down to regeneration and its fruits, as listed in the fifteenth Psalm.

7¶

There seems to be much greater difficulty in those passages which call good works by the name of righteousness and declare that man is justified by them. The passages of the former class are very numerous, as when the keeping of the commandments is called justification or righteousness. Of the other class we have an example in the words of Moses: "It shall be our righteousness, if we observe to do all these commandments" (Deut. 6:25). But if you object that this is a legal promise, which, having an impossible condition attached to it, proves nothing, there are other passages to which the same answer cannot be made; for instance, "If the man be poor," "thou shalt deliver him the pledge again when the sun goeth down," "and it shall be righteousness unto thee before the Lord thy God" (Deut. 24:13). Likewise the words of the prophet: "Then stood up Phinehas, and executed judgment: and so the plague was stayed. And that was counted unto him for righteousness unto all generations for evermore" (Ps. 106:30, 31). Accordingly, the Pharisees of our day think they have full scope here for exultation. French, "de crier contre nous en cest endroit" — here to raise an outcry against us.

Now it is not found perfect in any man in the world. Hence we must conclude that no good work in itself merits the name of righteousness.

8¶

I come to the second class (sections 1 and 7, at the beginning), in which the chief difficulty lies. Paul finds nothing stronger to prove justification by faith than what is written of Abraham, that he "believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness" (Rom. 4:3; Gal. 3:6). Therefore, since it is said that the deed of Phinehas "was counted unto him for righteousness" (Ps. 106:30, 31), we may argue that what Paul contends for concerning faith applies also to works. Our opponents, accordingly, as if the point were proved, lay it down that though we are not justified without faith, it is not by faith alone, and that our justification is completed by works. Here I beseech believers, since they know that the true standard of righteousness must be drawn from Scripture alone, to consider with me seriously and reverently how Scripture can fairly be reconciled with that view. Paul, knowing that justification by faith was the refuge of those who lacked righteousness of their own, confidently infers that all who are justified by faith are excluded from the righteousness of works. But since it is clear that this justification is common to all believers, he infers with equal confidence that no man is justified by works; indeed, more than that, that justification is without any help from works. But it is one thing to determine what power works have in themselves, and another to determine what place they are to hold after justification by faith has been established. If a price is to be set on works according to their own worth, we hold that they are unfit to appear in the presence of God; that man, accordingly, has no works in which he can glory before God; and that hence, stripped of all help from works, he is justified by faith alone. Justification, moreover, we define in this way: the sinner, being admitted into communion with Christ, is for his sake reconciled to God; when cleansed by his blood he obtains the forgiveness of sins, and, clothed with righteousness just as if it were his own, stands secure before the judgment seat of heaven. Since forgiveness of sins has first been given, the good works which follow have a value other than their own merit, because whatever is imperfect in them is covered by the perfection of Christ, and all their blemishes and pollutions are wiped away by his purity, so as never to come under the notice of the divine tribunal. The guilt of all transgressions, by which men are prevented from offering God acceptable service, being thus blotted out, and the imperfection which usually stains even good works being buried, the good works done by believers are counted righteous, or, what is the same thing, are imputed for righteousness.

9¶

Now, if anyone should raise this against me as an objection to justification by faith, I would first ask him whether a man is counted righteous for one or two holy works while in all the other acts of his life he is a transgressor of the law. That would indeed be worse than absurd. I would next ask whether he is counted righteous on account of many good works if he is guilty of transgression in some one point. Even this he will not venture to maintain against the authority of the law, which pronounces, "Cursed be he that confirmeth not all the words of this law to do them" (Deut. 27:26). I would go still further and ask whether there is any work which may not justly be convicted of impurity or imperfection. How, then, will it appear to that eye before which even the heavens are not clean, and the angels are charged with folly (Job 4:18)? So he will be forced to confess that no good work exists which is not defiled, both by transgressions on the other side and by its own corruption, so that it cannot be honored as righteousness. But if it is certainly owing to justification by faith that works which are otherwise impure, unclean, defective, and unworthy of the sight, not to say of the love, of God are imputed for righteousness, why do they, by boasting of this imputation, aim at the destruction of that justification without which the boast would be empty? Do they wish to have a viper's brood? French, "Voudrions nous faire une lignee serpentine, que les enfans meutrissent leur mere?" — Would we have a viperish brood, in which the children murder the parent?

10¶

In this way we can admit not only that there is a partial righteousness in works (as our adversaries maintain), but that they are approved by God as if they were absolutely perfect. If we remember on what foundation this rests, every difficulty will be resolved. A work first begins to be acceptable when it is received with pardon. And where does pardon come from, except that God looks on us and all that belongs to us as in Christ? Therefore, just as we ourselves, when grafted into Christ, appear righteous before God because our iniquities are covered with his innocence, so our works are righteous and are counted righteous, because everything otherwise defective in them is buried by the purity of Christ and is not imputed. Thus we may rightly say that not only we ourselves but our works also are justified by faith alone. Now, if that righteousness of works, whatever it may be, depends on faith and free justification and is produced by it, it ought to be included under it and, so to speak, made subordinate to it, as the effect to its cause; so far is it from having any right to be set up to weaken or destroy the doctrine of justification. The whole sentence in the French reads as follows: "Or si cette justice des œvres telle quelle procede de la foy et de la justification gratuite, il ne faut pas qu'on la prenne pour destruire ou obscurcir la grace dont elle depend; mais plustost doit estre enclose en icelle, comme le fruict à arbre." Now, if this righteousness of works, such as it is, proceeds from faith and free justification, it must not be used to destroy or obscure the grace on which it depends, but should rather be included in it, like the fruit in the tree.

11¶

But they say that we have a still more serious matter to deal with in James, who opposes us in express terms. For he asks, "Was not Abraham our father justified by works?" and adds, "You see then how that by works a man is justified, and not by faith only" (James 2:21, 24). What then? Will they draw Paul into a quarrel with James? If they hold James to be a servant of Christ, his statements must be understood so as not to dissent from Christ speaking by the mouth of Paul. By the mouth of Paul the Spirit declares that Abraham obtained justification by faith, not by works; we also teach that all are justified by faith without the works of the law. By James the same Spirit declares that both Abraham's justification and ours consist of works, and not of faith only. It is certain that the Spirit cannot be at variance with himself. Where, then, will the agreement be found? It is enough for our opponents if they can tear up that justification by faith which we regard as fixed by the deepest roots: French, "Il suffit à nos adversaires s'ils peuvent deraciner la justice de foy, laquelle nous voulons estre plantee au profond du cœur." It is enough for our opponents if they can root up justification by faith, which we desire to be planted at the bottom of the heart.

12¶

We have not made good our point until we dispose of the other fallacy, namely, that James places a part of justification in works. If you would make James consistent with the other Scriptures and with himself, you must give the word justify, as he uses it, a different meaning from the one it has in Paul. In Paul's sense we are said to be justified when the remembrance of our unrighteousness is blotted out and we are counted righteous. Had James meant the same thing, it would have been absurd for him to quote the words of Moses, "Abraham believed God," and so on. The context runs thus: "Was not Abraham our father justified by works, when he had offered Isaac his son upon the altar? Seest thou how faith wrought with his works, and by works was faith made perfect? And the Scripture was fulfilled which saith, Abraham believed God, and it was imputed unto him for righteousness." If it is absurd to say that the effect came before its cause, then either Moses falsely declares in that passage that Abraham's faith was imputed for righteousness, or Abraham did not merit righteousness by his obedience in offering up Isaac. Before Ishmael was born, who was a grown youth at the birth of Isaac, Abraham was justified by his faith. How, then, can we say that he obtained justification by an obedience which followed long afterward? Therefore, either James wrongly inverts the proper order (which it would be impious to suppose), or he did not mean to say that Abraham was justified as if he deserved to be counted just. What then? It seems certain that he is speaking of the manifestation, not the imputation, of righteousness, as if he had said: those who are justified by true faith prove their justification by obedience and good works, not by a bare and imaginary semblance of faith. In a word, he is not discussing the manner of justification, but requiring that the justification of believers be active and fruitful. And as Paul contends that men are justified without the help of works, so James will not allow anyone who is devoid of good works to be regarded as justified. Proper attention to the aim of the passage will thus clear up every doubt; for the error of our opponents lies chiefly in this, that they think James is defining the manner of justification, whereas his only purpose is to destroy the depraved security of those who vainly pretended faith as an excuse for their contempt of good works. Therefore, let them twist the words of James as they may, they will never draw more out of them than these two propositions: that an empty phantom of faith does not justify, and that the believer, not content with such an imagination, shows his justification by good works.

13¶

They gain nothing by quoting Paul to the same effect, that "not the hearers of the law are just before God, but the doers of the law shall be justified" (Rom. 2:13). I do not wish to evade the difficulty by the solution of Ambrose, that Paul spoke in this way because faith in Christ is the fulfillment of the law. This I regard as a mere evasion, and one for which there is no need, since the explanation is perfectly plain. The apostle's aim is to put down the absurd confidence of the Jews, who gave out that they alone had knowledge of the law, though at that very time they were its greatest despisers. So that they might not pride themselves so much on a bare acquaintance with the law, he reminds them that when justification is sought by the law, what is required is not the knowledge of it but the keeping of it. We certainly do not mean to dispute that the righteousness of the law consists in works, and not only so, but that justification consists in the dignity and merits of works. But this does not prove that we are justified by works, unless they can produce someone who has fulfilled the law. That Paul meant nothing else is abundantly plain from the context. After charging Jews and Gentiles alike with unrighteousness, he comes down to particulars and says that "as many as have sinned without law shall also perish without law," referring to the Gentiles, and that "as many as have sinned in the law shall be judged by the law," referring to the Jews. Moreover, since they, winking at their transgressions, boasted merely of the law, he adds most fittingly that the law was given with a view to justifying not those who only heard it, but only those who obeyed it; as if he had said: Do you seek righteousness in the law? Do not bring forward the mere hearing of it, which is of little weight in itself, but bring works by which you may show that the law has not been given to you in vain. Since they were all lacking in these, it followed that they had no ground for boasting in the law. Paul's meaning, therefore, rather leads to the opposite argument: the righteousness of the law consists in the perfection of works; but no man can boast of fulfilling the law by works; and therefore there is no righteousness by the law.

14¶

They now turn to those passages in which believers boldly submit their righteousness to the judgment of God and wish to be judged accordingly, as in the following: "Judge me, O Lord, according to my righteousness, and according to mine integrity that is in me." Again, "Hear the right, O Lord"; "Thou hast proved mine heart; thou hast visited me in the night; thou hast tried me, and shalt find nothing." Again, "The Lord rewarded me according to my righteousness; according to the cleanness of my hands hath he recompensed me. For I have kept the ways of the Lord, and have not wickedly departed from my God." "I was also upright before him, and I kept myself from mine iniquity." Again, "Judge me, O Lord; for I have walked in mine integrity"; "I have not sat with vain persons, neither will I go in with dissemblers"; "Gather not my soul with sinners, nor my life with bloody men: in whose hands is mischief, and their right hand is full of bribes. But as for me, I will walk in mine integrity." Ps. 7:9; 17:1; 18:20; 26:1, 9, 10. Further on, see chapter 14, section 18; chapter 20, section 10.

15¶

There are other passages not unlike those quoted above, at which some may still hesitate. Solomon says, "The just man walketh in his integrity" (Prov. 20:7). Again, "In the way of righteousness is life; and in the pathway thereof there is no death" (Prov. 12:28). For this reason Ezekiel says that he who "hath walked in my statutes, and hath kept my judgments, to deal truly; he is just, he shall surely live" (Ezek. 18:9, 21; 23:15). None of these declarations do we deny or obscure. But let one of the sons of Adam come forward with such integrity. If there is none, they must either perish from the presence of God or betake themselves to the refuge of mercy. Still, we do not deny that the integrity of believers, though partial and imperfect, is a step toward immortality. How so, except that the Lord does not test by their merit the works of those whom he has taken into the covenant of grace, but embraces them with fatherly forbearance? By this we do not mean, with the Schoolmen, that works derive their value from accepting grace. For their meaning is that works which are otherwise unfit to obtain salvation on the terms of the law are made fit for that purpose by the divine acceptance. I, on the other hand, maintain that these works, stained both by other transgressions and by their own deficiencies, have no other value than this, that the Lord in his forbearance pardons them; in other words, that the righteousness which he bestows on man is free. Here they bring in, out of season, those passages in which the apostle prays for all perfection for believers: "To the end he may stablish your hearts unblameable in holiness before God, even our Father" (1 Thess. 3:13, and elsewhere). These words were strongly pressed by the Celestines of old in maintaining the perfection of holiness in the present life. To this we think it enough to reply briefly with Augustine, that the goal to which all the godly ought to aspire is to appear in the presence of God without spot or blemish; but since the course of the present life is at best nothing more than progress, we will never reach the goal until we have laid aside the body of sin and been completely united to the Lord. If anyone chooses to give the name of perfection to the saints, I will not quarrel with him obstinately, provided he defines this perfection in the words of Augustine: "When we speak of the perfect virtue of the saints, part of this perfection consists in the recognition of our imperfection, both in truth and in humility" (Augustine, To Boniface, book 3, chapter 7).

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

A Word for 2026 — U.S.M.C. Ministries Calvin reconciles law and gospel without blunting either: the law promises life to the one who keeps it perfectly, and since none of us does, those promises reach us only when Christ has first clothed us in his righteousness — then God, in fatherly indulgence, even rewards the imperfect works of his children, not by their worth but by his mercy. James and Paul do not quarrel; the believer truly justified by faith proves it by obedience. For households and congregations in 2026, this is the steadying word against two errors that still hunt us: the legalist who thinks his striving secures God's favor, and the deceiver who claims a faith that bears no fruit. We teach our children both truths at once — that they cannot earn the Father's smile, and that genuine faith always moves the hands and feet. Walk uprightly, work righteousness, speak truth in the heart — not to climb to God, but because he has already drawn you near, and the Spirit is at work conforming you to himself.
