What It Is to Know God — Tendency of This Knowledge

▶ Listen — read by Alan (modernized for 2026)
Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. The knowledge of God the Creator defined. The substance of this knowledge, and the use to be made of it.
  2. 2. Further illustration of the use, together with a necessary reproof of vain curiosity, and refutation of the Epicureans. The character of God as it appears to the pious mind, contrasted with the absurd views of the Epicureans. Religion defined.
1¶

By the knowledge of God I mean the kind of knowledge by which we not only grasp that some God exists, but also understand what is good for us to know about him and what gives him glory — in short, what it is fitting for us to know concerning him. For strictly speaking, we cannot say that God is truly known where there is no religion or godliness. I am not speaking here of that kind of knowledge by which fallen and condemned people lay hold of God as their Redeemer in Christ the Mediator. I am speaking only of that plain and original knowledge to which the simple course of nature would have led us, had Adam never fallen.

It is true that now, in the present ruin of the human race, no one will see God as a Father, or as the author of salvation, or as favorable in any way, until Christ steps in to make our peace. Even so, it is one thing to recognize that God our Maker upholds us by his power, governs us by his providence, nourishes us by his goodness, and visits us with every kind of blessing — and quite another thing to embrace the grace of reconciliation offered to us in Christ. Since, then, the Lord first appears — both in the creation of the world and in the general teaching of Scripture — simply as a Creator, and afterward as a Redeemer in Christ, a twofold knowledge of him arises. The first of these is what we now have to consider; the second will follow afterward in its place.

Now although our mind cannot conceive of God without offering him some worship, it is still not enough simply to hold that he is the only being whom all ought to worship and adore. We must also be persuaded that he is the fountain of all goodness, and that we must seek everything in him, and in no one but him. What I mean is this: we must be persuaded not only that, just as he once formed the world, so he sustains it by his boundless power, governs it by his wisdom, preserves it by his goodness — and in particular rules the human race with justice and judgment, bears with us in mercy, and shields us by his protection. We must also be persuaded that not a single particle of light, or wisdom, or justice, or power, or uprightness, or genuine truth will be found anywhere that does not flow from him and of which he is not the cause. In this way we must learn to expect and ask all things from him, and to give thanks to him for whatever we receive.

For this sense of God's perfections is the true teacher of godliness, and out of godliness religion springs. By godliness I mean that union of reverence and love toward God which the knowledge of his benefits inspires. For until people feel that they owe everything to God, that they are cherished by his fatherly care, and that he is the author of all their blessings — so that nothing is to be sought apart from him — they will never submit to him in willing obedience. Indeed, unless they ground all their happiness in him, they will never give themselves up to him truly and wholeheartedly.

2¶

Those, then, who in studying this question set out to inquire what the essence of God is only mislead us with cold speculations. It is far more in our interest to know what kind of being God is, and what is consistent with his nature. For what good is it to join the followers of Epicurus in acknowledging some God who has thrown off all care for the world and only takes pleasure in his own ease? What is the use, in short, of knowing a God with whom we have nothing to do? The effect of knowing him ought to be, first, to teach us reverence and fear; and second, to lead us, under his guidance and teaching, to ask every good thing from him, and, when we receive it, to credit it to him.

For how can the thought of God enter your mind without at once giving rise to this thought as well: that since you are his handiwork, you are bound by the very law of creation to submit to his authority — that your life is owed to him, and that whatever you do should have reference to him? If this is so, it certainly follows that your life is badly corrupted if it is not shaped in obedience to him, since his will ought to be the law of our lives. On the other hand, your idea of his nature is not clear unless you acknowledge him to be the origin and fountain of all goodness. From this would arise both trust in him and a longing to cling to him — if only the corruption of the human mind did not pull it away from the right course of inquiry.

For first of all, the godly mind does not invent for itself some God of its own, but looks to the one true God alone. Nor does it make up for him any character it pleases, but is content to take him as he reveals himself, always guarding with the utmost care against overstepping his will and wandering, by reckless presumption, from the right path. The person who knows God in this way sees how he governs all things, and so trusts him as his guardian and protector and casts himself entirely on his faithfulness. Seeing that he is the source of every blessing, the moment he is in any difficulty or feels any need, he turns at once to his protection and relies on his help. Persuaded that he is good and merciful, he rests on him with sure confidence, and does not doubt that the divine kindness will provide a remedy for his every time of need.

Acknowledging him as his Father and his Lord, he counts himself bound to respect his authority in all things, to reverence his majesty, to aim at advancing his glory, and to obey his commands. Regarding him as a just judge, armed with severity to punish wrongdoing, he keeps the judgment seat always before his eyes. Standing in awe of it, he restrains himself and is afraid to provoke God's anger. Yet he is not so terrified by the thought of judgment that he would wish to slip away from God, even if a way of escape lay open before him. On the contrary, he embraces him no less as the avenger of wickedness than as the rewarder of the righteous, because he sees that it belongs equally to God's glory to store up punishment for the one and eternal life for the other. What is more, it is not merely the fear of punishment that holds him back from sin. Loving and revering God as his Father, honoring and obeying him as his Master, he would recoil at the very thought of offending him even if there were no hell at all.

This is what pure and genuine religion is: confidence in God joined with serious fear — a fear that includes within it willing reverence, and that brings with it the kind of true worship that the law prescribes. And we ought to weigh more carefully the fact that all people alike pay God outward homage, but very few truly reverence him. On every side there is an abundance of showy ceremonies, but sincerity of heart is rare.

To know God, Calvin insists, is not to settle whether some deity exists but to apprehend him as the fountain of every good thing, and so to render him reverence and love — that union he calls piety, the root from which true religion springs. A God we merely concede but do not trust, fear, and obey is the idle God of Epicurus, with whom we have nothing to do. Our age is thick with that bargain: a vague higher power kept at a polite distance, ceremony without surrender, ostentation without sincerity of heart. Calvin's warning lands squarely on us — all do homage, few truly reverence. The remedy is not louder worship but a heart persuaded that it owes everything to its Father, and would shrink from offending him though there were no hell to fear. Build a household where God is sought as the source of every blessing, not consulted as a last resort. That is the difference between a Christian home and a religious one.
