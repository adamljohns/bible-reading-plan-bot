What It Is to Know God — Tendency of This Knowledge

Listen — AI narration by Mr. Pemberton (modernized for 2026)
Download the MP3
Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. The knowledge of God the Creator defined. The substance of this knowledge, and the use to be made of it.
  2. 2. Further illustration of the use, together with a necessary reproof of vain curiosity, and refutation of the Epicureans. The character of God as it appears to the pious mind, contrasted with the absurd views of the Epicureans. Religion defined.
1¶

By the knowledge of God I understand that knowledge by which we not only conceive that there is some God, but also grasp what it is in our interest and for his glory to know about him; what, in short, it is fitting for us to know concerning him. For, properly speaking, we cannot say that God is known where there is no religion or piety. I am not now referring to that kind of knowledge by which men, lost in themselves and under the curse, apprehend God as a Redeemer in Christ the Mediator. I speak only of that simple and original knowledge to which the mere order of nature would have led us, had Adam remained upright. For although no one, in the present ruin of the human race, will now perceive God to be either a Father, or the author of salvation, or favorable in any respect, until Christ steps in to make our peace; still it is one thing to perceive that God our Maker upholds us by his power, rules us by his providence, nourishes us by his goodness, and visits us with every kind of blessing, and another thing to embrace the grace of reconciliation offered to us in Christ. Since, then, the Lord first appears, both in the creation of the world and in the general teaching of Scripture, simply as Creator, and afterward as Redeemer in Christ, a twofold knowledge of him arises from this: the first of these is now to be considered, and the second will follow afterward in its order. But although our mind cannot conceive of God without rendering some worship to him, it will not be enough simply to hold that he is the only being whom all ought to worship and adore, unless we are also persuaded that he is the fountain of all goodness, and that we must seek everything in him, and in none but him. My meaning is this: we must be persuaded not only that, as he once formed the world, so he sustains it by his boundless power, governs it by his wisdom, preserves it by his goodness, and in particular rules the human race with justice and judgment, bears with them in mercy, and shields them by his protection; but also that not a particle of light, or wisdom, or justice, or power, or uprightness, or genuine truth will be found anywhere which does not flow from him, and of which he is not the cause. In this way we must learn to expect and ask all things from him, and thankfully to ascribe to him whatever we receive. For this sense of the divine perfections is the proper teacher to instruct us in piety, from which religion springs. By piety I mean that union of reverence and love toward God which the knowledge of his benefits produces. For until men feel that they owe everything to God, that they are cherished by his fatherly care, and that he is the author of all their blessings, so that nothing is to be looked for apart from him, they will never submit to him in willing obedience; indeed, unless they place their entire happiness in him, they will never give up their whole selves to him in truth and sincerity.

2¶

Those, therefore, who in considering this question set out to inquire what the essence of God is, only deceive us with cold speculations, since it is much more in our interest to know what kind of being God is, and what things agree with his nature. For what use is it to join the Epicureans in acknowledging some god who has cast off the care of the world and delights only in his own ease? What good is it, in short, to know a God with whom we have nothing to do? Rather, the effect of our knowledge ought to be, first, to teach us reverence and fear; and, second, to lead us, under its guidance and teaching, to ask every good thing from him, and, when it is received, to ascribe it to him. For how can the idea of God enter your mind without at once giving rise to the thought that, since you are his workmanship, you are bound by the very law of creation to submit to his authority? That your life is owed to him? That whatever you do ought to be done with reference to him? If so, it undoubtedly follows that your life is sadly corrupted if it is not shaped in obedience to him, since his will ought to be the law of our lives. On the other hand, your idea of his nature is not clear unless you acknowledge him to be the origin and fountain of all goodness. From this would arise both confidence in him and a desire to cling to him, were it not that the depravity of the human mind leads it away from the right course of inquiry.

For, first of all, the godly mind does not invent for itself some god of its own making, but looks only to the one true God; nor does it imagine for him whatever character it pleases, but is content to have him as he reveals himself, always guarding with the utmost care against transgressing his will and wandering from the right path in reckless presumption. The man by whom God is known in this way, seeing how he governs all things, trusts him as his guardian and protector, and casts himself entirely upon his faithfulness. Perceiving him to be the source of every blessing, if he is in any distress or feels any need, he at once flees to his protection and relies on his help. Persuaded that he is good and merciful, he rests upon him with sure confidence, and does not doubt that in God's mercy a remedy will be provided for his every time of need. Acknowledging him as his Father and his Lord, he considers himself bound to respect his authority in all things, to revere his majesty, to aim at the advancement of his glory, and to obey his commands. Regarding him as a just judge, armed with severity to punish crimes, he keeps the judgment seat always in view. Standing in awe of it, he restrains himself and fears to provoke his anger. Nevertheless, he is not so terrified by the thought of judgment that he would wish to withdraw himself from it, even if a way of escape lay open before him; indeed, he embraces God no less as the avenger of wickedness than as the rewarder of the righteous, because he sees that it belongs equally to God's glory to store up punishment for the one and eternal life for the other. Besides, it is not the mere fear of punishment that holds him back from sin. Loving and revering God as his Father, honoring and obeying him as his Master, he would shrink from the very thought of offending him even if there were no hell.

Such is pure and genuine religion, namely, confidence in God joined with serious fear: a fear which both includes within it willing reverence and brings with it such lawful worship as is prescribed by the law. And it ought to be considered all the more carefully that all men without distinction pay homage to God, but very few truly revere him. On every side there is an abundance of showy ceremonies, but sincerity of heart is rare.

To know God, Calvin insists, is not to settle whether some deity exists but to apprehend him as the fountain of every good thing, and so to render him reverence and love — that union he calls piety, the root from which true religion springs. A God we merely concede but do not trust, fear, and obey is the idle God of Epicurus, with whom we have nothing to do. Our age is thick with that bargain: a vague higher power kept at a polite distance, ceremony without surrender, ostentation without sincerity of heart. Calvin's warning lands squarely on us — all do homage, few truly reverence. The remedy is not louder worship but a heart persuaded that it owes everything to its Father, and would shrink from offending him though there were no hell to fear. Build a household where God is sought as the source of every blessing, not consulted as a last resort. That is the difference between a Christian home and a religious one.
