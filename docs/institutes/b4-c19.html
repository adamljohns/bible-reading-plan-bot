1¶

The foregoing discussion of the sacraments might have the effect, among the teachable and sober-minded, of keeping them from indulging their curiosity, or from embracing, without the authority of the word, any sacraments other than the two which they know to have been instituted by the Lord. But since the notion of seven sacraments, which is in almost everyone's mouth and has been spread through all the schools and sermons, has taken root by sheer age and is even now seated in men's minds, I thought it worthwhile to give the other five, which are commonly classed with the true and genuine sacraments of the Lord, a separate and closer examination, and, after wiping away every false gloss, to hold them up to the view of the simple, so that they may see what their true nature is and how falsely they have until now been regarded as sacraments. Here at the outset I would declare to all the godly that I do not enter into this dispute about a word out of love of quarreling, but am moved by weighty reasons to attack the abuse of it. I am not unaware that Christians are masters of words, as they are of all things, and that they may therefore adapt words to things as they please, provided a godly meaning is kept, even if there is some impropriety in the manner of expression. All this I grant, though it would be better to make words serve things than things serve words. But with the name of sacrament the case is different. For those who set down seven sacraments at the same time give this definition to them all, namely, that they are visible forms of invisible grace; and at the same time they make them all vehicles of the Holy Spirit, instruments for conferring righteousness, and causes of obtaining grace. Accordingly, the Master of Sentences himself denies that the sacraments of the Mosaic Law are properly called by this name, because they did not exhibit what they figured. Is it tolerable, I ask, that the symbols which the Lord has consecrated with his own lips and distinguished with excellent promises should be regarded as no sacraments, while this honor is meanwhile transferred to rites which men have either devised themselves, or at least observe without any express command from God? Let them therefore either change the definition or refrain from this use of the word, which may afterward give rise to false and absurd opinions. Extreme unction, they say, is a figure and cause of invisible grace, because it is a sacrament. If we cannot possibly admit the inference, we must certainly meet them on the matter of the name, so that we do not accept it on terms that may give occasion to such an error. On the other hand, when they prove it to be a sacrament, they add the reason that it consists of the outward sign and the word. If we find neither command nor promise, what else can we do but protest against it?

2¶

It now appears that we are not quarreling about a word but raising a not unnecessary discussion about the reality. Accordingly, we most firmly maintain what we earlier established by invincible argument: that the power of instituting a sacrament belongs to God alone, since a sacrament ought, by the sure promise of God, to raise up and comfort the consciences of believers, which could never receive this assurance from men. A sacrament ought to be a testimony of God's goodwill toward us. Of this no man or angel can be a witness, since God has no counselor (Isaiah 40:13; Romans 11:34). He alone, with rightful authority, testifies of himself to us by his word. A sacrament is a seal of God's attestation or promise. Now it could not be sealed by bodily things, or by the elements of this world, unless they were confirmed and set apart for this purpose by the will of God. Man, therefore, cannot institute a sacrament, because it is not in man's power to make such divine mysteries lie hidden under things so lowly. The word of God must come first to make a sacrament a sacrament, as Augustine most admirably shows (Homilies on John, 80). Moreover, it is useful to keep some distinction between sacraments and other ceremonies, if we would not fall into many absurdities. The apostles prayed on bended knees; therefore our knees may not be bent without a sacrament (Acts 9:20; 20:36). The disciples are said to have prayed toward the east; so looking toward the east is a sacrament. Paul would have men in every place lift up pure hands (1 Timothy 2:8), and it is repeatedly stated that the saints prayed with uplifted hands; let the stretching out of hands, then, also become a sacrament. In short, let all the gestures of the saints pass into sacraments. I would not greatly object to this, provided it were not tied to those greater harms.

3¶

If they would press us with the authority of the ancient Church, I say that they are using a false gloss. This number seven is nowhere found in the ecclesiastical writers, nor is it clearly known when it crept in. I admit, indeed, that the writers sometimes use the term sacrament freely, but what do they mean by it? All ceremonies, outward rites, and exercises of piety. But when they speak of those signs which ought to be testimonies of God's favor toward us, they are content with the two, Baptism and the Eucharist. Lest anyone suppose that I allege this falsely, I will give a few passages from Augustine here. "First, I want you to hold that the principal point in this discussion is that our Lord Jesus Christ (as he himself says in the gospel) has placed us under a yoke which is easy and a burden which is light. Hence he has bound together the fellowship of his new people by sacraments very few in number, most easy to observe, and most excellent in meaning: such is baptism, consecrated by the name of the Trinity; such is the communion of the body and blood of the Lord; and any other, if it is commended in the canonical Scriptures" (Augustine, To Januarius, Epistle 118). Again: "After the resurrection of our Lord, our Lord himself and the apostolic discipline appointed, instead of many signs, a few, and these most easy to perform, most majestic in meaning, most chaste in practice: such is baptism and the celebration of the body and blood of the Lord" (Augustine, On Christian Doctrine, Book 3, chapter 9). Why does he make no mention here of the sacred number, I mean seven? Is it likely that he would have left it out if it had then been established in the Church, especially since he is otherwise more curious in noting numbers than might be necessary? Indeed, when he mentions Baptism and the Supper and is silent as to the others, Ambrose, On Those Who Are Initiated into the Mysteries, and On the Sacraments.

4¶

It was the ancient custom for the children of Christians, when they had grown up, to appear before the bishop to fulfill the duty required of adults who presented themselves for baptism. These sat among the catechumens until they had been duly instructed in the mysteries of the faith and could make a confession of it before bishop and people. Infants, therefore, who had been initiated by baptism but had not then made a confession of faith to the Church, were brought forward again by their parents toward the end of boyhood or at the beginning of adolescence, and were examined by the bishop according to the form of the Catechism then in common use. In order that this act, which in any case rightly required gravity and holiness, might carry more reverence and dignity, the ceremony of laying on of hands was also used. So the boy, once his faith was approved, was dismissed with a solemn blessing. Ancient writers often mention this custom. Pope Leo says (Epistle 39), "If anyone returns from the heretics, let him not be baptized again, but let what was lacking to him there, namely, the power of the Spirit, be conferred by the laying on of the bishop's hands." Our opponents will cry out here that the name of sacrament is rightly given to that by which the Holy Spirit is conferred. But Leo explains elsewhere what he means by these words (Epistle 77): "Let him who was baptized by heretics not be rebaptized, but be confirmed by the laying on of hands with the invocation of the Holy Spirit, because he received only the form of baptism without sanctification." Jerome also mentions it (Against the Luciferians). Now, though I do not deny that Jerome is somewhat deluded when he says that the practice is apostolic, he is nevertheless very far from the follies of these men. And he softens the expression when he adds that this blessing is given to bishops only, more in honor of the priesthood than from any necessity of law. This laying on of hands, which is done simply by way of blessing, I commend, and I would like to see it restored to its pure use today.

5¶

A later age, having almost blotted out the reality, introduced a kind of fictitious confirmation as a divine sacrament. They imagined that the power of confirmation consisted in conferring the Holy Spirit, for an increase of grace, on one who had been prepared for righteousness in baptism, and in strengthening for the conflict those who in baptism were regenerated to life. This confirmation is performed with anointing and the following form of words: "I sign thee with the sign of the holy cross, and confirm thee with the chrism of salvation, in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit." All very fair and venerable. But where is the word of God that promises the presence of the Holy Spirit here? They cannot bring forward one iota. How will they assure us that their chrism is a vehicle of the Holy Spirit? We see oil, that is, a thick and greasy liquid, but nothing more. "Let the word be added to the element," says Augustine, "and it will become a sacrament." Let them produce this word, I say, if they would have us see anything more in the oil than oil. But if they would show themselves to be ministers of the sacraments as they ought, there would be no room for further dispute. The first duty of a minister is to do nothing without a command. Come, then, let them produce some command for this ministry, and I will not add a word. If they have no command, they cannot excuse their sacrilegious audacity. For this reason our Savior questioned the Pharisees about the baptism of John: "Was it from heaven, or of men?" (Matthew 21:25). If they had answered, Of men, he would have had them admit that it was frivolous and vain; if, From heaven, they would have been forced to acknowledge the doctrine of John. Accordingly, so as not to insult John too openly, they did not dare to say that it was of men. Therefore, if confirmation is of men, it is proved to be frivolous and vain; if they would persuade us that it is from heaven, let them prove it.

6¶

They defend themselves, indeed, by the example of the apostles, who, they presume, did nothing rashly. In this they are right, and we would not blame them if they showed themselves to be imitators of the apostles. But what did the apostles do? Luke relates (Acts 8:15, 17) that when the apostles at Jerusalem heard that Samaria had received the word of God, they sent Peter and John there; that Peter and John prayed for the Samaritans that they might receive the Holy Spirit, who had not yet come upon any of them, since they had only been baptized in the name of Jesus; that after prayer they laid their hands on them; and that by this laying on of hands the Samaritans received the Holy Spirit. Luke mentions this laying on of hands repeatedly. I hear what the apostles did: they faithfully carried out their ministry. It pleased the Lord that those visible and wonderful gifts of the Holy Spirit which he then poured out on his people should be administered and distributed by his apostles through the laying on of hands. I do not think there was any deeper mystery under this laying on of hands; rather, I take it that they used this kind of ceremony to signify by the outward act that they commended to God, and as it were offered to him, the one on whom they laid hands. If this ministry which the apostles then performed still remained in the Church, we too would be bound to observe the laying on of hands; but since that gift has ceased to be conferred, to what end is the laying on of hands? Certainly the Holy Spirit is still present with the people of God; without his guidance and direction the Church of God cannot stand. For we have a promise of perpetual duration, by which Christ invites the thirsty to come to him, that they may drink living water (John 7:37). But those miraculous powers and manifest operations which were distributed by the laying on of hands have ceased. They were only for a time. For it was right that the new preaching of the gospel, the new kingdom of Christ, should be marked out and magnified by unusual and unheard-of miracles. When the Lord ceased from these, he did not at once abandon his Church, but signified that the magnificence of his kingdom and the dignity of his word had been sufficiently displayed. In what respect, then, can these stage-players say that they imitate the apostles? The purpose of the laying on of hands was that the evident power of the Holy Spirit might be put forth immediately. This they do not accomplish. Why, then, do they claim for themselves the laying on of hands, which is indeed said to have been used by the apostles, but for an altogether different end?

7¶

The same answer is to be given if anyone were to insist that our Lord's breathing on his disciples (John 20:22) is a sacrament by which the Holy Spirit is conferred. But the Lord did this once for all, and did not also wish us to do it. In the same way the apostles too laid on their hands, in keeping with that time in which it pleased the Lord that the visible gifts of the Spirit should be dispensed in answer to their prayers; not so that posterity might, as those apes do, mimic the empty and useless sign without the reality. But if they prove that they imitate the apostles in the laying on of hands (though in this they bear no resemblance to the apostles, unless it be in displaying some absurd false zeal), French, "en laquelle toutesfois ils n'ont rien semblable a eux, sinon une folle et perverse singerie"; that is, in which, however, they have nothing like them but a foolish and perverse aping.

8¶

But let us look still more closely, and see how many monsters this greasy oil fosters and feeds. These anointers say that the Holy Spirit is given in baptism for righteousness and in confirmation for an increase of grace; that in baptism we are regenerated to life, and in confirmation equipped for the conflict. And accordingly they are not ashamed to deny that baptism can be duly completed without confirmation. How wicked! Are we not, then, buried with Christ by baptism and made partakers of his death, so that we may also share in his resurrection? This fellowship with the life and death of Christ Paul explains as the mortification of our flesh and the quickening of the Spirit, our old man being crucified so that we may walk in newness of life (Romans 6:6). What is it to be equipped for the conflict, if not this? But if they thought it nothing to trample on the word of God, why did they not at least respect the Church, to which they would be thought so obedient in everything? What heavier charge can be brought against their doctrine than the decree of the Council of Melita? The French adds, "du ternps de Sainct Augustin"; that is, of the time of St. Augustine.

9¶

They add, moreover, that all believers ought, after baptism, to receive the Holy Spirit by the laying on of hands in order to become complete Christians, since there can never be a Christian who has not been anointed with chrism by episcopal confirmation. These are their exact words. On Consecration, Distinction 5; Council of Orleans, chapter Ut Jejuni; On Consecration, Distinction 5.

10¶

Lastly, they conclude that this sacred anointing is to be held in greater reverence than baptism, because the former is administered specially by the higher order of priests, while the latter is dispensed in common by any priest at all (Distinction 5, De his vero). What can you say here, except that they are plainly mad to pride themselves this way on their own inventions, while by comparison they carelessly despise the sacred ordinances of God? Sacrilegious mouth! Do you dare to set oil, merely polluted with your foul breath and charmed by your muttered words, against the sacrament of Christ, and compare it with water sanctified by the word of God? But even this was not enough for your wickedness: you must also prefer it. Such are the answers of the holy see, such the oracles of the apostolic tripod. But some of them have begun to moderate this madness, which even in their own judgment had gone too far (Lombard, Sentences, Book 4, Distinction 7, chapter 2). It is to be held in greater reverence, they say, not perhaps because of any greater power and usefulness it confers, but because it is given by more dignified persons and on a more dignified part of the body, the forehead; or because it gives a greater increase of strength, though baptism is more effective for forgiveness. But by their first reason do they not prove themselves Donatists, who judge the worth of the sacrament by the dignity of the minister? Grant, however, that confirmation may be called more dignified because of the dignity of the bishop's hand; still, if anyone asks how this great prerogative was conferred on bishops, what reason can they give but their own whim? The right was exercised only by the apostles, who alone dispensed the Holy Spirit. Are bishops alone apostles? Are they apostles at all? But let us grant this too: why, on the same grounds, do they not maintain that the sacrament of the blood in the Lord's Supper is to be touched only by bishops? Their reason for refusing it to laymen is that it was given by our Lord to the apostles only. If to the apostles only, why not then infer, to bishops only? But in that place they make the apostles simple presbyters, whereas here another fit of giddiness seizes them, and they suddenly elect them bishops. Lastly, Ananias was not an apostle, and yet Paul was sent to him to receive his sight, to be baptized, and to be filled with the Holy Spirit (Acts 9:17). I will add one thing more, though it is piling on: if by divine right this office was peculiar to bishops, why have they dared to transfer it to common presbyters, as we read in one of the Epistles of Gregory? (Distinction 95, chapter Pervenit).

11¶

How frivolous, inept, and stupid is the other reason, that their confirmation is worthier than God's baptism because in confirmation it is the forehead that is smeared with oil, and in baptism the crown of the head. As if baptism were performed with oil and not with water! I call all the godly to witness whether it is not the one aim of these wretches to corrupt the purity of the sacraments with their leaven. I have said elsewhere that what is of God in the sacraments can scarcely be glimpsed amid the crowd of human inventions. If anyone did not believe me then, let him now believe his own teachers. Here, passing over the water and counting it of no value, they set great value on the oil alone in baptism. We maintain against them that in baptism too the forehead is sprinkled with water, compared with which we do not value your oil one straw, whether in baptism or in confirmation. But if anyone alleges that oil sells for more, I answer that by this added value whatever good might otherwise be in it is spoiled; so far is it from being lawful to peddle this most vile imposture by fraud. They betray their impiety by the third reason, when they pretend that a greater increase of strength is conferred in confirmation than in baptism. By the laying on of hands the apostles dispensed the visible gifts of the Spirit. In what respect does the oil of these men prove its fruitfulness? But away with these guides, who cover one sacrilege with many acts of sacrilege. It is a Gordian knot, which it is better to cut than to spend so much labor untying.

12¶

When they see that the word of God and every plausible argument fail them, they pretend, as usual, that the practice is of the highest antiquity and is confirmed by the consent of many ages. Even if this were true, they gain nothing by it. A sacrament is not of earth but of heaven; not of men but of God only. They must prove God to be the author of their confirmation if they would have it regarded as a sacrament. But why thrust antiquity forward, since the ancient writers, whenever they mean to speak precisely, nowhere mention more than two sacraments? If the bulwark of our faith were to be sought from men, we have an impregnable citadel in this: the fictitious sacraments of these men were never recognized as sacraments by the ancient writers. They speak of the laying on of hands, but do they call it a sacrament? Augustine plainly affirms that it is nothing but prayer (On Baptism, Against the Donatists, Book 3, chapter 16). Let them not yelp out here one of their vile distinctions, that the laying on of hands Augustine referred to was not the confirming kind but the healing or reconciling kind. His book is extant and in men's hands; if I twist it to any meaning other than the one Augustine himself wrote, they are welcome not only to heap reproaches on me in their usual way, but to spit on me. He is speaking of those who returned from schism to the unity of the Church. He says that they have no need to be baptized again, for the laying on of hands is enough, so that the Lord may bestow the Holy Spirit on them through the bond of peace. But since it might seem absurd to repeat the laying on of hands any more than baptism, he shows the difference: "What," he asks, "is the laying on of hands but prayer over the man?" That this is his meaning is clear from another passage, where he says, "Because of the bond of love, which is the greatest gift of the Holy Spirit, without which all the other holy qualities a man may possess are of no avail for salvation, the hand is laid on reformed heretics" (Book 5, chapter 23).

13¶

I wish we could keep the custom which, as I have observed, existed in the early Church before this abortive mask of a sacrament appeared. It would not be such a confirmation as they pretend, one that cannot even be named without injury to baptism, but a catechizing in which those in boyhood, or just past it, would give an account of their faith before the Church. And the best method of catechizing would be to draw up a form for this purpose, containing and briefly explaining the substance of nearly all the heads of our religion, in which the whole body of the faithful ought to agree without controversy. A boy of ten would present himself to the Church to make a profession of faith; he would be questioned on each head and give an answer to each. If he did not know some point, or did not understand it well, he would be taught. In this way, while the whole Church looked on as witness, he would profess the one, true, sincere faith with which the body of the faithful with one accord worship one God. If this discipline were in force today, it would certainly rouse the sluggishness of certain parents who carelessly neglect the instruction of their children, as if it were no concern of theirs, but who could not then neglect it without public disgrace. There would be greater agreement in faith among the Christian people, and not so much ignorance and crudeness; some would not be so easily carried away by new and strange dogmas; in short, it would furnish everyone with an orderly arrangement of Christian doctrine.

OF PENITENCE.

14¶

The next place they give to Penitence, of which they speak so confusedly and without method that consciences can draw nothing certain or solid from their doctrine. Elsewhere (Book 3, chapters 3 and 4) we have explained at length, first, what the Scriptures teach about repentance, and secondly, what these men teach about it. All we need to take up now is the grounds for the opinion of it as a sacrament which has long prevailed in the schools and churches. First, however, I will speak briefly of the rite of the early Church, which these men have used as a pretext for establishing their fiction. In the order observed in public repentance, those who had performed the satisfactions imposed on them were reconciled by a formal laying on of hands. This was the symbol of absolution, by which the sinner himself regained his confidence of pardon before God, and the Church was admonished to put away the memory of the offense and receive him kindly into favor. Cyprian often calls this to give peace. In order that the act might carry more weight and esteem with the people, it was appointed that the authority of the bishop should always be brought in. Hence the decree of the second Council of Carthage: "No presbyter may publicly reconcile a penitent at mass"; and another, of the Council of Arausica: "Let those who are departing this life during the time of penitence be admitted to communion without the reconciling laying on of hands; if they recover from the disease, let them stand in the order of penitents, and after they have fulfilled their time, receive the reconciling laying on of hands from the bishop." Again, in the third Council of Carthage: "A presbyter may not reconcile a penitent without the authority of the bishop." The aim of all these enactments was to keep the strictness they wished to be observed in that matter from being lost through excessive laxity. Accordingly they wanted the case to be heard by the bishop, who would probably be more careful in examining it. And yet Cyprian says somewhere that not the bishop only laid on hands, but the whole clergy as well. For he speaks thus: "They do penitence for a proper time; next they come to communion, and receive the right of communion by the laying on of the hands of the bishop and clergy" (Book 3, Epistle 14). Later, in the course of time, the matter came to this, that they used the ceremony in private absolutions also, without public penitence. Hence the distinction in Gratian (Decretum 26, Question 6) between public and private reconciliation. I consider the ancient practice of which Cyprian speaks to have been holy and wholesome for the Church, and I could wish it restored today. The more recent form, though I do not dare to disapprove it, or at least to condemn it strongly, I judge to be less necessary. Be that as it may, we see that the laying on of hands in penitence was a ceremony ordained by men, not by God, and is to be ranked among indifferent things and outward exercises, which indeed are not to be despised, but hold a lower place than those which have been commended to us by the word of the Lord.

15¶

The Romanists and Schoolmen, whose habit it is to corrupt everything by false interpretation, labor anxiously to find a sacrament here; and it is no wonder, for they are looking for a thing where it is not. At best, they leave the matter tangled, undecided, uncertain, confused, and confounded by the variety of opinions. Accordingly they say (Sentences, Book 4, Distinction 22, chapter 3) either that outward penitence is a sacrament, and if so, ought to be regarded as a sign of inward penitence, that is, contrition of heart, which will be the matter of the sacrament; or that both together make a sacrament, not two but one complete sacrament; but that the outward is the sacrament only, the inward both the matter and the sacrament, while the forgiveness of sins is the matter only and not the sacrament. Let those who remember the definition of a sacrament which we gave above test by it what they say is a sacrament, and it will be found that it is not an outward ceremony appointed by God to confirm our faith. But if they allege that my definition is not a law they are bound to obey, let them hear Augustine, whom they pretend to regard as a saint. French, "Auquel ils font semblant de porter une reverence inviolable"; that is, for whom they pretend to have an inviolable respect.

16¶

And (to dispatch these beasts in their own arena) if any sacrament is to be sought here, would it not have been far more plausible to maintain that the priest's absolution is a sacrament than penitence, whether outward or inward? For it could plainly have been said that it is a ceremony to confirm our faith in the forgiveness of sins, and that it has the promise of the keys, as they describe them: "Whatsoever ye shall bind or loose on earth, shall be bound or loosed in heaven." But someone will object that most of those absolved by priests receive nothing of the kind through the absolution, whereas according to their dogma the sacraments of the new dispensation ought to effect what they figure. This is ridiculous. Just as in the eucharist they make out a twofold eating, a sacramental eating common to good and bad alike, and a spiritual eating proper only to the good, why should they not also pretend that absolution is given in two ways? And yet I have never been able to understand what they meant by their dogma. How far it is at odds with the truth of God we showed when we discussed that subject formally. Here I only wish to show that no scruple should keep them from giving the name of sacrament to the priest's absolution. For they might have answered through the mouth of Augustine, Augustine, Questions on the Old Testament; Book 3, On the Baptism of Infants; On Baptism, Against the Donatists, Book 5.

17¶

Lest they grow proud, however, wherever they place the sacrament, I deny that it can rightly be regarded as a sacrament: first, because there is no special promise of God to this effect, which is the only ground of a sacrament. The French adds, "Car, comme nous avoos assez declairé ci dessus, la promesse des clefs n'appartient nullement a faire quelque estat particulier d'absolution, mais seulement à la predication de l'Evangile soit qu'elle soit faite ou a plusieurs, ou a un seul, sans y mettre difference; c'est a dire, que par icelle promesse notre Seigneur ne fonde point une absolution speciale qui soit faite distinctement à un chacun mais celle qui se fait indifferement a tous pecheurs, sans addresse particuliere." That is: For, as we have sufficiently shown above, the promise of the keys does not pertain to establishing any particular form of absolution, but only to the preaching of the Gospel, whether it is addressed to many or to one only, without making any difference; that is to say, by this promise our Lord does not found a special absolution given separately to each, but one given without distinction to all sinners, without particular application.

Sentences, Book 4, Distinction 14, chapter 1. On Penitence, Distinction 1, chapter 2. Augustine, cited in the Decretum, 15, Question 1, chapter Fermissime.

OF EXTREME UNCTION, SO CALLED.

18¶

The third fictitious sacrament is Extreme Unction, which is performed only by a priest and, as they put it, in extremis, with oil consecrated by the bishop, and with this form of words: "By this holy unction, and his most tender mercy, may God forgive you whatever sin you have committed by the eye, the ear, the smell, the touch, the taste" (see Calvin, Epistle on Shunning Unlawful Rites). They pretend that it has two powers: the forgiveness of sins, and relief of bodily disease if that is expedient, or, if not expedient, the salvation of the soul. For they say that the institution was laid down by James, whose words are, "Is any sick among you? let him send for the elders of the Church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord; and the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up: and if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him" (James 5:14). The same account is to be given here of this anointing as we gave a little earlier of the laying on of hands; in other words, it is mere hypocritical play-acting by which, without reason or result, they would resemble the apostles. Mark relates that the apostles on their first mission, in keeping with the command they had received from the Lord, raised the dead, cast out devils, cleansed lepers, and healed the sick, and in healing they used oil. He says they "anointed with oil many that were sick, and healed them" (Mark 6:13). It was to this that James referred when he directed that the presbyters of the Church be called to anoint the sick. That no deeper mystery lay under this ceremony will easily be seen by those who consider how much freedom both our Lord and his apostles used in such outward things. John 9:6; Matthew 9:29; Luke 18:42; Acts 3:6; 5:16; 19:12.

19¶

And what better reason have they for making a sacrament of this anointing than of any of the other symbols mentioned in Scripture? Why do they not consecrate some pool of Siloam into which the sick may plunge at certain seasons? That, they say, would be done in vain. Certainly not more in vain than the anointing. Why do they not lie down on the dead, since Paul, in raising the dead youth, lay upon him? Why is clay made of dust and spittle not a sacrament? The other cases, they say, were special, but this one is commanded by James. That is, James spoke in keeping with the time when the Church still enjoyed this blessing from God. They affirm, indeed, that there is still the same power in their anointing, but our experience is otherwise. Let no one now wonder that they have so confidently deceived souls which they knew to be dull and blind because deprived of the word of God, that is, of his light and life, since they do not blush to try to deceive the bodily senses of those who are alive and have all their senses about them. They make themselves ridiculous, therefore, by pretending that they are endowed with the gift of healing. The Lord is certainly present with his people in every age, and heals their sicknesses as often as there is need, no less than in former times; and yet he does not put forth those manifest powers, nor dispense miracles by the hands of apostles, because that gift was temporary, and, owing in some measure to the ingratitude of men, it soon ceased.

20¶

Therefore, just as the apostles, not without reason, openly declared by the symbol of oil that the gift of healing committed to them was not their own but the power of the Holy Spirit, so, on the other hand, these men insult the Holy Spirit by making his power consist in a filthy oil of no efficacy. It is just as if someone were to say that all oil is the power of the Holy Spirit because it is called by that name in Scripture, and that every dove is the Holy Spirit because he appeared in that form. Let them see to this. It is enough for us that we perceive with absolute certainty that their anointing is no sacrament, since it is neither a ceremony appointed by God nor has any promise. For when we require these two things in a sacrament, that it be a ceremony appointed by God and that it have a promise from God, we at the same time require that the ceremony be delivered to us and that the promise have reference to us. 134 D134 This distinction in no way calls into question the value of the various sacraments which, in distinct ages, God has been pleased to appoint. On the contrary, it affirms their integrity and value as signs and seals of God's covenant of grace, while emphasizing the fact that they are specifically designed for, applicable within, and valid for particular historical contexts. Calvin draws the obvious inference: some sacraments are not intended for the present age.

21¶

Even if it were granted that this precept of anointing, which has nothing to do with the present age, were perfectly suited to it, they would not even then have advanced their anointing much, with which they have smeared us until now. James would have all the sick anointed; these men smear their oil not on the sick but on half-dead corpses, when life is trembling on the lips, or, as they say, in extremis. If they have in their sacrament a ready cure with which they can either ease the bitterness of disease or at least give some comfort to the soul, they are cruel never to cure in time. James would have the sick man anointed by the elders of the Church. They admit no anointer but a petty priest. When they interpret James's elders to be priests, and allege that the plural is used for honor, the thing is absurd; as if the Church at that time had swarmed with priests, so that they could set out in a long procession carrying a dish of sacred oil. James, in directing simply that the sick be anointed, seems to me to mean no anointing other than with common oil, nor is any other mentioned in Mark's narrative. These men do not deign to use any oil but what has been consecrated by a bishop, that is, warmed with much breath, charmed by much muttering, and saluted nine times on bended knee: Thrice Hail, holy oil! thrice Hail, holy chrism! thrice Hail, holy balsam! From whom did they get these exorcisms? James says that when the sick man has been anointed with oil and prayer has been made over him, if he has committed sins they shall be forgiven him; namely, that his guilt being forgiven, he will obtain a lightening of the punishment. He does not mean that sins are wiped away by oil, but that the prayers with which believers commended their afflicted brother to God would not be in vain. These men are impiously false in saying that sins are forgiven by their sacred, that is, abominable, anointing. See how little they gain even when they are allowed to abuse the passage of James as they please. And to spare us the trouble of a laborious proof, their own annals relieve us of all difficulty; for they relate that Pope Innocent, who presided over the church of Rome in the age of Augustine, decreed that not elders only but all Christians should use oil for anointing in their own need or in that of their friends. The French adds, "Comment accorderont ils cela avec ce qu'ils veulent faire accroire?" That is: How will they reconcile this with what they wish to have believed?

OF ECCLESIASTICAL ORDERS.

22¶

The fourth place in their list is held by the sacrament of Orders, one so prolific that it begets of itself seven lesser sacraments. It is quite ridiculous that, after affirming that there are seven sacraments, when they begin to count they make out thirteen. It cannot be alleged that these are one sacrament because they all tend toward one priesthood and are a kind of steps to the same thing. For since it is certain that the ceremonies in each are different, and they themselves say that the graces are different, no one can doubt that, if their dogmas are admitted, they ought to be called seven sacraments. And why debate it as a doubtful matter, when they themselves plainly and distinctly declare that they are seven? First, then, we shall glance at them in passing and show into how many absurdities they lead us when they would commend their orders to us as sacraments; and secondly, we shall see whether the ceremony which churches use in ordaining ministers ought to be called a sacrament at all. They make seven ecclesiastical orders or degrees, which they distinguish with the title of a sacrament. These are Doorkeepers, Readers, Exorcists, Acolytes, Subdeacons, Deacons, and Priests. And they say there are seven because of the seven kinds of graces of the Holy Spirit with which those promoted to them ought to be endowed. This grace is increased and heaped up more generously upon promotion. The number itself has been consecrated by a perversion of Scripture, because they think they read in Isaiah that there are seven gifts of the Holy Spirit, whereas in truth no more than six are mentioned by Isaiah, who in any case did not mean to include them all in that passage. For in other passages there are mentioned the spirit of life, of sanctification, and of the adoption of sons, just as there we find the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and of the fear of the Lord. Isaiah 11:2; Ezekiel 1:20; Romans 1:4; 8:15.

Isidore, Etymologies, Book 7, cited in the chapter Cleros. Distinctions 21, 33, chapter Lector, and chapter Ostiarius.

23¶

But the crowning folly of all is that in each of these orders they make Christ their colleague. First, they say John 2:15; 10:7; Luke 4:17; Matthew 7:33; John 8:12; 13:5; Matthew 26:26; 27:50.

24¶

Still, so that they may not be able to impose on foolish women, their vanity must be exposed in passing. With great pomp and solemnity they elect their readers, psalmists, doorkeepers, and acolytes to perform services which they actually entrust either to boys or at least to those whom they call laymen. Who for the most part lights the candles, who pours the wine and water from the pitcher, but a boy or some poor layman who earns his bread by it? Do not the same persons chant? Do they not open and shut the doors of the churches? Who ever saw an acolyte or a doorkeeper performing his office in their churches? Indeed, when one who as a boy performed the duty of acolyte is admitted to the order of acolyte, he ceases to be the very thing he begins to be called; so that they seem deliberately to throw away the office when they take up the title. See why they think it necessary to be consecrated by sacraments and to receive the Holy Spirit! It is in order to do nothing. If they plead that this is the fault of the times, because men neglect and abandon their offices, let them at the same time confess that there is in the Church today no use or benefit of these sacred orders which they extol so wonderfully, and that their whole Church is full of anathema, since she allows the candles and flagons, which none are worthy to touch but consecrated acolytes, to be handled by boys and profane persons, and since she hands over to children her chants, which ought to be heard only from consecrated lips. And to what end, pray, do they consecrate exorcists? I hear that the Jews had their exorcists, but I see that they were so called from the exorcisms they practiced (Acts 19:13). Who ever heard of these fictitious exorcists giving one sample of their profession? It is pretended that power has been given them to lay their hands on energumens, catechumens, and demoniacs; but they cannot persuade the demons that they are endowed with such power, not only because the demons do not submit to their orders, but because the demons even command them. You will scarcely find one in ten of them who is not possessed by a wicked spirit. All, then, that they babble about their paltry orders is a mixture of ignorant and stupid falsehoods. Of the ancient acolytes, doorkeepers, and readers we spoke when explaining the government of the Church. All we proposed here was to combat that novel invention of a sevenfold sacrament in ecclesiastical orders, of which we read nowhere except among the silly, raving Sorbonnists and Canonists.

25¶

Let us now attend to the ceremonies they use. First, all whom they enroll in their militia they initiate into the clerical state by a common symbol. They shave them on the top of the head, so that the crown may signify royal honor, because clergy ought to be kings in governing themselves and others. Peter speaks of them thus: "Ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a peculiar people" (1 Peter 2:9). But it was sacrilege for them to claim for themselves alone what is given to the whole Church, and to glory proudly in a title of which they had robbed the faithful. Peter addresses the whole Church; these men twist it to a few shaven crowns, as if it had been said to them alone, Be ye holy; as if they alone had been purchased by the blood of Christ; as if they alone had been made by Christ kings and priests unto God. Then they assign other reasons (Sentences, Book 4, Distinction 24). The top of the head is bared to show that their mind is free, with unveiled face, to behold the glory of God; or to teach them to cut off the vices of the eye and the lip. Or the shaving of the head is the laying aside of temporal things, while the circle of the crown is the remnant of goods kept for their support. Everything is in figure, because, forsooth, the veil of the temple is not yet torn. Accordingly, persuaded that they have performed their part excellently because they have figured such things by their crown, they perform none of them in reality. How long will they delude us with such masks and impostures? The clergy, by shaving off some hair, signify (Sentences, in the place cited) that they have cast away an abundance of temporal goods, that they contemplate the glory of God, that they have put to death the lust of the ear and the eye; yet no class of men is more grasping, more stupid, or more lustful. Why do they not rather display true holiness than give a hypocritical show of it in false and lying signs?

26¶

Moreover, when they say that the clerical crown has its origin and nature from the Nazirites, what else are they saying than that their mysteries are derived from Jewish ceremonies, or rather are sheer Judaism? When they add that Priscilla, Aquila, and Paul himself, after taking a vow, shaved their heads in order to be purified, they betray their gross ignorance. For we nowhere read this of Priscilla, while as to Aquila it is uncertain, since that tonsure may refer just as well to Paul as to Aquila (Acts 18:18). But so as not to leave them in possession of what they claim, namely, that they have an example in Paul, the simpler reader should note that Paul never shaved his head for any sanctification, but only in deference to the weakness of the brethren. Vows of this kind I am accustomed to call vows of love, not of piety; that is, vows not undertaken for the worship of God but only out of regard for the infirmity of the weak, as he himself says that to the Jews he became a Jew (1 Corinthians 9:20). This, therefore, he did, and that only once and for a short time, in order to accommodate himself to the Jews for a little while. When these men imitate the purifications of the Nazirites (Numbers 6:18) to no purpose, what else are they doing than setting up a new Judaism while they wrongly affect to rival the ancient one? In the same spirit the Decretal Epistle was composed which enjoins the clergy, following the apostle, not to let their hair grow but to shave it all around (chapter Prohibitur, Distinction 24); as if the apostle, in showing what is fitting for all men, had been concerned about the round tonsure of the clergy. From this let my readers consider what kind of force or dignity there can be in the mysteries that follow, to which this is the introduction.

27¶

Where the clerical tonsure came from is abundantly clear from Augustine alone (On the Work of Monks, and Retractations). Since in that age no one wore long hair except the effeminate and those who affected an unmanly beauty and elegance, it was thought a bad example to allow the clergy to do so. They were therefore ordered either to cut or to shave their hair, so that they might not have the appearance of effeminate self-indulgence. And so common was the practice that some monks, in order to appear holier than others by a conspicuous difference in dress, let their locks hang loose. The French adds, "Voila comment la tonsure n'estoit point une chose speciale aux clercs, mais estoit en usance quasi à tous." That is: See how the tonsure was not a thing peculiar to the clergy, but was in use, so to speak, among all.

28¶

There remain the three orders which they call major. Of these, what they call the subdiaconate was moved into this class after the crowd of minor orders began to multiply. But since they think they have authority for these from the word of God, they honor them specially with the name of Holy Orders. Let us see how they twist the ordinances of God to their own ends. We begin with the order of presbyter or priest. To these two names they give one meaning, understanding by them those whose part it is, as they say, to offer the sacrifice of Christ's body and blood on the altar, to frame prayers, and to bless the gifts of God. Hence at ordination they receive the paten with the host, as symbols of the power conferred on them of offering sacrifices to appease God, and their hands are anointed, a symbol meant to teach that they have received the power of consecrating. But of the ceremonies later. Of the thing itself I say that, far from having, as they pretend, one particle of support from the word of God, they could not more wickedly corrupt the order which he has appointed. And first, it ought to be held as settled (as we maintained when treating of the Papal Mass) that all who call themselves priests in the sense of offering expiatory victims do injury to Christ. He was appointed and consecrated Priest by the Father with an oath, after the order of Melchizedek, without end and without successor (Psalm 110:4; Hebrews 5:6; 7:3). He once offered a victim of eternal expiation and reconciliation, and now, having entered the sanctuary of heaven, he also intercedes for us. In him we are all priests, but to offer praise and thanksgiving, in short, ourselves and all that is ours, to God. It belonged to him alone to appease God and expiate sins by his offering. When these men claim it for themselves, what follows but that they have an impious and sacrilegious priesthood? It is certainly wicked beyond measure to dare to distinguish it with the title of sacrament. As for the true office of presbyter, which was commended to us by the lips of Christ, I willingly give it that place. For in it there is a ceremony which, first, is taken from the Scriptures, and secondly, is declared by Paul to be not empty or superfluous but a faithful symbol of spiritual grace (1 Timothy 4:14). My reason for not giving a place to the third is that it is not ordinary or common to all believers, but is a special rite for a particular function. But while this honor is ascribed to the Christian ministry, Popish priests may not pride themselves on it. Christ ordered dispensers of his gospel and his sacred mysteries to be ordained, not sacrificers to be installed; and his command was to preach the gospel and feed the flock, not to slay victims. He promised the gift of the Holy Spirit, not for making expiation for sins, but for duly undertaking and maintaining the government of the Church (Matthew 28:19; Mark 16:15; John 21:15).

29¶

With the reality the ceremonies agree perfectly. When our Lord commissioned the apostles to preach the gospel, he breathed on them (John 20:22). By this symbol he represented the gift of the Holy Spirit which he bestowed on them. This breathing these worthy men have kept; and, as if they were bringing the Holy Spirit up from their throat, they mutter over their petty priests, "Receive the Holy Spirit." So they leave nothing that they do not absurdly mimic. I do not say in the manner of actors (who have art and meaning in their gestures), but like apes who imitate at random without discrimination. We follow, they say, the example of the Lord. But the Lord did many things which he did not intend to be examples for us. Our Lord said to his disciples, "Receive the Holy Spirit" (John 20:22). He also said to Lazarus, "Lazarus, come forth" (John 11:43). He said to the paralytic, "Rise, take up thy bed, and walk" (John 5:8). Why do they not say the same to all the dead and the paralyzed? He gave a demonstration of his divine power when, by breathing on the apostles, he filled them with the gift of the Holy Spirit. If they attempt to do the same, they rival God and all but challenge him to a contest. But they are very far from producing the effect, and only mock Christ by that absurd gesture. Such indeed is the effrontery of some that they dare to assert that the Holy Spirit is conferred by them; but what truth there is in this we learn from experience, which cries aloud that all who are consecrated priests turn from horses into asses, and from fools into madmen. And yet it is not on this point that I am contending against them; I am only condemning the ceremony itself, which ought not to be made a precedent, since it was used as the special symbol of a miracle; so far is it from furnishing them with an example to imitate.

30¶

But from whom, pray, did they receive their anointing? They answer that they received it from the sons of Aaron, from whom their order also took its origin (Sentences, Book 4, Distinction 14, chapter 8; and in the Canons, Distinction 21, chapter 1). So they constantly choose to defend themselves with perverse examples rather than confess that any of their rash practices is of their own devising. Meanwhile they do not notice that in professing to be the successors of the sons of Aaron they do injury to the priesthood of Christ, which alone was foreshadowed and typified by all the ancient priesthoods. In him, therefore, they were all summed up and completed; in him they ceased, as we have said repeatedly and as the Epistle to the Hebrews declares without the help of any gloss. But if they take such delight in Mosaic ceremonies, why do they not hurry oxen, calves, and lambs to their sacrifices? They have, indeed, a great part of the ancient tabernacle and of the whole Jewish worship. The only thing lacking to their religion is that they do not sacrifice oxen and calves. Who does not see that this practice of anointing is far more harmful than circumcision, especially when superstition and a Pharisaical opinion of the merit of the work are added to it? The Jews placed their confidence of justification in circumcision; these men look for spiritual gifts in anointing. Therefore, in wishing to be rivals of the Levites, they become apostates from Christ and cast themselves out of the pastoral office.

31¶

It is, if you please, the sacred oil that impresses an indelible character. As if oil could not be washed off with sand and salt, or, if it clings more closely, with soap. But, they say, that character is spiritual. What has oil to do with the soul? Have they forgotten what they quote from Augustine, that if the word is taken away from the water there will be nothing but water, and that it is owing to the word that it is a sacrament? What word can they point to in their oil? Is it that Moses was commanded to anoint the sons of Aaron (Exodus 30:30)? But there he receives commands concerning the tunic, the ephod, the breastplate, the miter, and the crown of holiness with which Aaron was to be adorned, and concerning the tunics, belts, and miters his sons were to wear. He receives commands about sacrificing the calf and burning its fat, about cutting up and burning rams, about sanctifying earrings and vestments with the blood of one of the rams, and countless other observances. Since they have passed over all these, I wonder why the anointing with oil alone pleases them. If they delight in being sprinkled, why are they sprinkled with oil rather than with blood? They are attempting, forsooth, an ingenious device: they are trying by a kind of patchwork to make one religion out of Christianity, Judaism, and Paganism. Their anointing, therefore, is without savor; it lacks salt, that is, the word of God. There remains the laying on of hands, which, though I admit it to be a sacrament in true and lawful ordination, I deny has any such place in this fable, where they neither obey the command of Christ nor look to the end to which the promise ought to lead us. If they would not have the sign denied them, they must fit it to the reality to which it is dedicated.

32¶

As to the order of the diaconate, I would raise no dispute if the office which existed under the apostles and in a purer Church were restored to its integrity. But what resemblance to it do we see in their fictitious deacons? I am not speaking of the men, lest they complain that I unjustly judge their doctrine by the vices of those who profess it; but I contend that those whom their doctrine sets before us find no support in the deacons whom the apostolic Church appointed. They say it belongs to their deacons to assist the priests and to minister in everything done in the sacraments, as in baptism, in the chrism, the paten, and the chalice; to bring the offerings and lay them on the altar; to prepare and set the table of the Lord; to carry the cross; and to announce and read out the gospel and epistle to the people (Sentences, Book 4, Distinction 24, chapter 8; also chapter Perlectis, Distinction 25). Is there one word here about the true office of deacon? Let us now attend to the appointment. The bishop alone lays hands on the deacon being ordained; he places the prayer book and stole on his left shoulder, so that he may understand that he has received the easy yoke of the Lord, in order to subject to the fear of the Lord everything pertaining to the left side; he gives him a text of the gospel to remind him that he is its herald. What have these things to do with deacons? They act just as if someone were to say he was ordaining apostles when he was only appointing persons to light the incense, clean the images, sweep the churches, set traps for mice, and put out dogs. Who would allow this class of men to be called apostles and compared with the very apostles of Christ? After this, let them not pretend that those whom they appoint to mere play-acting are deacons. Indeed, by the very name they declare what the nature of the office is. For they call them Levites and wish to trace their nature and origin to the sons of Levi. As far as I am concerned, they are welcome to it, provided they do not afterward deck themselves in borrowed feathers.

33¶

What use is there in speaking of subdeacons? For whereas in fact they had charge of the poor in ancient times, these men assign them some trifling function, such as carrying the chalice and paten, the pitcher of water, and the napkin to the altar, pouring out water for the hands, and so on. Then by the offerings which they are said to receive and bring in, they mean those which they themselves swallow up, as if these had been devoted to destruction. There is an admirable correspondence between the office and the manner of induction into it, namely, receiving from the bishop the paten and chalice, and from the archdeacon the pitcher of water, the manual, and trumpery of this kind. They call on us to admit that the Holy Spirit is contained in these trifles. What godly man can be brought to grant this? But to finish at once, we may conclude the same of this as of the others, and there is no need to repeat at length what has been explained above. For the modest and teachable (and it is such that I have undertaken to instruct), it will be enough that there is no sacrament of God except where a ceremony is shown joined to a promise, or rather where a promise is seen in a ceremony. Here there is not one syllable of a sure promise, and it is therefore vain to look for a ceremony to confirm the promise. On the other hand, we read of no ceremony appointed by God for the usages they employ, and therefore there can be no sacrament.

OF MARRIAGE.

34¶

Last of all comes marriage, which, while all admit it to be an institution of God, no one ever saw to be a sacrament until the time of Gregory. And would it ever have entered the mind of any sober man? It is a good and holy ordinance of God. So too agriculture, architecture, shoemaking, and barbering are lawful ordinances of God; but they are not sacraments. For in a sacrament what is required is not only that it be a work of God, but that it be an outward ceremony appointed by God to confirm a promise. That there is nothing of the kind in marriage even children can judge. But it is a sign, they say, of a sacred thing, that is, of the spiritual union of Christ with the Church. If by the term sign they understand a symbol set before us by God to assure us in our faith, they wander far from the mark. If they mean a sign merely because it has been used as a comparison, I will show how sharply they reason. Paul says, "One star differeth from another star in glory. So also is the resurrection of the dead" (1 Corinthians 15:41, 42). Here is one sacrament. Christ says, "The kingdom of heaven is like to a grain of mustard-seed" (Matthew 13:31). Here is another sacrament. Again, "The kingdom of heaven is like unto leaven" (Matthew 13:33). Here is a third sacrament. Isaiah says, "He shall feed his flock like a shepherd" (Isaiah 40:11). Here is a fourth sacrament. In another passage he says, "The Lord shall go forth as a mighty man" (Isaiah 42:13). Here is a fifth sacrament. And where will be the end or limit? In this way everything will be a sacrament. All the parables and comparisons in Scripture will be so many sacraments. Indeed, even theft will be a sacrament, since it is written, "The day of the Lord so cometh as a thief in the night" (1 Thessalonians 5:2). Who can bear the ignorant chatter of these sophists? I grant, indeed, that whenever we see a vine, the best thing is to call to mind what our Savior says: "I am the true vine, and my father is the husbandman." "I am the vine, ye are the branches" (John 15:1, 5). And whenever we meet a shepherd with his flock, it is good also to remember, "I am the good shepherd, and know my sheep, and am known of mine" (John 10:14). But anyone who would class such comparisons with sacraments should be sent to the madhouse.

35¶

They bring forward the words of Paul, in which they say the name of sacrament is given to marriage: "He that loveth his wife loveth himself. For no man ever yet hated his own flesh; but nourisheth and cherisheth it, even as the Lord the Church: for we are members of his body, of his flesh, and of his bones. For this cause shall a man leave his father and mother, and shall be joined unto his wife, and they two shall be one flesh. This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the Church" (Ephesians 5:28, 32). To handle Scripture this way is to confound heaven and earth. Paul, in order to show husbands how they ought to love their wives, sets Christ before them as an example. As Christ poured out his tender affection on the Church, which he has espoused to himself, so Paul would have every man feel toward his wife. Then he adds, "He that loveth his wife loveth himself," "even as the Lord the Church." Moreover, to show how Christ loved the Church as himself, and indeed how he made himself one with his bride the Church, he applies to her what Moses relates that Adam said of himself. For when Eve was brought before him, knowing that she had been formed out of his side, he exclaimed, "This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh" (Genesis 2:23). That all this was spiritually fulfilled in Christ and in us, Paul declares when he says that we are members of his body, of his flesh, and of his bones, and so one flesh with him. At length he breaks out into the exclamation, "This is a great mystery"; and lest anyone be misled by the ambiguity, he says that he is not speaking of the union of husband and wife, but of the spiritual marriage of Christ and the Church. And truly it is a great mystery that Christ allowed a rib to be taken from himself, from which we might be formed; that is, that though he was strong, he was pleased to become weak, so that we might be strengthened by his strength, and should no longer live ourselves, but he live in us (Galatians 2:20).

The thing that misled them was the term sacrament. French, "Ills ont eto trompé du mot de Sacrement qui est en la translation commune." That is: They have been misled by the word Sacrament, which is in the common translation.

37¶

And so as not to delude the Church in this matter alone, what a long train of errors, lies, frauds, and wickedness have they attached to one error! So you may say that they sought nothing but a hiding place for abominations when they turned marriage into a sacrament. Once they had obtained this, they took to themselves the hearing of marriage cases: since the thing was spiritual, profane judges were not to meddle with it. Then they enacted laws by which they confirmed their tyranny, laws partly impious toward God and partly full of injustice toward men; such as that marriages contracted between minors without their parents' consent should be valid; that no lawful marriages can be contracted between relatives within the seventh degree, and that such marriages, if contracted, should be dissolved. Moreover, they frame degrees of kinship contrary to the laws of all nations, and even to the polity of Moses, and enact that a husband who has put away an adulteress may not marry again; that spiritual kin cannot be joined in marriage; that marriage cannot be celebrated from Septuagesima to the Octave of Easter, in the three weeks before the nativity of John, or from Advent to Epiphany; and countless other rules which it would be too tedious to mention. We must now get out of their mire, in which our discussion has stuck longer than we would have wished. I think, however, that much has been gained if I have in some measure stripped these asses of their lion's skin.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin sifts the five so-called sacraments — confirmation, penance, extreme unction, orders, marriage — and finds them wanting, for a sacrament is a sign Christ himself instituted and sealed with his promise, and what the Lord did not ordain we have no liberty to elevate. The principle endures beyond the particulars: the Church does not author grace, she receives it; she must not multiply what Christ has given nor burden consciences with inventions. A word for 2026: in a season eager to manufacture new rites and badges of belonging, the believer is freed and steadied by holding only what the Word commands — baptism and the Supper, given by Christ, sufficient and sure. This is not cold minimalism but glad obedience; marriage, for instance, loses nothing of its holiness by not being a sacrament. Teach your family to ask of every practice, however venerable, the simple question Calvin asked: did the Lord institute it? Conscience kept under Scripture is conscience kept free.
