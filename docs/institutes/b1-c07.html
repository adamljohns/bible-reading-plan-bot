The Testimony of the Spirit Necessary to Give Full Authority to Scripture

Sections in this chapter
  1. The authority of Scripture derived not from men, but from the Spirit of God. Objection, That Scripture depends on the decision of the Church. Refutation, I. The truth of God would thus be subjected to the will of man. II. It is insulting to the Holy Spirit. III. It establishes a tyranny in the Church. IV. It forms a mass of errors. V. It subverts conscience. VI. It exposes our faith to the scoffs of the profane.
  2. Another reply to the objection drawn from the words of the Apostle Paul. Solution of the difficulties started by opponents. A second objection refuted.
  3. A third objection founded on a sentiment of Augustine considered.
  4. Conclusion, That the authority of Scripture is founded on its being spoken by God. This confirmed by the conscience of the godly, and the consent of all men of the least candour. A fourth objection common in the mouths of the profane. Refutation.
  5. Last and necessary conclusion, That the authority of Scripture is sealed on the hearts of believers by the testimony of the Holy Spirit. The certainty of this testimony. Confirmation of it from a passage of Isaiah, and the experience of believers. Also, from another passage of Isaiah.
1¶

Before going any farther, it seems right to say something about the authority of Scripture, so that our minds may not only be prepared to receive it with reverence, but may also be stripped of every doubt.

Once what claims to be the Word of God is acknowledged to be exactly that, no one — unless he is empty of common sense and of ordinary human feeling — will be so desperately brazen as to refuse to believe the speaker. But no answers are handed down from heaven day by day, and the Scriptures are the only records in which God has been pleased to entrust his truth to lasting remembrance; so the full authority they ought to carry among the faithful is not acknowledged unless they are believed to have come from heaven as directly as if God himself had been heard speaking them aloud. This subject richly deserves a fuller treatment and a more careful weighing than I can give it here. But my readers will forgive me for attending more to what my plan allows than to what the breadth of this topic demands.

A most destructive error has become widespread — namely, that Scripture carries weight only so far as the Church's vote grants it, as though the eternal and unbreakable truth of God could rest upon the will of men. With great insult to the Holy Spirit, the question is pressed: who can assure us that the Scriptures came from God? Who can guarantee that they have reached our own times safe and unspoiled? Who can persuade us that this book is to be received with reverence and that one struck from the list, if the Church did not settle all these matters with certainty? And so, it is said, both the reverence owed to Scripture and the question of which books are to be admitted into the canon hang upon the Church's ruling. In this way ungodly men, seeking to smuggle in unbridled tyranny under cover of the Church, do not care what absurdities they tangle themselves and others in, so long as they wring from the simple this one admission — that there is nothing the Church cannot do. But what is to become of wretched consciences searching for some solid assurance of eternal life, if all the promises concerning it have no better support than human judgment? Once they are told this, will they stop doubting and trembling? And on the other side, to what jeers of the wicked is our faith exposed — into how deep a suspicion is it brought with everyone — if it is believed to hold only a precarious authority lent to it by the goodwill of men?

2¶

These ravings are splendidly refuted by a single phrase of an apostle. Paul testifies that the Church is "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets" (Ephesians 2:20). If the teaching of the apostles and prophets is the foundation of the Church, then that teaching must have had its certainty before the Church began to exist. Nor is there any room for the quibble that, although the Church takes her first beginning from that teaching, it still remains uncertain which writings are to be ascribed to the apostles and prophets until the Church's judgment steps in. For if the Christian Church was founded at the first upon the writings of the prophets and the preaching of the apostles, then that teaching, wherever it may be found, was certainly recognized and established before the Church existed, since without this the Church herself could never have come into being. The French edition adds: as the foundation goes before the house.

3¶

I am aware that it is customary to quote a sentence from Augustine in which he says that he would not believe the gospel if he were not moved by the authority of the Church (Aug. Cont. Epist. Fundament. c. 5). But it is easy to see from the context how inaccurate and unfair it is to give his words such a meaning. He was arguing against the Manichees, who demanded to be believed without question, claiming that they had the truth while never showing that they had it. Since they pretended to appeal to the gospel in support of Manes, he asks what they would do if they met a man who did not even believe the gospel — what sort of argument they would use to win him over to their opinion. He then adds, "But I would not believe the gospel," and so on; meaning that if he himself were a stranger to the faith, the only thing that could move him to embrace the gospel would be the authority of the Church. And is it any wonder that a man who does not know Christ should pay respect to men?

Augustine, therefore, is not saying here that the faith of the godly rests upon the authority of the Church, nor does he mean that the certainty of the gospel depends upon it. He says only that unbelievers would have no certainty of the gospel, such as might thereby win them to Christ, if they were not influenced by the agreement of the Church. And he clearly shows this to be his meaning when he puts it this way a little earlier: "When I have praised my own creed and ridiculed yours, who do you suppose is to judge between us? Or what more is to be done than to leave those who, after inviting us to certainty, go on to command us to believe what is uncertain, and to follow those who invite us first of all to believe what we are not yet able to grasp, so that, growing stronger through faith itself, we may become able to understand what we believe — no longer men, but God himself inwardly strengthening and enlightening our minds?" These are unquestionably the words of Augustine (August. Cont. Epist. Fundament. cap. 4), and the plain conclusion to draw from them is that this holy man had no intention of hanging our faith in Scripture on the nod or the ruling of the Church — the French edition adds: diverting it from the only foundation which it has in Scripture.

Augustine, in De Ordine, book 2, chapter 9, writes: "We are moved toward learning in two ways, by authority and by reason: in time authority comes first, but in substance reason is prior," and so on. "And so, although the authority of good men may seem more wholesome for the untrained crowd, and reason better suited to the learned, yet because no man becomes skilled except out of being unskilled," and so on, "it comes about that, for all who long to learn things good, great, and hidden, nothing but authority opens the door," and so on. He has many other excellent things to the same effect.

At the same time, I do not deny that he often presses the Manichees with the agreement of the whole Church while arguing in support of the Scriptures, which they rejected. So he rebukes Faustus (lib. 32) for refusing to submit to gospel truth — truth so well grounded, so firmly established, so gloriously renowned, and handed down by sure succession from the days of the apostles. But nowhere does he hint that the authority we grant to the Scriptures depends on the definitions or the devices of men. He simply brings forward the universal judgment of the Church as a point entirely pertinent to the case, and one, moreover, on which he had the advantage over his opponents. Anyone who wishes to see this more fully proved may read his short treatise De Utilitate Credendi (The Advantages of Believing), where it will be found that the only readiness to believe which he recommends is the kind that provides an introduction and makes a fitting start to inquiry; for he declares that we ought not to be satisfied with opinion, but to strive after substantial truth.

4¶

We must hold on to what I said a moment ago — that our faith in a teaching is not established until we are perfectly convinced that God is its author. For this reason the highest proof of Scripture is always drawn from the character of him whose Word it is. The prophets and apostles do not boast of their own sharpness, or of any of the qualities that win a speaker credit, nor do they linger over arguments; they appeal instead to the sacred name of God, so that the whole world may be compelled to submit. The next thing to consider is how it becomes clear — not merely probable, but certain — that the name of God is not being claimed rashly or craftily. If, then, we would take the most effective counsel for our consciences, and keep them from being driven about in a whirl of uncertainty, from wavering, and even from stumbling over the smallest obstacle, our conviction of the truth of Scripture must come from a higher source than human guesses, judgments, or reasonings; namely, from the secret testimony of the Spirit. It is true, of course, that if we choose to proceed by way of argument, it is easy to establish by evidence of various kinds that, if there is a God in heaven, the Law, the Prophecies, and the Gospel came from him. Indeed, even if learned men, men of the greatest talent, take the opposite side and summon up and parade all the powers of their genius in the debate, unless they are shamelessly brazen they will be forced to confess that Scripture shows clear evidence of having been spoken by God, and therefore of containing his heavenly teaching. We shall see a little farther on that the volume of sacred Scripture far surpasses all other writings. Indeed, if we look at it with clear eyes and unbiased judgment, it will at once present itself with a divine majesty that will subdue our presumptuous opposition and compel us to pay it homage.

Still, it is preposterous to try to build up a full faith in Scripture by discussion. True, if I were called to contend with the craftiest despisers of God, I trust that — though I possess neither the highest ability nor the highest eloquence — I should not find it hard to stop their noisy mouths. I could, without much ado, put down the boasts they mutter in corners, if anything were to be gained by refuting their quibbles. But though we may defend the sacred Word of God against those who contradict it, it does not follow that we shall thereby plant in their hearts the certainty which faith requires. Ungodly men suppose that religion rests on nothing more than opinion, and so, in order not to believe foolishly or on slight grounds, they desire and insist that it be proved by reason that Moses and the prophets were divinely inspired. But my answer is that the testimony of the Spirit is superior to reason. For as God alone can properly bear witness to his own words, so those words will not win full credit in the hearts of men until they are sealed by the inward testimony of the Spirit. The same Spirit, then, who spoke through the mouth of the prophets must penetrate our hearts, in order to convince us that they faithfully delivered the message with which they were divinely entrusted. Isaiah expresses this connection most fittingly in these words: "My Spirit that is upon thee, and my words which I have put in thy mouth, shall not depart out of thy mouth, nor out of the mouth of thy seed, nor out of the mouth of thy seed's seed, saith the Lord, from henceforth and for ever" (Isaiah 59:21). Some worthy people are unsettled because, while the wicked grumble at the Word of God and go unpunished, they have no clear proof at hand to silence them. They forget that the Spirit is called a pledge and a seal to confirm the faith of the godly for this very reason: that until he enlightens their minds, they are tossed back and forth on a sea of doubts.

5¶

Let it therefore be held as settled that those who are inwardly taught by the Holy Spirit rest wholly in Scripture; and that Scripture, carrying its own evidence along with it, does not condescend to submit to proofs and arguments, but owes the full conviction with which we ought to receive it to the testimony of the Spirit. The French edition adds: for though in its own majesty it has enough to command reverence, nevertheless it then begins truly to touch us when it is sealed in our hearts by the Holy Spirit.

Such, then, is a conviction that asks for no reasons — a knowledge that agrees with the highest reason, namely a knowledge in which the mind rests more firmly and securely than in any reasons; such, in short, is the conviction which revelation from heaven alone can produce. I am saying nothing more than every believer experiences within himself, though my words fall far short of the reality. I will not dwell on this subject at present, because we shall return to it again; for now, let us simply understand that the only true faith is the faith which the Spirit of God seals upon our hearts. Indeed, the modest and teachable reader will find reason enough in the promise contained in Isaiah, that all the children of the renewed Church "shall be taught of the Lord" (Isaiah 54:13). God bestows this singular privilege on his elect only, whom he separates from the rest of mankind. For what is the beginning of true doctrine but a prompt eagerness to hear the Word of God? And God, through the mouth of Moses, demands this very hearing: "It is not in heavens that thou shouldest say, Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us, that we may hear and do it? But the word is very nigh unto thee, in thy mouth and in thy heart" (Deuteronomy 30:12, 14). Since God has been pleased to reserve the treasure of understanding for his children, it is no wonder that so much ignorance and dullness is seen in people generally. And in that general company I include even those specially chosen, until they are grafted into the body of the Church. Isaiah, moreover, in reminding us that the prophetic teaching would prove unbelievable not only to strangers but also to the Jews, who wished to be thought of as God's household, supplies the reason when he asks, "To whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed?" (Isaiah 53:1). If at any time, then, we are troubled by the small number of those who believe, let us call to mind, on the other hand, that none comprehend the mysteries of God save those to whom it is given.

The certainty that Scripture is God's own Word does not finally rest on the verdict of the Church, on scholars, or on our own reasoning, but on the inward testimony of the Spirit, who seals the truth upon the hearts of believers as surely as if heaven had spoken aloud. This is no small comfort in 2026, when the credibility of everything is auctioned to whichever institution or algorithm shouts loudest, and even the faithful are tempted to make their assurance hang on the latest consensus. Calvin will not have it — the foundation goes before the house; the prophets and apostles came before the Church she rests upon. So when your children ask how you know the Bible is true, do not hand them only arguments, sound as those may be; tell them, and pray they come to know, that the same Spirit who spoke through the prophets must speak within. Cultivate in your home that quiet, teachable hearing of the Word, for none comprehend the mysteries of God save those to whom it is given.
