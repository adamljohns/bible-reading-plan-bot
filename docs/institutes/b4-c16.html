1¶

But since, in our day, certain frenzied spirits have stirred up, and even now keep stirring up, great disturbance in the Church over infant baptism, I cannot avoid adding something here, by way of appendix, to restrain their fury. If anyone thinks I am more long-winded than the subject deserves, let him reflect that, in a matter of the greatest importance, so much is owed to the peace and purity of the Church that we should not be squeamish about anything that helps secure both. Let me add that I will arrange this discussion so that it serves, in no small measure, to shed still more light on the subject of baptism. Now, since we see that the practice we follow of baptizing little children is attacked and disputed by some malicious spirits, as if it had not been appointed by God but newly invented by men, or at least only some years after the days of the apostles, I think it will be very fitting to confirm weak consciences in this matter and to refute the lying objections that such deceivers might raise, in order to overthrow the truth of God in the hearts of simple people who may not be skilled in answering their tricks and quibbles.

2¶

In the first place, then, here is a well-known teaching, one on which all the godly are agreed: that the right way to consider the signs does not lie merely in the outward ceremonies, but depends chiefly on the promise and the spiritual mysteries that the ceremonies themselves are appointed to represent. Therefore, whoever would thoroughly understand the effect of baptism, its purpose and true character, must not stop at the element and the bodily object, but must look forward to the divine promises offered to us in it, and rise to the inner secrets it represents. The one who understands these has reached the solid truth and, so to speak, the whole substance of baptism, and from that will perceive the nature and use of the outward sprinkling. On the other hand, the one who passes them by in contempt, and keeps his thoughts entirely fixed on the visible ceremony, will understand neither the force nor the proper nature of baptism, nor grasp what is meant or what end is served by the use of water. This is confirmed by passages of Scripture too numerous and too clear to need any lengthy discussion here. It remains, then, to inquire into the nature and power of baptism, as shown by the promises given in it. Scripture shows, first, that it points to that cleansing from sin which we obtain by the blood of Christ; and second, to the putting to death of the flesh, which consists in sharing in his death, by which believers are reborn to newness of life, and so to fellowship with Christ. To these general headings may be referred everything the Scriptures teach about baptism, with this addition, that it is also a symbol by which we testify our religion to others.

3¶

Now, since before baptism was instituted the people of God had circumcision in its place, let us see how far these two signs differ and how far they resemble each other. In this way it will become clear what correspondence there is between them. When the Lord commands Abraham to observe circumcision (Genesis 17:10), he first declares that he would be a God to him and to his offspring, adding that in himself there was a complete sufficiency of all things, and that Abraham might count on his hand as a fountain of every blessing. These words include the promise of eternal life, as our Savior explains when he uses it to prove the immortality and resurrection of believers. "God," he says, "is not the God of the dead, but of the living" (Matthew 22:32). For the same reason Paul, showing the Ephesians how great a destruction the Lord had delivered them from, seeing that they had not been admitted to the covenant of circumcision, concludes that at that time they were strangers to the covenant of promise, without God, and without hope (Ephesians 2:12), all these things being included in the covenant. Now, the first access to God, the first entrance to immortal life, is the forgiveness of sins. It follows, then, that this corresponds to the promise of our cleansing in baptism. The Lord afterward covenants with Abraham that he is to walk before him in sincerity and innocence of heart: this applies to mortification, or regeneration. And so that no one should doubt whether circumcision was the sign of mortification, Moses explains it more clearly elsewhere, when he exhorts the people of Israel to circumcise the foreskin of their heart, because the Lord had chosen them for his own people out of all the nations of the earth. Just as the Lord, in choosing Abraham's offspring for his people, commands them to be circumcised, so Moses declares that they are to be circumcised in heart, thus explaining what is signified by that bodily circumcision. Then, so that no one should attempt this in his own strength, he shows that it is the work of divine grace. The prophets so often drive this home that there is no need here to gather the passages that occur everywhere. We have, therefore, a spiritual promise given to the fathers in circumcision, like the one given to us in baptism, since it represented to them both the forgiveness of sins and the mortification of the flesh. Besides, just as we have shown that Christ, in whom both of these reside, is the foundation of baptism, so he must also be the foundation of circumcision. For he is promised to Abraham, and in him all nations are blessed. To seal this grace, the sign of circumcision is added.

Gen 17:10 · Mt 22:32 · Eph 2:12
4¶

There is now no difficulty in seeing where the two signs agree and where they differ. The promise, in which we have shown that the power of the signs consists, is one and the same in both, namely, the promise of the fatherly favor of God, of the forgiveness of sins, and of eternal life. And the thing represented is one and the same, namely, regeneration. The foundation on which the fulfillment of these things depends is one in both. Therefore there is no difference in the inner meaning, by which the whole power and special nature of the sacrament is to be measured. The only difference that remains is in the outward ceremony, which is the smallest part of it, the chief part consisting in the promise and the thing signified. From this we may conclude that everything that applies to circumcision applies also to baptism, always excepting the difference in the visible ceremony. To this comparison we are led by that rule of the apostle in which he directs us to test every interpretation of Scripture by the analogy of faith.

5¶

Now, if we are to inquire whether or not baptism is rightly given to infants, will we not say that the man is trifling, or rather raving, who would stop at the element of water and the outward observance, and not let his mind rise to the spiritual mystery? If reason is heard, it will undoubtedly appear that baptism is properly administered to infants as something due to them. In ancient times the Lord did not bestow circumcision on them without making them partakers of all the things that circumcision signified. He would have deceived his people with mere fraud had he soothed them with deceptive symbols: the very idea is shocking. He plainly declares that the circumcision of the infant will stand in place of a seal of the promise of the covenant. But if the covenant remains firm and fixed, it applies no less to the children of Christians today than to the children of the Jews under the Old Testament. Now, if they share in the thing signified, how can they be denied the sign? If they obtain the reality, how can they be refused the figure? The outward sign is so joined in the sacrament with the word that it cannot be separated from it; but if they could be separated, to which of the two should we attach the greater value? Surely, when we see that the sign is subordinate to the word, we shall say that it ranks below it, and assign it the lower place. Since, then, the word of baptism is meant for infants, why should we deny them the sign, which is an appendage of the word? This one reason, even if no other could be supplied, would be more than enough to refute all who oppose it. The objection that there was a fixed day for circumcision is mere quibbling. We admit that we are not now, like the Jews, bound to certain days; but when the Lord declares that, although he prescribes no day, yet he is pleased that infants should be formally admitted to his covenant, what more do we ask?

6¶

Scripture gives us a still clearer knowledge of the truth. For it is most evident that the covenant which the Lord once made with Abraham applies no less to Christians now than it did in ancient times to the Jewish people, and therefore that word has no less reference to Christians than to Jews. Unless, indeed, we imagine that Christ, by his coming, diminished or cut back the grace of the Father, an idea not free from detestable blasphemy. Therefore, both the children of the Jews, because, being made heirs of that covenant, they were set apart from the heathen, were called a holy seed; and for the same reason the children of Christians, or those who have only one believing parent, are called holy, and, by the apostle's testimony, differ from the impure seed of idolaters. Then, since the Lord, immediately after the covenant was made with Abraham, ordered it to be sealed in infants by an outward sacrament, how can it be said that Christians are not to attest it today, and seal it in their children? Let no one object that the only symbol by which the Lord ordered his covenant to be confirmed was that of circumcision, which was long ago abolished. It is easy to answer that, in keeping with the form of the old dispensation, he appointed circumcision to confirm his covenant; but now that it is abolished, the same reason for confirmation still remains, a reason which we have in common with the Jews. Therefore it is always necessary to consider carefully what is common to both, and where they differed from us. The covenant is common, and the reason for confirming it is common. The manner of confirming it differs only in this, that they had circumcision, in place of which we now have baptism. Otherwise, if the testimony by which the Jews were assured of the salvation of their offspring is taken away from us, the result will be that, by the coming of Christ, the grace of God, which was formerly given to the Jews, is more obscure and less perfectly attested to us. If this cannot be said without grievous insult to Christ, by whom the infinite goodness of the Father has been shed upon the earth and declared to men more brightly and kindly than ever, then it must be confessed that this grace cannot be more limited, and less clearly shown, than under the dim shadows of the law.

7¶

And so our Lord Jesus Christ, to give an example by which the world might learn that he had come to enlarge rather than to limit the grace of the Father, kindly takes the little children in his arms, and rebukes his disciples for trying to prevent them from coming (Matthew 19:13), because they were keeping away from him those to whom the kingdom of heaven belonged, through whom alone there is access to heaven. But it will be asked, what resemblance is there between baptism and our Savior embracing little children? He is not said to have baptized them, but to have received, embraced, and blessed them; and therefore, if we would follow his example, we must give infants the benefit of our prayers, not baptize them. But let us attend to our Savior's act a little more carefully than these men do. For we must not lightly overlook the fact that our Savior, in ordering little children to be brought to him, adds the reason, "of such is the kingdom of heaven." And he afterward shows his goodwill by his action, when he embraces them, and with prayer and blessing commends them to his Father. If it is right that children should be brought to Christ, why should they not be admitted to baptism, the symbol of our communion and fellowship with Christ? If the kingdom of heaven is theirs, why should they be denied the sign by which the door, as it were, is opened to the Church, so that, being admitted into it, they may be enrolled among the heirs of the heavenly kingdom? How unjust we would be to drive away those whom Christ invites to himself, to rob those whom he adorns with his gifts, to shut out those whom he freely admits. But if we insist on discussing the difference between our Savior's act and baptism, how much higher should we esteem baptism, by which we testify that infants are included in the divine covenant, than the taking up, embracing, laying hands on children, and praying over them, acts by which Christ, when present, declares both that they are his, and that they are made holy by him. By the other quibbles with which the objectors try to evade this passage, they only betray their ignorance. They quibble that, because our Savior says "Let the little children come," they must have been several years old, and able to come. But they are called by the Evangelists infants and little children, terms which mean infants still at their mothers' breasts. The word "come" is simply used for "approach." See the quibbles men are forced to resort to when they have hardened themselves against the truth! There is nothing more solid in their claim that the kingdom of heaven is not assigned to children, but to those like children, since the expression is "of such," not "of these themselves." If this is granted, what will be the reason our Savior uses to show that they are not strangers to him on account of their young age? When he orders that little children be allowed to come to him, nothing is plainer than that mere infancy is meant. And so that this should not seem absurd, he adds, "Of such is the kingdom of heaven." But if infants must necessarily be included, the expression "of such" clearly shows that infants themselves, and those like them, are intended.

Mt 19:13
8¶

Everyone must now see that infant baptism, which receives such strong support from Scripture, is by no means of human invention. Nor is there anything plausible in the objection that we nowhere read of even one infant having been baptized by the hands of the apostles. For although this is not expressly told by the Evangelists, yet since infants are not expressly excluded when any baptized household is mentioned (Acts 16:15, 32), what man of sense will argue from this that they were not baptized? If such kinds of arguments were valid, we would have to bar women from the Lord's Supper in the same way, since we do not read that they were ever admitted to it in the days of the apostles. But here we are content with the rule of faith. For when we reflect on the nature of the ordinance of the Lord's Supper, we readily judge who the persons are to whom its use is to be granted. The same we observe in the case of baptism. For, attending to the purpose for which it was instituted, we clearly see that it applies no less to children than to those of greater age, and that therefore they cannot be deprived of it without plainly defrauding the will of its divine Author. The claim they spread among ordinary people, that a long series of years passed after the resurrection of Christ during which infant baptism was unknown, is a shameful falsehood, since there is no writer, however ancient, who does not trace its origin to the days of the apostles.

Acts 16:15 · Acts 16:32
9¶

It remains briefly to point out what benefit comes from this observance, both to believers who bring their children to the church to be baptized, and to the infants themselves, to whom the sacred water is applied, so that no one may despise the ordinance as useless or unnecessary. Yet anyone who would ridicule baptism on this pretext would also be ridiculing the divine ordinance of circumcision; for what can they bring forward to attack the one that may not be turned back against the other? In this way the Lord punishes the arrogance of those who at once condemn whatever their carnal sense cannot grasp. But God supplies us with other weapons to put down their stupidity. His holy institution, from which we feel that our faith draws wonderful comfort, does not deserve to be called unnecessary. For the divine symbol given to the child, as with the impress of a seal, confirms the promise made to the godly parent, and declares that the Lord will be a God not to him only, but to his offspring, visiting with his grace and goodness not him alone, but his descendants also to the thousandth generation. When the infinite goodness of God is thus displayed, it, in the first place, furnishes the most ample material for proclaiming his glory, and fills godly hearts with no ordinary joy, urging them more strongly to love their kind Father, when they see that, for their sake, he extends his care to their descendants. I am not moved by the objection that the promise ought to be enough to confirm the salvation of our children. It has seemed otherwise to God, who, seeing our weakness, has been pleased in this to stoop to it. So then, let those who embrace the promise of mercy to their children consider it their duty to offer them to the Church, to be sealed with the symbol of mercy, and let them stir themselves up to surer confidence on seeing with their own eyes the covenant of the Lord engraved on the bodies of their children. On the other hand, children gain some benefit from their baptism, when, being grafted into the body of the Church, they become an object of greater concern to the other members. Then, when they have grown up, they are by this strongly urged to an earnest desire to serve God, who received them as sons by the formal symbol of adoption before, from their young age, they were able to recognize him as their Father. In short, we ought to stand greatly in awe of the warning that God will take vengeance on everyone who scorns to mark his child with the symbol of the covenant (Genesis 17:15), such contempt being a rejection, and, as it were, a renouncing of the grace offered.

10¶

Let us now examine the arguments by which some raging madmen never stop assailing this holy ordinance of God. First, feeling themselves pressed beyond measure by the resemblance between baptism and circumcision, they insist that there is a wide difference between the two signs, that the one has nothing in common with the other. They maintain that the things meant are different, that the covenant is altogether different, and that the persons included under the name of children are different. When they first set out to prove this, they claim that circumcision was a figure of mortification, not of baptism. This we gladly grant them, for it admirably supports our view, in support of which the only proof we use is that baptism and circumcision are both signs of mortification. From this we conclude that the one was substituted for the other, baptism representing to us the very thing which circumcision signified to the Jews. In asserting a difference of covenant, with what barbarous boldness do they corrupt and destroy Scripture! And that not in one passage only, but in such a way as to leave no passage safe and whole. They paint the Jews as so carnal that they resemble brute beasts more than men, representing the covenant made with them as reaching no further than this temporary life, and the promises given to them as shrinking down into present and bodily blessings. If this teaching is accepted, what remains but that the Jewish nation was loaded for a time with divine kindness (just as pigs are fattened in their pen), only to perish eternally at last? Whenever we cite circumcision and the promises attached to it, they answer that circumcision was a literal sign, and that its promises were carnal.

11¶

Certainly, if circumcision was a literal sign, the same must be said of baptism, since in the second chapter of Colossians the apostle makes the one no more spiritual than the other. For he says that in Christ we "are circumcised with the circumcision made without hands, in putting off the body of the sins of the flesh, by the circumcision of Christ." To explain his meaning, he immediately adds that we are "buried with him in baptism." What do these words mean but simply that the truth and fulfillment of baptism is the truth and fulfillment of circumcision, since they represent one and the same thing? For his aim is to show that baptism is the same thing to Christians that circumcision once was to the Jews. Now, since we have already clearly shown that the promises of both signs, and the mysteries represented by them, agree, we shall not dwell on the point any longer here. I would only remind believers to consider for themselves, without my saying anything, whether that is to be regarded as an earthly and literal sign which has nothing heavenly or spiritual beneath it. But so that they may not blind simple people with their smoke, we shall, in passing, dispose of one objection by which they cloak this most shameless falsehood. It is absolutely certain that the original promises which contained the covenant God made with the Israelites under the old dispensation were spiritual, and had to do with eternal life, and were, of course, in the same way received spiritually by the fathers, so that from them they might hold a sure hope of immortality, and aspire to it with their whole soul. Meanwhile, we are far from denying that he testified his kindness to them by carnal and earthly blessings, though we hold that by these the hope of the spiritual promises was confirmed. In this way, when he promised eternal blessedness to his servant Abraham, he, in order to place a visible token of his favor before his eyes, added the promise of possessing the land of Canaan. In the same way we should understand all the earthly promises given to the Jewish nation, with the spiritual promise always holding first place, as the head to which the others pointed. Having dealt with this subject fully when treating the difference between the old and the new dispensations, I now only touch on it briefly.

12¶

Under the term "children" the difference they note is this: that the children of Abraham, under the old dispensation, were those who came from his offspring, but that the term is now given to those who imitate his faith, and therefore that carnal infancy, which was grafted into the fellowship of the covenant by circumcision, was a figure of the spiritual children of the new covenant, who are reborn by the word of God to immortal life. In these words we do indeed find a small spark of truth, but these giddy spirits err grievously in this: that, seizing on whatever comes first to hand, when they ought to go further, and compare many things together, they obstinately fasten on one single word. So it cannot but happen that they are again and again deceived, because they do not exert themselves to gain a full knowledge of any subject. We certainly admit that the carnal offspring of Abraham for a time held the place of the spiritual offspring, which is grafted into him by faith (Galatians 4:28; Romans 4:12). For we are called his sons, though we have no natural relationship with him. But if they mean, as they plainly show, that the spiritual promise was never made to the carnal offspring of Abraham, they are greatly mistaken. We must, therefore, take a better aim, one to which we are directed by the infallible guidance of Scripture. The Lord therefore promises Abraham that he shall have a seed in whom all the nations of the earth will be blessed, and at the same time assures him that he will be a God both to him and to his seed. All who in faith receive Christ as the author of the blessing are the heirs of this promise, and accordingly are called the children of Abraham.

Scripture cited
Gal 4:28 · Rom 4:12
13¶

Although, after the resurrection of Christ, the boundaries of the kingdom began to be extended far and wide into all nations without distinction, so that, according to Christ's declaration, believers were gathered from all quarters to sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 8:11), still, for many ages before, the Jews had enjoyed this great mercy. And since he had chosen them, passing by all other nations, to be for a time the keepers of his favor, he marked them out as his own special purchased people (Exodus 19:5). As a witness of this kindness, he appointed circumcision, by which symbol the Jews were taught that God watched over their safety, and by which they were raised to the hope of eternal life. For what can ever be lacking to the one whom God has once taken under his protection? Therefore the apostle, to prove that the Gentiles, as well as the Jews, were the children of Abraham, speaks in this way: "Faith was reckoned to Abraham for righteousness. How then was it reckoned? When he was in circumcision, or in uncircumcision? Not in circumcision, but in uncircumcision. And he received the sign of circumcision, a seal of the righteousness of the faith which he had while yet uncircumcised, that he might be the father of all those who believe, though they are not circumcised, that righteousness might be imputed to them also; and the father of circumcision to those who are not of the circumcision only, but who also walk in the steps of that faith of our father Abraham, which he had while yet uncircumcised" (Romans 4:9-12). Do we not see that both are made equal in dignity? For, to the time appointed by the divine decree, he was the father of circumcision. But when, as the apostle writes elsewhere (Ephesians 2:14), the wall of partition that separated the Gentiles from the Jews was broken down, access was given to them also to the kingdom of God, and he became their father, and that without the sign of circumcision, its place being taken by baptism. In saying expressly that Abraham was not the father of those who were of the circumcision only, his purpose was to put down the haughtiness of some who, setting aside all regard for godliness, prided themselves on mere ceremonies. In the same way we may, in our own day, refute the empty pride of those who, in baptism, seek nothing but water.

Mt 8:11 · Exod 19:5 · Rom 4:9-12 · Eph 2:14
14¶

But against this, a passage from the Epistle to the Romans is brought forward, where the apostle says that those who are of the flesh are not the children of Abraham, but that only those who are the children of promise are reckoned as the seed (Rom. 9:7). For he seems to suggest that physical descent from Abraham, which we regard as carrying some weight, counts for nothing. But we must pay careful attention to the subject the apostle is treating there. His aim is to show the Jews that the goodness of God was not bound to the seed of Abraham; indeed, that this descent contributes nothing of itself. To prove it, he brings forward the cases of Ishmael and Esau. These were rejected as though they had been strangers, even though by the flesh they were genuinely Abraham's offspring; and so the blessing rests on Isaac and Jacob. This proves what the apostle goes on to affirm: that salvation depends on the mercy God grants to whomever he pleases, and that the Jews have no ground to boast or pride themselves on the name of the covenant unless they keep the law of the covenant, that is, obey the Word. On the other hand, having torn down their empty confidence in their lineage, he was aware that the covenant made with Abraham's descendants could not rightly prove fruitless. So he declares that physical descent from Abraham should still be given its proper honor, on account of which the Jews were the first and natural heirs of the gospel, except insofar as they were rejected as unworthy because of their ingratitude, and even then rejected in such a way as not to leave their nation utterly empty of the heavenly blessing. For this reason, though they were defiant breakers of the covenant, he calls them holy. Such is the respect he shows to the holy line that God had honored with his sacred covenant, while we, by comparison with them, are called later-born, or even untimely, children of Abraham, and that not by nature but by adoption, just as if a twig were broken off from its own tree and grafted onto another stock. Therefore, so that they might not be robbed of their privilege, it was necessary that the gospel be preached first to them. For they are, as it were, the firstborn in the family of God. The honor owed them on this account must therefore be paid, until they reject the offer and, by their ingratitude, cause it to be transferred to the Gentiles. Yet however great the stubbornness with which they persist in fighting against the gospel, we are not for that reason to despise them. We must remember that, with respect to the promise, the blessing of God still rests among them; and, as the apostle testifies, it will never wholly depart from them, since the gifts and calling of God are without repentance (Rom. 11:29).

Scripture cited
Rom 9:7 · Rom 11:29
15¶

Such is the value of the promise given to Abraham's descendants; this is the scale on which it is to be weighed. And so, although we have no doubt that, in distinguishing the children of God from those who are illegitimate and foreign, God's election reigns freely, we perceive at the same time that he was pleased to embrace the seed of Abraham with his mercy in a special way, and, for the fuller confirmation of it, to seal it with circumcision. The case of the Christian Church is entirely the same. For just as Paul declares there that the Jews are sanctified by their parents, so elsewhere he says that the children of Christians derive their sanctification from their parents. From this it follows that those who are tainted with impurity are rightly separated from the others. Now who can doubt the falsehood of what these men go on to assert: that the infants who were once circumcised merely prefigured the spiritual infancy produced by the regeneration of the Word of God? When the apostle says that Jesus Christ was a minister of the circumcision for the truth of God, to confirm the promises made to the fathers (Rom. 15:8), he is not philosophizing subtly, as if he had said: Since the covenant made with Abraham concerns his seed, Christ, in order to perform and discharge the promise made by the Father, came for the salvation of the Jewish nation. Do you see how he holds that, after the resurrection of Christ, the promise is to be fulfilled to the seed of Abraham, not allegorically but literally, just as the words express it? To the same effect is Peter's declaration to the Jews: 'The promise is to you and to your children' (Acts 2:39); and in the next chapter he calls them the children of the covenant, that is, heirs. Not far different is that other passage of the apostle, quoted above, in which he regards and describes circumcision performed on infants as an attestation of the communion they have with Christ. And indeed, if we listen to the absurdities of these men, what will become of the promise by which the Lord, in the second commandment of his law, pledges to be gracious to the descendants of his servants for a thousand generations? Shall we here resort to allegory? That would be the merest quibble. Shall we say it has been abrogated? In that way we would do away with the law, which Christ came not to destroy but to fulfill, since it works for our everlasting good. Therefore let it be beyond dispute that God is so good and generous to his people that he is pleased, as a mark of his favor, to extend their privileges to the children born to them.

Rom 15:8 · Acts 2:39
16¶

The distinctions these men try to draw between baptism and circumcision are not only ridiculous and without any semblance of reason, but also contradict one another. For when they claim that baptism refers to the first day of the spiritual struggle, and circumcision to the eighth day, when mortification has already been accomplished, they at once forget that distinction and change their tune, presenting circumcision as a figure of the mortification of the flesh, and baptism as a burial, given only to those who are already dead. What are these dizzy contradictions but the dreams of a fevered mind? According to the first view, baptism ought to come before circumcision; according to the second, it should come after it. This is not the first time we have seen the minds of men wander back and forth when they substitute their own dreams for the infallible Word of God. We hold, therefore, that their first distinction is mere fancy. Were we inclined to make an allegory of the eighth day, theirs would not be the right way to do it. It would be far better, with the early Christians, to connect the number eight with the resurrection, which took place on the eighth day, and on which, as we know, newness of life depends; or with the whole course of this present life, throughout which mortification ought to go forward, ending only when life itself ends. Although it seems that God meant to provide for the tenderness of infancy by deferring circumcision to the eighth day, since the wound would have been more dangerous if inflicted immediately after birth. How much more reasonable is the statement of Scripture, that we, when already dead, are buried by baptism (Rom. 6:4); since it plainly states that we are buried into death so that we may thoroughly die, and from then on aim at that mortification. Equally clever is their objection that women should not be baptized if baptism is to be made conformable to circumcision. For if it is most certain that the sanctification of the seed of Israel was attested by the sign of circumcision, it cannot be doubted that it was appointed alike for the sanctification of males and females. And although the rite could be performed only on males, yet the females were, through them, partners and sharers in circumcision. Therefore, setting aside all such quibbling distinctions, let us fix our eyes on the very complete resemblance between baptism and circumcision, as seen in their inner meaning, the promise, the use, and the effect.

Scripture cited
Rom 6:4
17¶

They seem to think they produce their strongest reason for denying baptism to children when they argue that children, because of their young age, are not yet able to understand the mystery sealed there, namely, spiritual regeneration, which does not apply to earliest infancy. From this they conclude that children are to be regarded only as sons of Adam until they reach an age fit for receiving the second birth. But all this stands in direct opposition to the truth of God. For if they are to be counted sons of Adam, they are left in death, since in Adam we can do nothing but die. On the contrary, Christ commands them to be brought to him. Why? Because he is life. Therefore, in order to make them alive, he makes them partners with himself; whereas these men would drive them away from Christ and condemn them to death. For if they claim that infants do not perish when counted as sons of Adam, the error is more than sufficiently refuted by the testimony of Scripture (1 Cor. 15:22). For since it declares that in Adam all die, it follows that no hope of life remains except in Christ. Therefore, in order to become heirs of life, we must share in him. Again, since it is written elsewhere that we are all by nature the children of wrath (Eph. 2:3) and conceived in sin (Ps. 51:5), of which condemnation is the inseparable companion, we must part with our own nature before we can have any access to the kingdom of God. And what could be clearer than the words, 'Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God' (1 Cor. 15:50)? Therefore, let everything that is our own be done away with—which cannot happen without regeneration—and then we shall come to possess the kingdom. In short, if Christ speaks truly when he declares that he is life, we must necessarily be grafted into him, by whom we are delivered from the bondage of death. But how, they ask, are infants regenerated, when they have no knowledge of good or evil? We answer that the work of God, though it is beyond the reach of our understanding, is not for that reason null. Moreover, infants who are to be saved—and that some are saved at this age is certain—must without question have been previously regenerated by the Lord. For if they bring inborn corruption with them from their mother's womb, they must be cleansed before they can be admitted into the kingdom of God, into which nothing that defiles shall enter (Rev. 21:27). If they are born sinners, as David and Paul affirm, they must either remain unaccepted and hated by God, or be justified. And why do we ask for more, when the Judge himself openly declares that 'unless a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God' (John 3:3)? But to silence this kind of objector, God gave, in the case of John the Baptist, whom he sanctified from his mother's womb (Luke 1:15), a proof of what he can do in others. They gain nothing by the quibble they fall back on here, that this was done only once, and therefore it does not at once follow that the Lord always acts this way with infants. That is not how we reason. Our only aim is to show that they unjustly and maliciously confine the power of God within limits within which it cannot be confined. As little weight is due to another evasion of theirs. They allege that, by the usual language of Scripture, 'from the womb' means the same as 'from childhood.' But it is easy to see that the angel had a different meaning when he announced to Zacharias that the child not yet born would be filled with the Holy Spirit. Instead of trying to lay down a law for God, let us hold that he sanctifies whomever he pleases, in the way he sanctified John, since his power is in no way diminished.

Scripture cited
1 Cor 15:22 · Eph 2:3 · Ps 51:5 · 1 Cor 15:50 · Rev 21:27 · John 3:3 · Luke 1:15
18¶

And indeed Christ was sanctified from earliest infancy, so that he might sanctify his elect in himself at any age, without distinction. For just as he, in order to wipe away the guilt of the disobedience that had been committed in our flesh, took on that very flesh, so that in it he might, on our behalf and in our place, render a perfect obedience, so also he was conceived by the Holy Spirit, so that, wholly filled with his holiness in the flesh he had assumed, he might pour it into us. If in Christ we have a perfect pattern of all the graces God bestows on all his children, then in this case we have proof that the age of infancy is not incapable of receiving sanctification. This much, at least, we set down as beyond dispute: that none of the elect is called out of this present life without having first been sanctified and regenerated by the Spirit of God.

19¶

But faith, they say, comes by hearing, and infants have not yet come to the use of hearing, nor can they be fit to know God, being, as Moses declares, without the knowledge of good and evil (Deut. 1:39). But they fail to notice that where the apostle makes hearing the beginning of faith, he is only describing the ordinary way and method the Lord usually employs in calling his people; he is not laying down an unbreakable rule for which no other method can be substituted. He has certainly called many, and given them the true knowledge of himself, by inward means, by the illumination of the Spirit, without the use of preaching. But since they think it very absurd to attribute any knowledge of God to infants, whom Moses describes as having no knowledge of good and evil, let them tell me where the danger lies if it is said that they now receive some part of that grace of which they are to have the full measure shortly after. For if fullness of life consists in the perfect knowledge of God, since some of those whom death carries off in the first moments of infancy pass into eternal life, they are surely admitted to behold the immediate presence of God. Those, therefore, whom the Lord is to illumine with the full brightness of his light—why may he not, if he so pleases, shine upon them even now with some small beam, especially if he does not remove their ignorance before he delivers them from the prison of the flesh? I would not rashly affirm that they are endued with the same faith we experience in ourselves, or that they have any knowledge at all resembling faith—this I would rather leave undecided.

Scripture cited
Deut 1:39
20¶

To gain firmer ground here, they add that baptism is a sacrament of repentance and faith, and since neither of these applies to tender infancy, we must take care not to empty it of its meaning by admitting infants to the communion of baptism. But these darts are aimed more against God than against us; for the fact that circumcision was a sign of repentance is fully established by many passages of Scripture (Jer. 4:4). Thus Paul calls it a seal of the righteousness of faith (Rom. 4:11). Let God, then, be asked why he ordered circumcision to be performed on the bodies of infants. For baptism and circumcision stand here in the same case, and they cannot grant anything to the one without conceding it to the other. If they fall back on their usual evasion—that by the age of infancy spiritual infants were then prefigured—we have already closed off that escape against them. We say, then, that since God gave circumcision, the sign of repentance and faith, to infants, it should not seem absurd that they are now made partakers of baptism, unless men choose to protest against an institution of God. But here, as in all his acts, enough of wisdom and righteousness shines forth to silence the slanders of the ungodly. For although infants, at the moment they were circumcised, did not understand what the sign meant, they were nonetheless truly circumcised for the mortification of their corrupt and polluted nature—a mortification toward which they afterward aspired when grown. In short, the objection is easily answered by the fact that children are baptized for future repentance and faith. Though these are not yet formed in them, the seed of both lies hidden in them by the secret working of the Spirit. This answer at once overturns all the objections twisted against us from the meaning of baptism: for instance, the title by which Paul distinguishes it when he calls it the 'washing of regeneration and renewing' (Tit. 3:5). From this they argue that it is to be given only to those capable of such feelings. But we, on the other hand, may object that neither should circumcision, which is also called regeneration, be conferred on any but the regenerate. In that way, we would condemn a divine institution. Thus, as we have already hinted, all the arguments that aim to undermine circumcision have no force in attacking baptism. Nor can they escape by saying that everything resting on the authority of God is absolutely fixed, even if there were no reason for it, but that this reverence is not owed to infant baptism, nor to other similar things not commended to us by the express Word of God. They are always caught in this dilemma: God's command to circumcise infants was either lawful and beyond reproach, or it deserved blame. If there was nothing improper or absurd in it, then no absurdity can be shown in the practice of infant baptism.

Scripture cited
Jer 4:4 · Rom 4:11 · Tit 3:5
21¶

The charge of absurdity with which they try to brand it, we dispose of in this way. If those on whom the Lord has bestowed his election depart this life before they reach adulthood, after having received the sign of regeneration, he renews them by the incomprehensible power of his Spirit, in the manner he alone sees to be fitting. And should they live to an age when they can be instructed in the meaning of baptism, they will be stirred up by it to greater zeal for renewal, since they will learn that they received the badge of it in earliest infancy, so that they might aspire to it throughout their whole lives. To the same effect are the two passages in which Paul teaches that we are buried with Christ by baptism (Rom. 6:4; Col. 2:12). For by this he does not mean that the one who is to be initiated by baptism must first have been buried with Christ; he is simply declaring the doctrine taught by baptism, and that to those already baptized. So the most senseless person cannot maintain from this passage that burial with Christ ought to precede baptism. In the same way, Moses and the prophets reminded the people of the thing meant by circumcision, which infants nonetheless received. To the same effect Paul says to the Galatians, 'As many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ' (Gal. 3:27). Why so? That they might thereafter live to Christ, to whom previously they had not lived. And although, in adults, the receiving of the sign ought to follow the understanding of its meaning, yet, as will shortly be explained, a different rule must be followed with children. No other conclusion can be drawn from a passage in Peter on which they lean heavily. He says that baptism is 'not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God by the resurrection of Jesus Christ' (1 Pet. 3:21). From this they contend that nothing is left for infant baptism, which becomes mere empty smoke, as being wholly at odds with the meaning of baptism. But the error that misleads them is that they always want the thing signified to come before the sign in order of time.

Scripture cited
Rom 6:4 · Col 2:12 · Gal 3:27 · 1 Pet 3:21
22¶

Everyone, I think, must clearly see that all arguments of this stamp are mere distortions of Scripture. The remaining arguments akin to these we shall examine briefly. They object that baptism is given for the remission of sins. When this is granted, it strongly supports our view; for since we are born sinners, we stand in need of forgiveness and pardon from the very womb. Moreover, since God does not shut out this age from the hope of mercy, but rather gives assurance of it, why should we deprive it of the sign, which is far inferior to the reality? The arrow, therefore, that they aim at us we throw back upon themselves. Infants receive forgiveness of sins; therefore they are not to be deprived of the sign. They bring forward the passage from Ephesians, that Christ gave himself for the Church, 'that he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the Word' (Eph. 5:26). Nothing could be quoted more fitting than this to overturn their error; it gives us an easy proof. If by baptism Christ means to attest the cleansing by which he purifies his Church, it would seem unjust to deny this attestation to infants, who are rightly counted part of the Church, since they are called heirs of the heavenly kingdom. For Paul includes the whole Church when he says it was cleansed by the washing of water. In the same way, from his statement elsewhere that by baptism we are grafted into the body of Christ (1 Cor. 7:13), we infer that infants, whom he counts among Christ's members, are to be baptized, so that they may not be severed from his body. See the violent assault they make, with all their engines of war, on the bulwarks of our faith.

Scripture cited
Eph 5:26 · 1 Cor 7:13
23¶

They now come down to the custom and practice of the apostolic age, alleging that there is no instance of anyone being admitted to baptism without a prior profession of faith and repentance. For when Peter is asked by his hearers, who were pierced to the heart, 'What shall we do?' his counsel is, 'Repent and be baptized, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ, for the remission of sins' (Acts 2:37, 38). Likewise, when Philip was asked by the eunuch to baptize him, he answered, 'If you believe with all your heart, you may.' From this they think they can establish that baptism cannot lawfully be given to anyone without prior faith and repentance. But if we yield to this argument, the first passage, in which there is no mention of faith, would prove that repentance alone is enough; and the second, which makes no mention of repentance, would prove that there is need only of faith. They will object, I suppose, that the one passage helps the other, and that both are therefore to be taken together. I, in my turn, maintain that these two must be compared with other passages that contribute something to the solution of this difficulty. There are many passages of Scripture whose meaning depends on their particular setting. Of this we have an example in the present case. Those to whom these things are said by Peter and Philip are of an age fit to strive after repentance and to receive faith. We firmly insist that such people are not to be baptized unless their conversion and faith can be discerned, at least as far as human judgment can ascertain. But it is perfectly clear that infants must be placed in a different category. For when anyone in former times joined the religious community of Israel, he had to be taught the covenant and instructed in the law of the Lord before he received circumcision, because he was of a different nation—in other words, an alien from the people of Israel, with whom the covenant that circumcision sealed had been made.

Scripture cited
Acts 2:37, 38
24¶

Thus the Lord, when he chose Abraham for himself, did not begin with circumcision, meanwhile concealing what he meant by that sign, but first announced that he intended to make a covenant with him, and then, after Abraham's faith in the promise, made him a partaker of the sacrament. Why does the sacrament come after faith in Abraham, but before all understanding in his son Isaac? It is right that a man who, in adulthood, is admitted to the fellowship of a covenant by one from whom he had until then been estranged, should first learn its conditions; but it is not so with the infant born to him. He, according to the terms of the promise, is included in the promise by hereditary right from his mother's womb. Or, to put the matter more briefly and more clearly: if the children of believers, without the help of understanding, are partakers of the covenant, there is no reason why they should be denied the sign because they are unable to swear to its terms. This, beyond doubt, is the reason the Lord sometimes declares that the children born to the Israelites are begotten and born to him (Ezek. 16:20; 23:37). For he certainly gives the place of sons to the children of those to whose seed he has promised that he will be a Father. But the child descended from unbelieving parents is reckoned an alien to the covenant until he is united to God by faith. So it is not strange that the sign is withheld where the thing signified would be empty and false. In that light, Paul says that the Gentiles, as long as they were sunk in idolatry, were strangers to the covenant (Eph. 2:11). The whole matter may, if I am not mistaken, be set out briefly and clearly in this way: Those who, in adulthood, embrace the faith of Christ, having until then been aliens from the covenant, are not to receive the sign of baptism without prior faith and repentance. These alone can give them access to the fellowship of the covenant; whereas children, who derive their origin from Christians, are received by God as heirs of the covenant immediately at their birth, and are therefore also to be admitted to baptism. To this we must refer the account of the Evangelist, that those who were baptized by John confessed their sins (Matt. 3:6). This example, we hold, ought to be observed in the present day. Were a Turk to offer himself for baptism, we would not at once perform the rite without first receiving a confession satisfactory to the Church.

Scripture cited
Ezek 16:20 · Eph 2:11 · Mt 3:6
25¶

Another passage they bring forward is from the third chapter of John, where our Savior's words seem to them to imply that a present regeneration is required in baptism: 'Unless a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God' (John 3:5). See, they say, how baptism is called regeneration by the lips of our Lord himself; on what pretext, then, and with what consistency, is baptism given to those who, it is perfectly obvious, are not at all capable of regeneration? First, they are in error in imagining that there is any mention of baptism in this passage, simply because the word 'water' is used. After our Savior had explained to Nicodemus the corruption of nature and the necessity of being born again, Nicodemus kept dreaming of a bodily birth, and so our Savior indicates the manner in which God regenerates us, namely, by water and the Spirit—in other words, by the Spirit, who, in watering and cleansing the souls of believers, works in the manner of water. By 'water and the Spirit,' therefore, I simply understand the Spirit, who is water. Nor is this expression new. It agrees perfectly with the one used in the third chapter of Matthew: 'He who comes after me is mightier than I; he shall baptize you with the Holy Spirit and with fire' (Matt. 3:11). Therefore, just as to baptize with the Holy Spirit and with fire is to confer the Holy Spirit, who in regeneration has the office and nature of fire, so to be born again of water and of the Spirit is nothing else than to receive that power of the Spirit which has the same effect on the soul that water has on the body. I know that a different interpretation is given, but I have no doubt that this is the genuine meaning, because our Savior's only purpose was to teach that all who aspire to the kingdom of heaven must lay aside their own disposition. And yet, were we inclined to imitate these men in their way of quibbling, we might easily—after granting what they want—reply to them that baptism comes before faith and repentance, since in this passage our Savior mentions it before the Spirit. This must certainly be understood of spiritual gifts; and if they follow baptism, I have gained all I contend for. But, quibbling aside, the simple interpretation to be adopted is the one I have given: that no one, until renewed by living water, that is, by the Spirit, can enter the kingdom of God.

Scripture cited
John 3:5 · Mt 3:11
26¶

This, moreover, plainly explodes the fiction of those who consign all the unbaptized to eternal death.

27¶

But they boast that they have their strongest stronghold in the very institution of baptism, which they find in the last chapter of Matthew, where Christ, sending his disciples into all the world, commands them to teach and then to baptize. Then, in the last chapter of Mark, it is added, "He that believeth and is baptized shall be saved" (Mark 16:16). What more, they say, could we ask, since the words of Christ plainly declare that teaching must come before baptism, and assign to baptism the place next after faith? Of this very order, they add, our Lord himself gave an example, in choosing not to be baptized until his thirtieth year. But see in how many ways they tangle themselves here, and betray their ignorance. First, they err more than childishly in this, that they trace the original institution of baptism to this passage, when in fact Christ had ordered it to be administered by the apostles from the very beginning of his ministry. There is no ground, then, for arguing that the law and rule of baptism is to be drawn from these two passages, as though they contained the first institution. But suppose we grant them their mistake for the sake of argument: how feeble is this way of reasoning! Were I inclined to evade them, I have not only a place of escape, but a wide field to range in. For when they cling so desperately to the order of the words, insisting that because it is said, "Go, preach and baptize," and again, "Whosoever believes and is baptized," therefore they must preach before baptizing and believe before being baptized, why may not we in turn object that they must baptize before teaching the observance of the things Christ commanded, since it is said, "Baptize, teaching them whatsoever I have commanded you"? We saw the same thing in that other passage where Christ speaks of being born again of water and of the Spirit. For if we read it as they insist on reading it, then baptism must come before spiritual rebirth, because it is mentioned first. Yet Christ teaches that we are to be born again, not of the Spirit and of water, but of water and of the Spirit.

Scripture cited
Mark 16:16
28¶

This argument they cannot break, and in which they place so much confidence, already seems badly shaken. But since we have ample protection in the plainness of the truth, I am unwilling to slip past the point with cheap subtleties. Let them, then, have a solid answer. The command Christ gives here has chiefly to do with the preaching of the gospel; baptism is added to it as a kind of appendage. He speaks of baptism only insofar as the administering of it is subordinate to the work of teaching. For Christ sends his disciples to publish the gospel to all nations of the world, so that by the teaching of salvation they may gather men who were previously lost into his kingdom. But who, or what, are those men? It is certain that the only ones mentioned are those who are able to receive his teaching. He goes on to say that such people, after being taught, were to be baptized, and he adds the promise: whoever believes and is baptized shall be saved. Is there a single syllable about infants in the whole discourse? What, then, is the shape of the argument with which they attack us? Those who are of adult age are to be instructed and brought to faith before being baptized; and therefore, they say, it is unlawful to extend baptism to infants. At the very most, all they can prove from this passage is that the gospel must be preached to those who are capable of hearing it before they are baptized — for the passage speaks only of such. Let them, if they can, raise an obstacle out of this against the baptism of infants.

29¶

But I will make their fallacies plain even to the blind, by a very simple comparison. Suppose someone were to insist that infants are to be denied food, on the pretext that the apostle allows no one to eat except those who work (2 Thessalonians 3:10). Would he not deserve to be hooted down by everyone? Why so? Because what was said of a certain class of men, and of a certain age, he twists and applies to all alike. The cleverness of these men in the present case is no better. What everyone sees was meant for adult age only, they apply to infants, binding them to a rule that was laid down only for those of riper years. As for the example of our Savior, it lends no support to their case. He was not baptized before his thirtieth year. That is indeed true, but the reason is obvious. He then chose to lay the firm foundation of baptism by his preaching — or rather, to confirm the foundation that John had already laid. Therefore, when he was pleased to institute baptism by his own teaching, he sanctified it in his own person, so as to give greater authority to his institution, and he did so at the most fitting time, namely, the beginning of his ministry. In short, all they can prove is that baptism had its origin and beginning together with the preaching of the gospel. But if they are determined to fix on the thirtieth year, why do they not keep to it? Instead, they admit anyone to baptism according to whatever judgment they have formed of his progress. Indeed, even Servetus, one of their own masters, although he stubbornly insisted on this age, had begun to play the prophet in his twenty-first year — as if any man could be allowed to claim for himself the office of a teacher in the Church before he was even a member of the Church.

Scripture cited
2 Thess 3:10
30¶

At last they object that there is no greater reason for admitting infants to baptism than to the Lord's Supper, to which, however, they are never admitted — as though Scripture did not draw a wide distinction between them in every way. In the early Church, the Lord's Supper was indeed often given to infants, as appears from Cyprian and Augustine; but the practice rightly fell out of use. For if we attend to the particular nature of baptism, it is a kind of entrance, an initiation, as it were, into the Church, by which we are numbered among the people of God — a sign of our spiritual rebirth, by which we are born again to be children of God. The Supper, on the other hand, is intended for those of riper years, who, having passed the tender period of infancy, are able to take solid food. This distinction is very clearly marked out in Scripture. For there, as far as baptism is concerned, the Lord makes no selection of age; but he does not admit all to share in the Supper. He restricts it to those who are able to discern the body and blood of the Lord, to examine their own conscience, to proclaim the Lord's death, and to understand its power. Could we want anything plainer than what the apostle says when he gives this exhortation: "Let a man examine himself, and so let him eat of that bread and drink of that cup"? (1 Corinthians 11:28). Examination, then, must come first, and it would be useless to expect this from infants. Again: "He that eateth and drinketh unworthily, eateth and drinketh damnation to himself, not discerning the Lord's body." If they cannot partake worthily without being able to discern rightly the holiness of the Lord's body, why should we hold out poison to our little children instead of life-giving food? Then what is our Lord's command? "Do this in remembrance of me." And what is the inference the apostle draws from it? "As often as ye eat this bread, and drink this cup, ye do show the Lord's death till he come." How, I ask, can we require infants to commemorate an event of which they have no understanding? How require them to "show forth the Lord's death," of whose nature and benefit they have no idea? Nothing of the kind is required by baptism. So there is the greatest difference between the two signs. We observe this also in the corresponding signs under the old dispensation. Circumcision, which, as is well known, answers to our baptism, was meant for infants; but the Passover, in whose place the Supper has come, did not admit every kind of guest without distinction. It was rightly eaten only by those who were old enough to ask its meaning (Exodus 12:26). If these men had even the least particle of soundness left in their minds, would they be this blind to a matter so plain and obvious?

Scripture cited
1 Cor 11:28 · Exod 12:26
31¶

Although I am reluctant to weary the reader with the string of fancies that Servetus — not the least among the Anabaptists, indeed the great glory of that crew — when girding himself for battle, thought, in bringing them forward, to be impressive arguments, it will be worth the trouble to dispose of them briefly. Although I am sorry to heap up so many frivolous daydreams, which may tire the reader, yet since Servetus, taking it upon himself to slander baptism as well, has imagined that he was bringing forward very fine arguments, it is right to beat them down briefly.

32¶

No sound-minded man, I take it, can now doubt how rashly the Church is troubled by those who stir up quarrels and disturbances over the baptism of infants. For it is worth observing what Satan intends by all this craft — namely, to rob us of the singular blessing of confidence and spiritual joy that is to be drawn from it, and so to take away from the glory of God's goodness. For how sweet it is to godly minds to be assured, not only by the word, but even by the witness of their own eyes, that they stand so high in the favor of their heavenly Father that he concerns himself with the welfare of their children! Here we may see how he acts toward us as a most provident parent, who does not cease to care for us even after our death, but takes thought and provides for our children. Ought not our whole heart to be stirred up within us, as David's was (Psalm 48:11), to bless his name for such a display of goodness? Without doubt, Satan's purpose in assaulting the baptism of infants with all his might is to keep out of sight, and gradually to wipe away, that witness of divine grace which the promise itself sets before our eyes. In this way, not only would men be wickedly ungrateful for the mercy of God, but they would also be less careful to train their children in godliness. For it is no slight encouragement to us to bring them up in the fear of God and in the keeping of his law, when we reflect that from their birth they have been counted and acknowledged by him as his children. Therefore, if we would not maliciously darken the kindness of God, let us present our infants to him, to whom he has assigned a place among his friends and his household — that is, among the members of the Church.

Scripture cited
Ps 48:11
A Word for 2026 · U.S.M.C. Ministries

Calvin defends the baptism of believers' children as no mere custom but a sign rooted in the covenant promise — the same promise that ran to Abraham and his seed now sealed under a better administration, for the God who said he would be a God to us and to our children has not grown stingier under the gospel. A word for 2026, offered with Reformed Baptist conviction, presses Calvin's covenantal heart even where we read the sign's recipients differently: the child in a believing home is no stranger to the promises, but is set within the reach of grace, surrounded by prayer, catechized from the cradle, claimed for Christ in hope. Whatever the timing of the water, do not treat your children as outsiders to the covenant of grace — bring them under the Word, teach them the promise is held out to them, plead with God for their hearts. Discipleship begins at the family table; raise them expecting their Redeemer to keep his word.
