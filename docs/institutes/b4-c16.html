1¶

But since in this age certain frenzied spirits have raised, and still continue to raise, great disturbance in the Church over infant baptism, I cannot avoid adding something here, by way of an appendix, to restrain their fury. If anyone thinks me more long-winded than the subject deserves, let him consider that in a matter of the greatest importance we owe so much to the peace and purity of the Church that we should not squeamishly object to whatever may serve both. I may add that I will try to arrange this discussion so that it will do no small amount to shed further light on the subject of baptism. The French from the beginning of the chapter is as follows: "Or d'autant que nous voyons l'observation que nous tenons de baptiser les petits enfans etre impugnée et debatue par aucuns esprits malins, comme si elle n'avoit point eté institutée de Dieu mais inventée nouvellement des hommes, ou pour le moins quelques années apres le tems des Apostres, j'estime qu'il viendra bien à propos, de confermer en cest endroit les consciences imbecilles, et refuter les objections mensonges qui pouroient faire teis seducteurs, pour renverser le verité de Dieu aux cœur des simples, qui ne seraient pas exercités pour repondre a leur cauteles et cavillations." That is: Now, since we see that our practice of baptizing little children is attacked and disputed by certain malicious spirits, as if it had not been appointed by God, but newly invented by men, or at least introduced some years after the days of the apostles, I think it will be very fitting to strengthen weak consciences on this point, and to refute the lying objections that such seducers might raise in order to overthrow the truth of God in the hearts of the simple, who may not be practiced in answering their tricks and quibbles.

2¶

In the first place, then, it is a well-known doctrine, and one on which all the godly agree, that the right consideration of signs does not lie merely in the outward ceremonies, but depends chiefly on the promise and the spiritual mysteries that the ceremonies themselves are appointed to represent. Therefore whoever would thoroughly understand the effect of baptism, its purpose and true character, must not stop at the element and the bodily object, but must look ahead to the divine promises that are offered to us in it, and rise to the inward secrets that are represented in it. Whoever understands these has reached the solid truth and, so to speak, the whole substance of baptism, and will see from this the nature and use of the outward sprinkling. On the other hand, whoever passes these by with contempt and keeps his thoughts fixed entirely on the visible ceremony will neither understand the force nor the proper nature of baptism, nor grasp what is meant or what purpose is served by the use of water. This is confirmed by passages of Scripture too numerous and too clear to need further discussion here. It remains, therefore, to inquire into the nature and power of baptism as shown by the promises given in it. Scripture shows, first, that it points to that cleansing from sin which we obtain through the blood of Christ; and, second, to the mortification of the flesh, which consists in sharing in his death, by which believers are regenerated to newness of life, and so to the fellowship of Christ. To these general heads may be referred all that the Scriptures teach concerning baptism, with this addition: that it is also a symbol by which we testify our religion before men.

3¶

Now, since before baptism was instituted the people of God had circumcision in its place, let us see how far these two signs differ and how far they resemble each other. In this way it will appear what correspondence there is between them. When the Lord commands Abraham to observe circumcision (Genesis 17:10), he first declares that he will be a God to him and to his seed, adding that in himself is a perfect sufficiency of all things, and that Abraham may count on his hand as the fountain of every blessing. These words include the promise of eternal life, as our Savior interprets them when he uses them to prove the immortality and resurrection of believers: "God," he says, "is not the God of the dead, but of the living" (Matthew 22:32). Hence Paul too, when showing the Ephesians how great the destruction was from which the Lord had delivered them, seeing that they had not been admitted to the covenant of circumcision, concludes that at that time they were aliens from the covenant of promise, without God and without hope (Ephesians 2:12), all of which are included in the covenant. Now, the first access to God, the first entrance to immortal life, is the forgiveness of sins. It follows that this corresponds to the promise of our cleansing in baptism. The Lord afterward covenants with Abraham that he is to walk before him in sincerity and innocence of heart; this applies to mortification, or regeneration. And lest anyone should doubt whether circumcision was the sign of mortification, Moses explains it more clearly elsewhere, when he urges the people of Israel to circumcise the foreskin of their heart, because the Lord had chosen them as his own people out of all the nations of the earth. Just as the Lord, in choosing the descendants of Abraham as his people, commands them to be circumcised, so Moses declares that they are to be circumcised in heart, thus explaining what that fleshly circumcision represents. Then, lest anyone should attempt this in his own strength, he shows that it is the work of divine grace. All this is pressed so often by the prophets that there is no need here to collect the passages, which occur everywhere. We have, therefore, a spiritual promise given to the fathers in circumcision, like the one given to us in baptism, since it represented to them both the forgiveness of sins and the mortification of the flesh. Moreover, just as we have shown that Christ, in whom both of these reside, is the foundation of baptism, so he must also be the foundation of circumcision. For he is promised to Abraham, and in him all nations are blessed. To seal this grace, the sign of circumcision is added.

4¶

There is now no difficulty in seeing where the two signs agree and where they differ. The promise, in which we have shown that the power of the signs consists, is one and the same in both, namely, the promise of the fatherly favor of God, of the forgiveness of sins, and of eternal life. And the thing represented is one and the same, namely, regeneration. The foundation on which the fulfillment of these things depends is one in both. Therefore there is no difference in the inward meaning, by which the whole power and distinctive nature of the sacrament is to be judged. The only difference that remains is in the outward ceremony, which is its least part, the chief part consisting in the promise and the thing signified. Hence we may conclude that everything that applies to circumcision applies also to baptism, always excepting the difference in the visible ceremony. To this correspondence and comparison we are led by that rule of the apostle in which he commands us to bring every interpretation of Scripture to the analogy of faith 127 D127 The "analogy of faith," to which we are to "bring every interpretation of Scripture," refers to the ultimate rule or standard of interpretation, the final test of all doctrine; namely, the teaching of Scripture as a whole. Analogy suggests comparison; thus we are to compare a proposed interpretation of a specific portion of Scripture with the interpretation which Scripture as a whole, either explicitly or generally, gives to itself. Analogy suggests proportion or measure; thus we are to ascertain the intention and importance of a single text of Scripture in proportion to its place and distribution in the whole body of revealed truth. Analogy also suggests relationship; thus we are to study the particular doctrines of Scripture in relation to the system of doctrine revealed therein.

5¶

Now, if we are to investigate whether or not baptism is rightly given to infants, will we not say that the man is trifling, or rather raving, who would stop at the element of water and the outward observance, and not allow his mind to rise to the spiritual mystery? If reason is heard, it will undoubtedly appear that baptism is properly administered to infants as something owed to them. The Lord did not in former times bestow circumcision on them without making them sharers in all the things that circumcision signified. He would have deceived his people with mere imposture had he quieted them with false symbols; the very idea is shocking. He plainly declares that the circumcision of the infant will serve as a seal of the promise of the covenant. But if the covenant remains firm and fixed, it applies no less to the children of Christians today than it did to the children of the Jews under the Old Testament. Now, if they are partakers of the thing signified, how can they be denied the sign? If they obtain the reality, how can they be refused the figure? The outward sign is so joined with the word in the sacrament that it cannot be separated from it; but if they could be separated, to which of the two should we attach the greater value? Surely, when we see that the sign serves the word, we will say that it is subordinate and assign it the lower place. Since, then, the word of baptism is intended for infants, why should we deny them the sign, which is an appendage of the word? This one reason, if no other could be given, would be more than enough to refute all opponents. The objection that there was a fixed day for circumcision is a mere quibble. We admit that we are not now, like the Jews, bound to certain days; but when the Lord declares that, although he prescribes no day, he is nevertheless pleased that infants should be formally admitted to his covenant, what more do we ask?

6¶

Scripture gives us a still clearer knowledge of the truth. For it is entirely evident that the covenant which the Lord once made with Abraham applies no less to Christians now than it did in former times to the Jewish people, and therefore that word refers no less to Christians than to Jews. Unless, indeed, we imagine that Christ by his coming diminished or cut short the grace of the Father, an idea not free from abominable blasphemy. Therefore, just as the children of the Jews were called a holy seed, because, having been made heirs of that covenant, they were set apart from the heathen, so for the same reason the children of Christians, even those who have only one believing parent, are called holy, and, by the testimony of the apostle, differ from the unclean seed of idolaters. Then, since the Lord, immediately after the covenant was made with Abraham, ordered it to be sealed in infants by an outward sacrament, how can it be said that Christians are not to attest it today and seal it in their children? Let no one object that the only symbol by which the Lord ordered his covenant to be confirmed was circumcision, which was long ago abolished. It is easy to answer that, in keeping with the form of the old dispensation, he appointed circumcision to confirm his covenant, but that, although circumcision is abolished, the same reason for confirmation still remains, a reason we hold in common with the Jews. Hence it is always necessary to consider carefully what is common to both, and in what respect they differed from us. The covenant is common, and the reason for confirming it is common. Only the manner of confirming it differs, in that they had circumcision, in place of which we now have baptism. Otherwise, if the testimony by which the Jews were assured of the salvation of their seed is taken from us, the result will be that, by the coming of Christ, the grace of God that was formerly given to the Jews is more obscure and less perfectly attested to us. If this cannot be said without extreme insult to Christ, through whom the infinite goodness of the Father has been poured out upon the earth and declared to men more brightly and kindly than ever before, it must be confessed that it cannot now be more restricted, or less clearly shown, than it was under the dim shadows of the law.

7¶

Hence our Lord Jesus Christ, to give an example from which the world might learn that he had come to enlarge rather than to limit the grace of the Father, kindly takes the little children in his arms and rebukes his disciples for trying to keep them from coming (Matthew 19:13), because they were keeping those to whom the kingdom of heaven belonged away from him, through whom alone there is access to heaven. But it will be asked: What likeness is there between baptism and our Savior's embracing of little children? He is not said to have baptized them, but to have received, embraced, and blessed them; and therefore, if we would imitate his example, we must give infants the help of our prayers, not baptize them. But let us look at our Savior's action a little more carefully than these men do. For we must not lightly pass over the fact that our Savior, in ordering little children to be brought to him, adds the reason: "of such is the kingdom of heaven." And afterward he shows his goodwill by action, when he embraces them and with prayer and blessing commends them to his Father. If it is right that children should be brought to Christ, why should they not be admitted to baptism, the symbol of our communion and fellowship with Christ? If the kingdom of heaven is theirs, why should they be denied the sign by which an entrance, as it were, is opened into the Church, so that, being admitted into it, they may be enrolled among the heirs of the heavenly kingdom? How unjust we would be to drive away those whom Christ invites to himself, to strip those whom he adorns with his gifts, to shut out those whom he freely admits. But if we insist on discussing the difference between our Savior's action and baptism, how much more highly shall we value baptism (by which we testify that infants are included in the divine covenant) than the taking up, embracing, laying hands on children, and praying over them, actions by which Christ, when present, declares both that they are his and that they are sanctified by him? By the other quibbles with which the objectors try to evade this passage, they only expose their ignorance. They argue that, because our Savior says, "Suffer little children to come," they must have been several years old and able to come. But the Evangelists call them βπέφη καὶ παιδιά, terms that denote infants still at their mothers' breasts. The word "come" is used simply for "approach." See the quibbles to which men are driven when they have hardened themselves against the truth! There is nothing more solid in their claim that the kingdom of heaven is not assigned to children, but to those like children, since the expression is "of such," not "of themselves." If this is granted, what becomes of the reason our Savior uses to show that they are not strangers to him because of their tender age? When he orders that little children be allowed to come to him, nothing is plainer than that mere infancy is meant. Lest this should seem absurd, he adds, "Of such is the kingdom of heaven." But if infants must necessarily be included, the expression "of such" clearly shows that infants themselves, and those like them, are intended.

8¶

Everyone must now see that infant baptism, which receives such strong support from Scripture, is by no means a human invention. Nor is there anything plausible in the objection that we nowhere read of even one infant being baptized by the hands of the apostles. For although this is not expressly recorded by the Evangelists, yet since infants are not expressly excluded when mention is made of a household being baptized (Acts 16:15, 32), what man of sense will argue from this that they were not baptized? If arguments of this kind were valid, it would likewise be necessary to bar women from the Lord's Supper, since we do not read that they were ever admitted to it in the days of the apostles. But here we are content with the rule of faith. For when we reflect on the nature of the ordinance of the Lord's Supper, we easily judge to whom its use is to be given. We observe the same in the case of baptism. For, considering the purpose for which it was instituted, we clearly see that it applies no less to children than to those of riper years, and that therefore they cannot be deprived of it without plainly defrauding the will of its divine Author. The claim they spread among the common people, that a long series of years passed after the resurrection of Christ during which infant baptism was unknown, is a shameful falsehood, since there is no writer, however ancient, who does not trace its origin to the days of the apostles.

9¶

It remains to point out briefly what benefit comes from this observance, both to believers who bring their children to the church to be baptized, and to the infants themselves, to whom the sacred water is applied, so that no one may despise the ordinance as useless or superfluous. Yet anyone who would think of ridiculing baptism on this pretext would also ridicule the divine ordinance of circumcision; for what can they bring forward to attack the one that may not be turned back against the other? So the Lord punishes the arrogance of those who at once condemn whatever their fleshly sense cannot grasp. But God supplies us with other weapons to check their stupidity. His holy institution, from which we feel that our faith draws wonderful comfort, does not deserve to be called superfluous. For the divine symbol given to the child, as with the impression of a seal, confirms the promise given to the godly parent, and declares that the Lord will be a God not to him only, but to his seed; visiting with his grace and goodness not him alone, but his descendants also, to the thousandth generation. When the infinite goodness of God is displayed in this way, it first of all supplies the richest material for proclaiming his glory, and fills godly hearts with no ordinary joy, urging them more strongly to love their affectionate Father when they see that, for their sake, he extends his care to their descendants. I am not moved by the objection that the promise ought to be enough to confirm the salvation of our children. God has judged otherwise, who, seeing our weakness, has been pleased in this matter to stoop to it. Let those, then, who embrace the promise of mercy for their children consider it their duty to offer them to the Church to be sealed with the symbol of mercy, and let them rouse themselves to surer confidence on seeing with their bodily eyes the covenant of the Lord engraved on the bodies of their children. On the other hand, children receive some benefit from their baptism when, being grafted into the body of the Church, they are made objects of greater care to the other members. Then, when they have grown up, they are strongly urged by it to an earnest desire to serve God, who received them as sons by the formal symbol of adoption before, in their infancy, they were able to recognize him as their Father. In short, we ought to stand in great awe of the warning that God will take vengeance on everyone who scorns to impress the symbol of the covenant on his child (Genesis 17:15), since such contempt is a rejection and, as it were, a renouncing of the grace offered.

10¶

Let us now discuss the arguments with which some furious madmen never cease to attack this holy ordinance of God. And first, feeling themselves pressed beyond measure by the likeness between baptism and circumcision, they contend that there is a wide difference between the two signs, so that the one has nothing in common with the other. They maintain that the things meant are different, that the covenant is altogether different, and that the persons included under the name of children are different. When they first proceed to the proof, they claim that circumcision was a figure of mortification, not of baptism. This we gladly grant them, for it supports our view admirably; for the only proof we use in its support is that baptism and circumcision are signs of mortification. From this we conclude that the one was put in place of the other, baptism representing to us the very thing that circumcision signified to the Jews. In asserting a difference of covenant, with what barbarous audacity do they corrupt and destroy Scripture! And that not in one passage only, but so as to leave no passage safe and whole. They portray the Jews as so carnal as to resemble beasts more than men, representing the covenant made with them as reaching no further than this temporary life, and the promises given to them as shrinking down to present and bodily blessings. If this dogma is accepted, what remains but that the Jewish nation was loaded for a time with divine kindness (just as swine are fattened in their sty), only to perish eternally at last? Whenever we cite circumcision and the promises attached to it, they answer that circumcision was a literal sign and that its promises were carnal.

11¶

Certainly, if circumcision was a literal sign, the same view must be taken of baptism, since in the second chapter of Colossians the apostle makes the one not a bit more spiritual than the other. For he says that in Christ we "are circumcised with the circumcision made without hands, in putting off the body of the sins of the flesh, by the circumcision of Christ." To explain his meaning he immediately adds that we are "buried with him in baptism." What do these words mean, except that the truth and fulfillment of baptism is the truth and fulfillment of circumcision, since they represent one thing? For his aim is to show that baptism is the same thing to Christians that circumcision formerly was to the Jews. Now, since we have already clearly shown that the promises of both signs, and the mysteries they represent, agree, we will not dwell on the point any longer for now. I would only remind believers to consider, without my saying anything more, whether that is to be regarded as an earthly and literal sign which has nothing heavenly or spiritual beneath it. But lest they should blind the simple with their smoke, we will dispose in passing of one objection with which they cloak this most shameless falsehood. It is absolutely certain that the original promises containing the covenant that God made with the Israelites under the old dispensation were spiritual and had reference to eternal life, and that they were, of course, likewise received spiritually by the fathers, so that they might draw from them a sure hope of immortality and aspire to it with their whole soul. At the same time, we are far from denying that he testified his kindness to them by carnal and earthly blessings, though we hold that by these the hope of the spiritual promises was confirmed. In this way, when he promised eternal blessedness to his servant Abraham, he added the promise of possessing the land of Canaan, in order to set a plain token of his favor before his eyes. In the same way we should understand all the earthly promises given to the Jewish nation, the spiritual promise always holding the first place, as the head to which the others referred. Since I handled this subject fully when treating of the difference between the old and the new dispensations, I only touch on it here.

12¶

Under the name of children, the difference they point to is this: that under the old dispensation the children of Abraham were those who descended from his seed, but that the name is now given to those who imitate his faith; and therefore that fleshly infancy, which was grafted into the fellowship of the covenant by circumcision, prefigured the spiritual children of the new covenant, who are regenerated by the word of God to immortal life. In these words we do indeed find a small spark of truth, but these giddy spirits err seriously in this: seizing on whatever first comes to hand, when they ought to go further and compare many things together, they stubbornly fasten on one single word. Hence it cannot but happen that they are deceived again and again, because they do not exert themselves to gain a full knowledge of any subject. We certainly admit that the fleshly seed of Abraham for a time held the place of the spiritual seed, which is grafted into him by faith (Galatians 4:28; Romans 4:12). For we are called his sons, though we have no natural relationship with him. But if they mean, as they show not obscurely, that the spiritual promise was never made to the fleshly seed of Abraham, they are badly mistaken. We must, therefore, take better aim, as we are directed by the infallible guidance of Scripture. The Lord, then, promises Abraham that he will have a seed in whom all the nations of the earth will be blessed, and at the same time assures him that he will be a God both to him and to his seed. All who by faith receive Christ as the author of the blessing are heirs of this promise, and accordingly are called the children of Abraham.

13¶

Although after the resurrection of Christ the boundaries of the kingdom began to be extended far and wide into all nations without distinction, so that, according to Christ's declaration, believers were gathered from every quarter to sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 8:11), still, for many ages before, the Jews had enjoyed this great mercy. And since he had chosen them (passing by all other nations) to be for a time the keepers of his favor, he designated them as his own purchased people (Exodus 19:5). In testimony of this kindness he appointed circumcision, by which symbol the Jews were taught that God watched over their safety, and by which they were raised to the hope of eternal life. For what can ever be lacking to one whom God has once taken under his protection? Therefore the apostle, to prove that the Gentiles as well as the Jews were the children of Abraham, speaks in this way: "Faith was reckoned to Abraham for righteousness. How was it then reckoned? when he was in circumcision, or in uncircumcision? Not in circumcision, but in uncircumcision. And he received the sign of circumcision, a seal of the righteousness of the faith which he had yet being uncircumcised; that he might be the father of all them that believe, though they be not circumcised: that righteousness might be imputed to them also: and the father of circumcision to them who are not of the circumcision only, but who also walk in the steps of that faith of our father Abraham, which he had yet being uncircumcised" (Romans 4:9-12). Do we not see that both are made equal in dignity? For until the time appointed by the divine decree, he was the father of circumcision. But when, as the apostle writes elsewhere (Ephesians 2:14), the dividing wall that separated the Gentiles from the Jews was broken down, access to the kingdom of God was given to them also, and he became their father, and that without the sign of circumcision, its place being supplied by baptism. In saying expressly that Abraham was not the father of those who were of the circumcision only, his aim was to check the haughtiness of some who, setting aside all regard for godliness, prided themselves on mere ceremonies. In the same way we may today refute the vanity of those who seek nothing in baptism but water.

14¶

But against this a passage is brought from the Epistle to the Romans, in which the apostle says that those who are of the flesh are not the children of Abraham, but that only those who are the children of the promise are counted as the seed (Romans 9:7). For he seems to suggest that fleshly descent from Abraham, which we think of some importance, is nothing. But we must pay careful attention to the subject the apostle is treating there. His aim being to show the Jews that the goodness of God was not restricted to the seed of Abraham, and indeed that descent of itself contributes nothing, he brings forward as proof the cases of Ishmael and Esau. These were rejected just as if they had been strangers, although according to the flesh they were the genuine offspring of Abraham, and the blessing rests in Isaac and Jacob. This proves what he afterward affirms, namely, that salvation depends on the mercy that God bestows on whomever he pleases, and that the Jews have no ground to glory or pride themselves on the name of the covenant unless they keep the law of the covenant, that is, obey the word. On the other hand, after casting down their vain confidence in their descent, because he knew that the covenant made with the descendants of Abraham could not rightly prove fruitless, he declares that due honor should still be paid to fleshly descent from Abraham, by reason of which the Jews were the first and native heirs of the gospel, except in so far as they were rejected as unworthy for their ingratitude; and yet they were rejected in such a way as not to leave their nation wholly without the heavenly blessing. For this reason, though they were stubborn breakers of the covenant, he calls them holy (such respect does he pay to the holy race that God had honored with his sacred covenant), while we, in comparison with them, are called posthumous or untimely-born children of Abraham, and that not by nature but by adoption, just as if a twig were broken from its own tree and grafted onto another stock. Therefore, that they might not be cheated of their privilege, it was necessary that the gospel should be preached to them first. For they are, as it were, the firstborn in the family of God. The honor due them on this account must therefore be paid them until they have rejected the offer and, by their ingratitude, caused it to be transferred to the Gentiles. Nor, however great the stubbornness with which they persist in warring against the gospel, are we for that reason to despise them. We must consider that, in respect of the promise, the blessing of God still rests among them and, as the apostle testifies, will never entirely depart from them, seeing that "the gifts and calling of God are without repentance" (Romans 11:29).

15¶

Such is the value of the promise given to the descendants of Abraham; such is the balance in which it is to be weighed. Hence, though we have no doubt that in distinguishing the children of God from bastards and foreigners the election of God reigns freely, we at the same time see that he was pleased to embrace the seed of Abraham with his mercy in a special way, and, to attest it more fully, to seal it by circumcision. The case of the Christian Church is entirely of the same kind; for as Paul there declares that the Jews are sanctified by their parents, so he says elsewhere that the children of Christians receive sanctification from their parents. From this it is inferred that those who are chargeable with uncleanness are rightly separated from others. Now, who can have any doubt about the falsehood of their next assertion, namely, that the infants who were formerly circumcised only prefigured the spiritual infancy that is produced by regeneration through the word of God? When the apostle says that "Jesus Christ was a minister of the circumcision for the truth of God, to confirm the promises made unto the fathers" (Romans 15:8), he is not philosophizing subtly, but means as if he had said: Since the covenant made with Abraham has respect to his seed, Christ came for the salvation of the Jewish nation in order to perform and discharge the promise made by the Father. Do you see how he holds that, after the resurrection of Christ, the promise is to be fulfilled to the seed of Abraham, not allegorically, but literally, as the words express? To the same effect is Peter's declaration to the Jews: "The promise is unto you and to your children" (Acts 2:39); and in the next chapter he calls them the children of the covenant, that is, heirs. Not very different from this is the other passage of the apostle quoted above, in which he regards and describes circumcision performed on infants as a testimony to the communion they have with Christ. And indeed, if we listen to the absurdities of these men, what will become of the promise by which the Lord, in the second commandment of his law, pledges to be gracious to the seed of his servants for a thousand generations? Shall we resort to allegory here? That would be the merest quibble. Shall we say that it has been abolished? In that way we would do away with the law that Christ came not to destroy but to fulfill, since it turns to our everlasting good. Therefore let it be beyond dispute that God is so good and generous to his people that he is pleased, as a mark of his favor, to extend their privileges to the children born to them.

16¶

The distinctions these men try to draw between baptism and circumcision are not only ridiculous and empty of any appearance of reason, but at odds with one another. For when they affirm that baptism refers to the first day of the spiritual struggle, and circumcision to the eighth day, when mortification has already been accomplished, they immediately forget the distinction and change their tune, representing circumcision as prefiguring the mortification of the flesh, and baptism as a burial, which is given to none but those who are already dead. What are these giddy contradictions but frenzied dreams? According to the first view, baptism ought to come before circumcision; according to the second, it should come after it. It is not the first time we have seen men's minds wander to and fro when they put their own dreams in place of the infallible word of God. We hold, therefore, that their first distinction is mere imagination. If we were inclined to make an allegory of the eighth day, theirs would not be the right way to do it. It would be much better, with the early Christians, to refer the number eight to the resurrection, which took place on the eighth day and on which we know that newness of life depends, or to the whole course of the present life, during which mortification ought to be in progress, ending only when life itself ends; although it would seem that God intended to provide for the tenderness of infancy by putting off circumcision to the eighth day, since the wound would have been more dangerous if inflicted immediately after birth. How much more reasonable is the declaration of Scripture that we, when already dead, are buried by baptism (Romans 6:4), since it plainly states that we are buried into death so that we may thoroughly die, and from then on strive toward that mortification? Equally clever is their quibble that women should not be baptized if baptism is to be made to conform to circumcision. For if it is most certain that the sanctification of the seed of Israel was attested by the sign of circumcision, there can be no doubt that it was appointed alike for the sanctification of males and females. But though the rite could be performed only on males, the females were nevertheless, through them, partners and sharers in circumcision. Therefore, setting aside all such quibbling distinctions, let us fix our attention on the very full likeness between baptism and circumcision, as seen in the inward office, the promise, the use, and the effect.

17¶

They seem to think they bring forward their strongest reason for denying baptism to children when they claim that, because of their tender age, children are not yet able to understand the mystery that is sealed in it, namely, spiritual regeneration, which does not apply to earliest infancy. From this they infer that children are to be regarded only as sons of Adam until they have reached an age fit to receive the second birth. But all this is directly opposed to the truth of God. For if they are to be counted sons of Adam, they are left in death, since in Adam we can do nothing but die. On the contrary, Christ bids them be brought to him. Why? Because he is life. Therefore, that he may give them life, he makes them partners with himself; whereas these men would drive them away from Christ and sentence them to death. For if they claim that infants do not perish when they are counted sons of Adam, the error is more than sufficiently refuted by the testimony of Scripture (1 Corinthians 15:22). For since it declares that in Adam all die, it follows that no hope of life remains except in Christ. Therefore, that we may become heirs of life, we must have communion with him. Again, since it is written elsewhere that we are all by nature children of wrath (Ephesians 2:3) and conceived in sin (Psalm 51:5), of which condemnation is the inseparable companion, we must part with our own nature before we have any access to the kingdom of God. And what can be clearer than the words, "Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" (1 Corinthians 15:50)? Therefore let everything that is our own be done away (this cannot happen without regeneration), and then we will come into this possession of the kingdom. In short, if Christ speaks truly when he declares that he is life, we must necessarily be grafted into him, by whom we are delivered from the bondage of death. But how, they ask, are infants regenerated, when they have no knowledge of either good or evil? We answer that the work of God, though beyond the reach of our understanding, is not therefore null. Moreover, infants who are to be saved (and it is certain that some are saved at this age) must without question first be regenerated by the Lord. For if they bring inborn corruption with them from their mother's womb, they must be cleansed before they can be admitted into the kingdom of God, into which nothing that defiles shall enter (Revelation 21:27). If they are born sinners, as David and Paul affirm, they must either remain unaccepted and hated by God, or be justified. And why do we ask for more, when the Judge himself openly declares that "except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God" (John 3:3)? But to silence this class of objectors, God gave, in the case of John the Baptist, whom he sanctified from his mother's womb (Luke 1:15), a proof of what he could do in others. They gain nothing by the quibble to which they resort here, namely, that this was done only once, and that it does not therefore follow that the Lord always deals this way with infants. That is not how we reason. Our only aim is to show that they unjustly and maliciously confine the power of God within limits in which it cannot be confined. No more weight is due to another evasion. They claim that, in the ordinary language of Scripture, "from the womb" means the same as "from childhood." But it is easy to see that the angel meant something different when he announced to Zacharias that the child not yet born would be filled with the Holy Spirit. Instead of trying to lay down a law for God, let us hold that he sanctifies whom he pleases, in the way he sanctified John, since his power is not diminished.

18¶

And indeed, Christ was sanctified from earliest infancy so that he might sanctify his elect in himself at any age, without distinction. For just as, in order to wipe away the guilt of the disobedience that had been committed in our flesh, he took on that very flesh, so that in it he might, for our sake and in our place, render perfect obedience, so he was conceived by the Holy Spirit so that, being wholly filled with his holiness in the flesh he had assumed, he might pour it into us. If in Christ we have a perfect pattern of all the graces that God bestows on all his children, in this instance we have a proof that the age of infancy is not incapable of receiving sanctification. This, at least, we set down as beyond dispute: that none of the elect is called away from the present life without first being sanctified and regenerated by the Spirit of God. 128 D128 This strong assertion must be seen in its relationship to the question of the salvation of elect infants dying in infancy. If they are to have remission of sins, a new nature, and the blessing of eternal life, it is clear that they must be regenerated.

19¶

But faith, they say, comes by hearing, the use of which infants have not yet gained; nor can they be fit to know God, being, as Moses declares, without the knowledge of good and evil (Deuteronomy 1:39). But they do not notice that where the apostle makes hearing the beginning of faith, he is only describing the ordinary order and arrangement that the Lord usually employs in calling his people, and is not laying down an unchanging rule for which no other method can be substituted. He has certainly called many and endowed them with the true knowledge of himself by inward means, by the illumination of the Spirit, without the use of preaching. But since they think it very absurd to attribute any knowledge of God to infants, whom Moses describes as lacking the knowledge of good and evil, let them tell me where the danger lies if infants are said to receive now some part of that grace of which they are to have the full measure a little later. For if fullness of life consists in the perfect knowledge of God, then since some of those whom death snatches away in the first moments of infancy pass into eternal life, they are certainly admitted to behold the immediate presence of God. Why, then, may the Lord not, if he so pleases, shine on those now with some small beam whom he is to illumine with the full brightness of his light, especially if he does not remove their ignorance before he delivers them from the prison of the flesh? I would not rashly affirm that they are endowed with the same faith that we experience in ourselves, or that they have any knowledge at all resembling faith (this I would rather leave undecided); 129 D129 It is instructive to take note of Calvin's careful restraint and sense of proportion in the previous few sentences. With respect to the question of the manner in which elect infants dying in infancy are saved, Calvin, while presupposing their need and the Spirit's supply of regeneration (see note on section 18), makes no definite assertion concerning the presence or absence of faith in them. This position of indecision (as Calvin terms it) is commendable, precisely because it does not presume beyond the teaching of Scripture.

20¶

To gain a stronger footing here, they add that baptism is a sacrament of repentance and faith, and that, since neither of these applies to tender infancy, we must beware of making its meaning empty and vain by admitting infants to the communion of baptism. But these darts are aimed more at God than at us, since the fact that circumcision was a sign of repentance is fully established by many passages of Scripture (Jeremiah 4:4). So Paul calls it a seal of the righteousness of faith (Romans 4:11). Let God, then, be asked why he ordered circumcision to be performed on the bodies of infants! For since baptism and circumcision stand in the same case here, they cannot grant anything to the latter without conceding it to the former. If they fall back on their usual evasion, that spiritual infants were then prefigured by the age of infancy, we have already closed off this way of escape. We say, then, that since God gave circumcision, the sign of repentance and faith, to infants, it should not seem absurd that they are now made partakers of baptism, unless men choose to cry out against an institution of God. But as in all his acts, so here too, enough wisdom and righteousness shine out to silence the slanders of the ungodly. For although infants, at the moment they were circumcised, did not understand what the sign meant, they were nevertheless truly circumcised for the mortification of their corrupt and polluted nature, a mortification toward which they afterward strove as adults. In short, the objection is easily disposed of by this fact: that children are baptized for future repentance and faith. Though these are not yet formed in them, the seed of both nevertheless lies hidden in them by the secret working of the Spirit. This answer at once overthrows all the objections that are twisted against us out of the meaning of baptism; for example, the title by which Paul distinguishes it when he calls it the "washing of regeneration and renewing" (Titus 3:5). From this they argue that it is not to be given to any but those who are capable of such experiences. But we in turn may object that neither ought circumcision, which is called regeneration, to be given to any but the regenerate. In this way we would condemn a divine institution. So, as we have already suggested, all the arguments that tend to shake circumcision have no force in attacking baptism. Nor can they escape by saying that everything resting on the authority of God is absolutely fixed, even if no reason for it could be given, but that this reverence is not due to infant baptism, nor to other similar things that are not commended to us by the express word of God. They always remain caught in this dilemma: the command of God to circumcise infants was either lawful and beyond criticism, or it deserved rebuke. If there was nothing improper or absurd in it, no absurdity can be shown in the observance of infant baptism.

21¶

The charge of absurdity with which they try to brand it we dispose of as follows. If those on whom the Lord has bestowed his election depart this life after receiving the sign of regeneration but before they become adults, he renews them by the incomprehensible power of his Spirit, in the way that he alone sees to be fitting. If they reach an age when they can be instructed in the meaning of baptism, they will be stirred by it to greater zeal for renewal, the badge of which they will learn that they received in earliest infancy, so that they might strive toward it throughout their whole lives. To the same effect are the two passages in which Paul teaches that we are buried with Christ by baptism (Romans 6:4; Colossians 2:12). For by this he does not mean that one who is to be initiated by baptism must first have been buried with Christ; he simply declares the doctrine taught by baptism, and that to those already baptized, so that not even the most senseless can maintain from this passage that burial with Christ ought to come before baptism. In this way Moses and the prophets reminded the people of the thing meant by circumcision, which, however, they had received as infants. To the same effect Paul says to the Galatians, "As many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ" (Galatians 3:27). Why? So that from then on they might live to Christ, to whom they had not lived before. And though in adults the receiving of the sign ought to follow the understanding of its meaning, yet, as will shortly be explained, a different rule must be followed with children. No other conclusion can be drawn from a passage in Peter on which they rely heavily. He says that baptism is "not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God by the resurrection of Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 3:21). From this they contend that nothing is left for infant baptism, which becomes mere empty smoke, as being altogether at odds with the meaning of baptism. But the delusion that misleads them is that they would always have the thing come before the sign in order of time. 130 D130 In connection with the sacraments, there are three aspects which must be carefully distinguished: 1. the spiritual reality which is signified (what Calvin calls "the thing") 2. the external sacrament itself (what Calvin calls "the sign") 3. our understanding of the spiritual significance of the sacrament (as mediated to us by the Word and Spirit). Calvin has called our attention to the very important fact that a particular time order of these aspects is not crucial to the proper use of the sacraments. He asserts that the spiritual reality itself (e.g., regeneration) may either precede or follow the external sacrament (i.e., 1 may precede 2, or 2 may precede 1). The order, then, of the three aspects enumerated above could be 1, 2, 3, or 1, 3, 2, or 2, 1, 3. (The reason why the order could not be 2, 3, 1, or 3, 1, 2, or 3, 2, 1, is that, because of that depravity which fills our minds with ignorance and spiritual darkness, our understanding of the sacrament's spiritual significance [3] must always follow the spiritual reality which is signified [1].) Calvin's specific interest in this section is, of course, to point out that the third possible order (2, 1, 3) is a live option. That is, the time order (in addition to the other possible orders) could be as follows: 2. the external sacrament itself (e.g., baptism) 1. the spiritual reality which is signified (e.g., regeneration) 3. our understanding of the spiritual significance of the sacrament. And the time lapse between number 2 and numbers 1 and 3 could amount to an indefinite number of years, just as it ordinarily did in the case of circumcised infants in Old Testament times.

22¶

Everyone, I think, must clearly see that all arguments of this kind are mere perversions of Scripture. We will briefly examine the other remaining arguments related to these. They object that baptism is given for the forgiveness of sins. When this is granted, it strongly supports our view; for since we are born sinners, we stand in need of forgiveness and pardon from the very womb. Moreover, since God does not shut this age out from the hope of mercy, but rather gives assurance of it, why should we deprive it of the sign, which is far inferior to the reality? The arrow they aim at us, therefore, we throw back at them. Infants receive forgiveness of sins; therefore they are not to be deprived of the sign. They bring forward the passage from Ephesians that Christ gave himself for the Church, "that he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the word" (Ephesians 5:26). Nothing more fitting than this could be cited to overthrow their error; it supplies us with an easy proof. If by baptism Christ intends to attest the washing by which he cleanses his Church, it would seem unfair to deny this attestation to infants, who are rightly considered part of the Church, since they are called heirs of the heavenly kingdom. For Paul includes the whole Church when he says that it was cleansed by the washing of water. In the same way, from his statement in another place that by baptism we are grafted into the body of Christ (1 Corinthians 7:13), we conclude that infants, whom he counts among his members, are to be baptized, so that they may not be cut off from his body. See the violent assault they make with all their siege engines on the ramparts of our faith!

23¶

They now come down to the custom and practice of the apostolic age, claiming that there is no instance of anyone having been admitted to baptism without a prior profession of faith and repentance. For when Peter is asked by his hearers, who were pricked in their heart, "What shall we do?" his advice is, "Repent and be baptized, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ, for the remission of sins" (Acts 2:37, 38). In the same way, when Philip was asked by the eunuch to baptize him, he answered, "If thou believest with all thine heart, thou mayest." From this they think they can establish that baptism cannot lawfully be given to anyone without prior faith and repentance. If we yield to this argument, the first passage, in which there is no mention of faith, will prove that repentance alone is sufficient, and the second, which makes no demand for repentance, that only faith is needed. They will object, I suppose, that the one passage helps the other, and that both are therefore to be taken together. I in turn maintain that these two must be compared with other passages that contribute something to the solution of this difficulty. There are many passages of Scripture whose meaning depends on their particular setting. We have an example of this in the present instance. Those to whom these things are said by Peter and Philip are of an age fit to strive after repentance and to receive faith. We firmly insist that such men are not to be baptized unless their conversion and faith are discerned, at least as far as human judgment can determine it. But it is perfectly clear that infants must be placed in a different class. For when anyone in former times joined the religious communion of Israel, he had to be taught the covenant and instructed in the law of the Lord before he received circumcision, because he was of a different nation; in other words, an alien from the people of Israel, with whom the covenant that circumcision ratified had been made.

24¶

So the Lord, when he chose Abraham for himself, did not begin with circumcision while concealing in the meantime what he meant by that sign, but first announced that he intended to make a covenant with him, and, after Abraham had believed the promise, made him a partaker of the sacrament. Why does the sacrament come after faith in Abraham, and before all understanding in his son Isaac? It is right that one who is admitted in adult age to the fellowship of a covenant by one from whom he had until then been estranged should first learn its terms; but it is not so with the infant born to him. He, according to the terms of the promise, is included in the promise by hereditary right from his mother's womb. Or, to put the matter more briefly and more clearly: if the children of believers, without the help of understanding, are partakers of the covenant, there is no reason why they should be denied the sign because they are unable to swear to its terms. This undoubtedly is the reason why the Lord sometimes declares that the children born to the Israelites are begotten and born to him (Ezekiel 16:20; 23:37). For he undoubtedly gives the place of sons to the children of those to whose seed he has promised that he will be a Father. But the child descended from unbelieving parents is considered a stranger to the covenant until he is united to God by faith. Hence it is not strange that the sign is withheld when the thing signified would be vain and false. In this sense Paul says that the Gentiles, as long as they were sunk in idolatry, were strangers to the covenant (Ephesians 2:11). The whole matter may, if I am not mistaken, be briefly and clearly set out as follows. Those who in adult age embrace the faith of Christ, having until then been strangers to the covenant, are not to receive the sign of baptism without prior faith and repentance. These alone can give them access to the fellowship of the covenant; whereas children descended from Christians, since they are received by God as heirs of the covenant immediately at birth, are also to be admitted to baptism. To this we must refer the Evangelist's account that those who were baptized by John confessed their sins (Matthew 3:6). This example, we hold, ought to be observed today. If a Turk were to offer himself for baptism, we would not perform the rite at once without receiving a confession that satisfied the Church.

25¶

Another passage they bring forward is from the third chapter of John, where our Savior's words seem to them to imply that a present regeneration is required in baptism: "Except a man be born of water, and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God" (John 3:5). See, they say, how baptism is called regeneration by the lips of our Lord himself; on what pretext, then, and with what consistency is baptism given to those who, as is perfectly obvious, are not at all capable of regeneration? First, they are mistaken in imagining that there is any mention of baptism in this passage merely because the word water is used. Nicodemus, after our Savior had explained to him the corruption of nature and the necessity of being born again, kept dreaming of a bodily birth; and so our Savior indicates the way in which God regenerates us, namely, by water and the Spirit; in other words, by the Spirit, who, in watering and cleansing the souls of believers, works in the manner of water. By "water and the Spirit," therefore, I simply understand the Spirit, who is water. Nor is the expression new. It agrees perfectly with the one used in the third chapter of Matthew: "He that cometh after me is mightier than I"; "he shall baptize you with the Holy Ghost, and with fire" (Matthew 3:11). Therefore, just as to baptize with the Holy Spirit and with fire is to confer the Holy Spirit, who in regeneration has the office and nature of fire, so to be born again of water and of the Spirit is nothing else than to receive that power of the Spirit which has the same effect on the soul that water has on the body. I know that a different interpretation is given, but I have no doubt that this is the true meaning, because our Savior's only purpose was to teach that all who aspire to the kingdom of heaven must lay aside their own disposition. And yet, if we were inclined to imitate these men in their way of quibbling, we might easily, after granting what they wish, reply that baptism comes before faith and repentance, since in this passage our Savior mentions it before the Spirit. This certainly must be understood of spiritual gifts, and if they follow baptism, I have gained all I am contending for. But, quibbling aside, the simple interpretation to be adopted is the one I have given, namely, that no man can enter the kingdom of God until he is renewed by living water, that is, by the Spirit.

26¶

This, moreover, plainly explodes the fiction of those who consign all the unbaptized to eternal death. See Calvin, Cont. Articulos Theologorum Paris., Art. 4. Also, Ad Concil. Trident. Also, Vera Eccles. Reformand. Ratio, and in the Appendix. Nævus in August. Lib. 1 ad Bonifac. et Epist. 28. Ambrose, de Vocat. Gentium, Lib. 2 cap. 8; de Abraham, Lib. 2 cap. 11.

27¶

But they boast that they have their strongest bulwark in the very institution of baptism, which they find in the last chapter of Matthew, where Christ, sending his disciples into all the world, commands them to teach and then to baptize. Then, in the last chapter of Mark, it is added, "He that believeth, and is baptized, shall be saved" (Mark 16:16). What more, they say, do we ask, since the words of Christ plainly declare that teaching must come before baptism, and assign to baptism the place next after faith? Our Lord himself gave an example of this order in choosing not to be baptized until his thirtieth year. In how many ways do they entangle themselves here and expose their ignorance! They err more than childishly in deriving the first institution of baptism from this passage, whereas Christ had ordered it to be administered by the apostles from the beginning of his ministry. There is no ground, therefore, for contending that the law and rule of baptism is to be sought in these two passages as containing its first institution. But to indulge them in their error, how feeble is this way of arguing! If I were inclined to evade the point, I have not only a place of escape but a wide field in which to range. For when they cling so desperately to the order of the words, insisting that because it is said, "Go, preach and baptize," and again, "Whosoever believes and is baptized," they must preach before baptizing and believe before being baptized, why may we not object in turn that they must baptize before teaching the observance of the things Christ commanded, because it is said, "Baptize, teaching whatsoever I have commanded you"? We noted the same thing in the other passage in which Christ speaks of regeneration by water and by the Spirit. For if we interpret it as they insist, then baptism must take precedence over spiritual regeneration, because it is mentioned first. Christ teaches that we are to be born again, not of the Spirit and of water, but of water and of the Spirit.

28¶

This unassailable argument, in which they place so much confidence, already seems considerably shaken; but since we have sufficient protection in the simplicity of the truth, I am unwilling to evade the point with petty subtleties. Let them, therefore, have a solid answer. The command Christ gives here relates chiefly to the preaching of the gospel; baptism is added to it as a kind of appendage. Then he speaks of baptism only in so far as its administration is subordinate to the office of teaching. For Christ sends his disciples to proclaim the gospel to all the nations of the world, so that by the doctrine of salvation they may gather men who were previously lost into his kingdom. But who, or what, are those men? It is certain that mention is made only of those who are fit to receive his doctrine. He adds that such men, after being taught, were to be baptized, attaching the promise: Whoever believes and is baptized shall be saved. Is there one syllable about infants in the whole discourse? What, then, is the form of the argument with which they attack us? Those who are of adult age are to be instructed and brought to faith before being baptized; therefore it is unlawful to make baptism common to infants. At the very most, they cannot prove anything else from this passage than that the gospel must be preached to those capable of hearing it before they are baptized; for the passage speaks only of such. From this, let them place an obstacle in the way of baptizing infants if they can.

29¶

But I will make their fallacies plain even to the blind by a very simple comparison. If anyone were to insist that infants are to be deprived of food, on the pretext that the apostle allows none to eat except those who work (2 Thessalonians 3:10), would he not deserve to be scorned by all? Why? Because he twists what was said of a certain class of men and a certain age, and applies it to all without distinction. The skill of these men in the present case is no greater. What everyone sees to be intended for adults alone they apply to infants, subjecting them to a rule that was laid down only for those of riper years. As for the example of our Savior, it lends no support to their case. He was not baptized before his thirtieth year. This is indeed true, but the reason is obvious: because he then resolved to lay the solid foundation of baptism by his preaching, or rather to confirm the foundation that John had laid before. Therefore, when he was pleased to institute baptism together with his doctrine, he sanctified it in his own person, in order to give greater authority to his institution, and that at the most fitting time, namely, the beginning of his ministry. In short, they can prove nothing more than that baptism had its origin and beginning with the preaching of the gospel. But if they are pleased to fix on the thirtieth year, why do they not observe it, instead of admitting anyone to baptism according to the opinion they may have formed of his progress? Indeed, even Servetus, one of their masters, although he stubbornly insisted on this age, had begun to play the prophet in his twenty-first year; as if any man could be tolerated in claiming for himself the office of a teacher in the Church before he was a member of the Church.

30¶

At last they object that there is no greater reason for admitting infants to baptism than to the Lord's Supper, to which, however, they are never admitted; as if Scripture did not in every way draw a wide distinction between them. In the early Church, indeed, the Lord's Supper was often given to infants, as appears from Cyprian and Augustine (Augustine, To Boniface, book 1); but the practice rightly fell into disuse. For if we consider the particular nature of baptism, it is a kind of entrance and, as it were, initiation into the Church, by which we are numbered among the people of God; a sign of our spiritual regeneration, by which we are born again to be children of God. The Supper, on the contrary, is intended for those of riper years, who, having passed the tender stage of infancy, are able to bear solid food. This distinction is very clearly pointed out in Scripture. For there, as far as baptism is concerned, the Lord makes no selection of age, whereas he does not admit all to partake of the Supper, but limits it to those who are able to discern the body and blood of the Lord, to examine their own conscience, to show forth the Lord's death, and to understand its power. Could we wish for anything clearer than what the apostle says when he urges, "Let a man examine himself, and so let him eat of that bread, and drink of that cup" (1 Corinthians 11:28)? Examination, therefore, must come first, and it would be vain to expect this from infants. Again: "He that eateth and drinketh unworthily, eateth and drinketh damnation to himself, not discerning the Lord's body." If they cannot partake worthily without being able rightly to discern the holiness of the Lord's body, why should we hold out poison to our young children instead of life-giving food? Then what is our Lord's command? "Do this in remembrance of me." And what is the inference the apostle draws from this? "As often as ye eat this bread, and drink this cup, ye do show the Lord's death till he come." How, I ask, can we require infants to commemorate an event of which they have no understanding; how can we require them "to show forth the Lord's death," of whose nature and benefit they have no idea? Nothing of the kind is prescribed by baptism. Therefore there is the greatest difference between the two signs. We observe this also in similar signs under the old dispensation. Circumcision, which, as is well known, corresponds to our baptism, was intended for infants; but the Passover, in whose place the Supper is put, did not admit all kinds of guests indiscriminately, but was rightly eaten only by those who were old enough to ask its meaning (Exodus 12:26). If these men had the least particle of soundness in their brains, would they be so blind about a matter so very clear and obvious?

31¶

Though I am unwilling to weary the reader with the string of fancies that Servetus, not the least among the Anabaptists, indeed the great glory of this crew, when girding himself for battle, thought to be plausible arguments when he brought them forward, it will be worthwhile to dispose of them briefly. French: "Combien qu'il me fasche d'amasser tant de reveries frivoles que pourront ennuyer les lecteurs, toutesfeis pource que Servet, se meslant aussi de mesdire du baptesme des petis enfans, a cuide amener de fort belles raisons, il sera raison de les rabattre brievement." That is: Although I am sorry to heap up so many frivolous fancies that may weary the reader, yet since Servetus, taking it upon himself to slander infant baptism also, has thought he was bringing forward very fine arguments, it will be right to dispose of them briefly.

32¶

No sound man, I suppose, can now doubt how recklessly the Church is disturbed by those who stir up quarrels and disturbances over infant baptism. For it is important to observe what Satan intends by all this cunning, namely, to rob us of the singular blessing of confidence and spiritual joy that is to be drawn from it, and to that extent to detract from the glory of the divine goodness. For how sweet it is to godly minds to be assured, not only by word but even by visible demonstration, that they stand so high in the favor of their heavenly Father that he concerns himself with the welfare of their descendants! Here we may see how he acts toward us as a most provident parent, not ceasing to care for us even after our death, but considering and providing for our children. Ought not our whole heart to be stirred within us, as David's was (Psalm 48:11), to bless his name for such a display of goodness? Doubtless Satan's design in attacking infant baptism with all his forces is to keep out of sight, and gradually to erase, that testimony of divine grace which the promise itself sets before our eyes. In this way, not only would men be wickedly ungrateful for the mercy of God, but they would be less careful in training their children in godliness. For it is no slight spur to us to bring them up in the fear of God and the observance of his law when we reflect that from their birth they have been regarded and acknowledged by him as his children. Therefore, if we would not maliciously obscure the kindness of God, let us present to him our infants, to whom he has assigned a place among his friends and household, that is, among the members of the Church.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin defends the baptism of believers' children as no mere custom but a sign rooted in the covenant promise — the same promise that ran to Abraham and his seed now sealed under a better administration, for the God who said he would be a God to us and to our children has not grown stingier under the gospel. A word for 2026, offered with Reformed Baptist conviction, presses Calvin's covenantal heart even where we read the sign's recipients differently: the child in a believing home is no stranger to the promises, but is set within the reach of grace, surrounded by prayer, catechized from the cradle, claimed for Christ in hope. Whatever the timing of the water, do not treat your children as outsiders to the covenant of grace — bring them under the Word, teach them the promise is held out to them, plead with God for their hearts. Discipleship begins at the family table; raise them expecting their Redeemer to keep his word.
