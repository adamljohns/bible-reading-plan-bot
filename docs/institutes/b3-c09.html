Book 3 · Chapter 9 · USMC modern English

Of Meditating on the Future Life

The three divisions of this chapter,—I. The principal use of the cross is, that it in various ways accustoms us to despise the present, and excites us to aspire to the future life, sec. 1, 2. II. In withdrawing from the present life we must neither shun it nor feel hatred for it; but desiring the future life, gladly quit the present at the command of our sovereign Master, sec. 3, 4. III. Our infirmity in dreading death described. The correction and safe remedy, sec. 6. 1. The design of God in afflicting his people. 1. To accustom us to despise the present life. Our infatuated love of it. Afflictions employed as the cure. 2. To lead us to aspire to heaven. 2. Excessive love of the present life prevents us from duly aspiring to the other. Hence the disadvantages of prosperity. Blindness of the human judgment. Our philosophizing on the vanity of life only of momentary influence. The necessity of the cross. 3. The present life an evidence of the divine favour to his people; and therefore, not to be detested. On the contrary, should call forth thanksgiving. The crown of victory in heaven after the contest on earth. 4. Weariness of the present life how to be tempered. The believer’s estimate of life. Comparison of the present and the future life. How far the present life should be hated. 5. Christians should not tremble at the fear of death. Two reasons. Objection. Answer. Other reasons. 6. Reasons continued. Conclusion.

1¶

Whatever kind of tribulation afflicts us, we should always consider its end to be this: that we may be trained to despise the present life and thereby stirred up to aspire to the future life. For since God knows well how strongly we are inclined by nature to a slavish love of this world, he uses the fittest means to call us back and shake off our lethargy, so as to keep us from clinging to it too strongly. Every one of us, indeed, wants to be thought to aspire to and aim at heavenly immortality throughout the whole course of his life. For we would be ashamed not to excel the lower animals in some respect, and their condition would be no worse than ours if we had no hope of immortality beyond the grave. But when you look at the plans, wishes, and actions of each man, you see nothing in them but the earth. Hence our stupidity: our minds are so dazzled by the glare of wealth, power, and honors that they can see no farther. The heart also, taken up with avarice, ambition, and lust, is weighed down and cannot rise above them. In short, the whole soul, ensnared by the allurements of the flesh, seeks its happiness on the earth. To meet this disease, the Lord makes his people aware of the vanity of the present life by constant proof of its miseries. So, that they may not promise themselves deep and lasting peace in it, he often allows them to be assailed by war, tumult, or plunder, or to be disturbed by other injuries. That they may not long too eagerly for fleeting and fading riches, or rest in those they already possess, he reduces them to want, or at least restricts them to a modest portion, at one time by exile, at another by barren harvests, at another by fire, or by other means. That they may not take too much complacent delight in the advantages of married life, he either troubles them with the misconduct of their partners, or humbles them by the wickedness of their children, or afflicts them with bereavement. But if he is indulgent to them in all these things, then, lest they should swell with vainglory or be lifted up with confidence, he sets plainly before them, by diseases and dangers, how unstable and fleeting are all the advantages that belong to mortals. We profit rightly by the discipline of the cross when we learn that this life, judged in itself, is restless, troubled, wretched in countless ways, and plainly in no respect happy; and that what are counted its blessings are uncertain, fleeting, vain, and corrupted by a great mixture of evil. From this we conclude that all we have to look for or hope for here is conflict, and that when we think of the crown, we must raise our eyes to heaven. For we must hold that our mind never rises in earnest to desire and aspire after the future until it has learned to despise the present life.

2¶

For there is no middle ground between the two: either the earth must be worthless in our estimation, or it will hold us enslaved by an intemperate love of it. Therefore, if we have any regard for eternity, we must strive diligently to free ourselves from these fetters. Moreover, since the present life has many enticements to allure us, and a great show of delight, grace, and sweetness to soothe us, it is of great importance to us to be called away from its charms now and then. The French reads, "Or pource que la vie presente a tousiours force de delices pour nous attraire, et a grande apparence d'amenité, de grace et de douceur pour nous amieller, il nous est bien mestier d'estre retiré d'heure en d'heure, à ce que nous ne soyons point abusez, et comme ensorcelez de telles flatteries" — Now because the present life always has a host of delights to attract us, and a great appearance of pleasantness, grace, and sweetness to entice us, it is of great importance to us to be withdrawn hour by hour, so that we may not be deceived and, as it were, bewitched by such flattery.

Latin, "Animal esseεφἡμερον" — is an ephemeral animal.

3¶

Still, the contempt which believers should train themselves to feel for the present life must not be of a kind that breeds hatred of it or ingratitude to God. This life, though it abounds in every kind of wretchedness, is rightly counted among the divine blessings that are not to be despised. Therefore, if we do not recognize the kindness of God in it, we are guilty of no small ingratitude toward him. To believers especially it ought to be a proof of divine benevolence, since it is wholly designed to promote their salvation. Before openly displaying the inheritance of eternal glory, God is pleased to show himself to us as a Father by lesser proofs — namely, the blessings he bestows on us every day. Therefore, since this life serves to acquaint us with the goodness of God, shall we disdain it as if it contained not one particle of good? We ought, then, to feel toward it and regard it in such a way as to place it among those gifts of divine kindness which are by no means to be despised. Even if there were no proofs in Scripture (and they are most numerous and clear), nature herself urges us to give thanks to God for having brought us into the light, granted us the use of it, and given us all the means needed for its preservation. And there is a much higher reason when we reflect that here we are, in a sense, being prepared for the glory of the heavenly kingdom. For the Lord has ordained that those who are at last to be crowned in heaven must first carry on a warfare on earth, so that they may not triumph before they have overcome the difficulties of war and won the victory. Another reason is that here we begin to taste in various ways the divine kindness, so that our hope and desire may be sharpened for its full display. Once we have concluded that our earthly life is a gift of the divine mercy, which, as we are bound to, we ought to remember with gratitude, we shall then rightly go on to consider its most wretched condition, and so escape from that excessive fondness for it to which, as I have said, we are naturally prone.

4¶

In proportion as this improper love diminishes, our desire for a better life should increase. I confess, indeed, that a most accurate opinion was formed by those who thought that the best thing was not to be born, and the next best to die early. For, being without the light of God and of true religion, what could they see in it that was not of dire and evil omen? Nor was it unreasonable for those — the French reads, "Le peuple des Scythes" — the Scythians.

5¶

But, strangest of all, many who boast of being Christians, instead of longing for death in this way, are so afraid of it that they tremble at the very mention of it as something ominous and dreadful. We cannot wonder, indeed, that our natural feelings should be somewhat shaken at the mention of our dissolution. But it is altogether intolerable that the light of godliness should not be strong enough in a Christian breast to overcome and suppress that fear with a greater comfort. For if we reflect that this tabernacle of ours, unstable, defective, corruptible, fading, wasting, and rotting, is dissolved in order that it may at once be renewed in sure, perfect, incorruptible, in short, heavenly glory, will not faith compel us eagerly to desire what nature dreads? If we reflect that by death we are called back from exile to live in our native country, a heavenly country, shall this give us no comfort? But, it is said, everything longs for permanent existence. I admit this, and for that very reason I contend that we ought to look to future immortality, where we may obtain that settled condition which nowhere appears on earth. For Paul admirably bids believers hasten cheerfully to death, not because they "would be unclothed, but clothed upon" (2 Corinthians 5:2). Shall the lower animals, and even inanimate creatures, wood and stone themselves, as if conscious of their present vanity, long for the final resurrection, so that they may be delivered from vanity with the sons of God (Romans 8:19); and shall we, endowed with the light of understanding, and, more than understanding, enlightened by the Spirit of God, rise no higher than the corruption of this earth when our very being is in question? But it is not my purpose, nor is this the place, to argue against this great perversity. At the outset I declared that I had no wish to engage in a lengthy discussion of commonplaces. My advice to those whose minds are timid in this way is to read Cyprian's short treatise De Mortalitate, unless it better suits what they deserve to send them to the philosophers, so that by looking at what they say on the contempt of death they may begin to blush. Let us, however, hold this as fixed: no man has made much progress in the school of Christ who does not look forward with joy to the day of death and of the final resurrection (2 Timothy 4:18; Titus 2:13); for Paul distinguishes all believers by this mark, and it is the usual course of Scripture to direct us there whenever it would furnish us with grounds for solid joy. "Look up," says our Lord, "and lift up your heads: for your redemption draweth nigh" (Luke 21:28). Is it reasonable, I ask, that what he meant to have a powerful effect in stirring us up to eagerness and exultation should produce nothing but sadness and dismay? If so, why do we still glory in him as our Master? Therefore let us come to a sounder mind, and, however much the blind and stupid longing of the flesh may resist, let us not hesitate to desire the coming of the Lord, not in wish only but with earnest sighs, as the most happy of all events. He will come as a Redeemer to deliver us from an immense abyss of evil and misery, and to lead us to the blessed inheritance of his life and glory.

6¶

So indeed it is: the whole body of the faithful, so long as they live on the earth, must be like sheep for the slaughter, so that they may be conformed to Christ their Head (Romans 8:36). Their situation would therefore be most pitiable if they did not, by raising their minds to heaven, rise above all that is in the world and above the present appearance of things (1 Corinthians 15:19). On the other hand, once they have lifted their heads above all earthly things, then even though they see the wicked flourishing in wealth and honor, enjoying deep peace, indulging in luxury and splendor, and reveling in every kind of delight, and even though they themselves should be wickedly attacked by them, suffer insult from their pride, be robbed by their avarice, or be assailed by any other passion of theirs, they will have no difficulty in holding up under these evils. They will turn their eyes to that day (Isaiah 25:8; Revelation 7:17) on which the Lord will receive his faithful servants, wipe away all tears from their eyes, clothe them in a robe of glory and joy, feed them with the inexpressible sweetness of his delights, and raise them to share with him in his greatness; in short, admit them to a share in his happiness. But the wicked who may have flourished on the earth he will cast out in utter disgrace; he will change their delights into torments, their laughter and joy into wailing and gnashing of teeth, their peace into the gnawing of conscience, and he will punish their luxury with unquenchable fire. He will also put their necks under the feet of the godly, whose patience they abused. For, as Paul declares, "it is a righteous thing with God to recompense tribulation to them that trouble you; and to you who are troubled rest with us, when the Lord Jesus shall be revealed from heaven" (2 Thessalonians 1:6, 7). This, indeed, is our only comfort; without it we must either give way to despondency or, to our own destruction, turn to the vain comforts of the world. The Psalmist confesses, "My feet were almost gone: my steps had well nigh slipt: for I was envious at the foolish when I saw the prosperity of the wicked" (Psalm 73:3, 4); and he found no resting place until he entered the sanctuary and considered the latter end of the righteous and of the wicked. To conclude in a word: the cross of Christ triumphs in the hearts of believers over the devil and the flesh, over sin and sinners, only when their eyes are directed to the power of his resurrection.

The cross does its deepest work, Calvin teaches, by loosening our grip on a world we love far too slavishly and lifting our eyes to the life to come — yet without breeding contempt for this life, which is itself a gift and a daily proof of the Father's kindness, the training-ground of warfare before the crown. The hard balance he strikes is the one our age cannot hold: it either worships the present life or, in despair, hates it. Teach the household both halves — gratitude for breath and bread and the ten thousand mercies of an ordinary day, and a steady aspiration to the country that does not fade. Strange, Calvin says, that those who name Christ should tremble at death as the worst of omens; no one has learned much in his school who cannot look toward that day with joy. When the wicked flourish and the faithful are pressed, lift your head — the Lord will wipe away every tear. Live, then, as a pilgrim: thankful for the road, longing for home.
