Of the Modes of Supplementing Satisfaction — Indulgences and Purgatory

A profane love of filthy lucre on the part of the Pope. The origin of indulgences unfolded, sec. 5. III. An examination of Popish purgatory. Its horrible impiety, sec. 6. 1¶

From this dogma of satisfaction arises that of indulgences. For the pretense is that what is lacking in our own ability is supplied by them; and they go to the insane length of defining them as a dispensing of the merits of Christ and the martyrs, which the Pope makes by his bulls. Though they are fitter for hellebore than for argument, and though it is scarcely worthwhile to refute these frivolous errors, which, already battered down, are beginning of their own accord to grow obsolete and totter toward their fall, yet, since a brief refutation may be useful to some of the unlearned, I will not leave it out. Indeed, the fact that indulgences have stood so long safe and unpunished, and have run riot with so much fury and tyranny, may be taken as proof of how deep a night of ignorance mankind was plunged into for some ages. Men saw themselves mocked openly and without disguise by the Pope and his bull-bearers; they saw the salvation of the soul made the object of a profitable traffic, salvation priced at a few coins, nothing given freely; they saw what was squeezed from them in the form of offerings shamefully spent on harlots, pimps, and gluttony, the loudest trumpeters of indulgences being their greatest despisers; they saw the monster stalking abroad and every day running riot with greater license, and that without end, new bulls constantly being issued and new sums extracted. Still indulgences were received with the greatest reverence, worshiped, and bought. Even those who saw more clearly than others regarded them as pious frauds, by which some good was gained even in deceiving. Now at last, when a considerable part of the world has begun to come to its senses, indulgences are cooling, and little by little they are even beginning to freeze, in preparation for their final extinction.

2¶

But since a great many who see the vile imposture, theft, and plunder (with which the dealers in indulgences have until now deceived us and made sport of us) are not aware of the true source of the ungodliness, it may be proper to show not only what indulgences truly are, but also that they are polluted in every part. French, "Il est expedient de monstrer ici non seulement quelles sont les indul grences, comme ils en usent; mais du tout que c'est, à les prendre en leur propre et meilleure nature, sans quelque qualité ou vice accidental;" it is expedient here to show not only what indulgences are as they are used, but what they are in themselves, taking them in their proper and best form, without any qualification or incidental vice.

3¶

There is an admirable passage against their blasphemies in Leo, a Roman Bishop (To the Palestinians, Epistle 81): "Although the death of many saints was precious in the sight of the Lord (Psalm 116:15), yet no innocent man's slaughter was the propitiation of the world. The just received crowns; they did not give them; and the fortitude of believers produced examples of patience, not gifts of righteousness. For their deaths were for themselves; and none by his own end paid the debt of another, except Christ our Lord, in whom alone all are crucified, all dead, buried, and raised up." Because this saying was so memorable, he has repeated it elsewhere (Epistle 95). Certainly one could not wish for a clearer refutation of this ungodly dogma. Augustine brings in the same thought no less fittingly: "Although brethren die for brethren, yet no martyr's blood is shed for the remission of sins. This Christ did for us, and in doing it he conferred on us not something we should imitate, but something that should make us grateful" (Augustine, Tractates on John, 84). Again, in another passage: "As he alone became the Son of God and the Son of man, that he might make us with himself sons of God, so he alone, without any ill desert, took on himself the penalty for us, that through him we might, without good desert, obtain undeserved favor" (To Boniface, book 4, chapter 4). Indeed, while their whole doctrine is a patchwork of sacrilege and blasphemy, this is the most blasphemous part of all. Let them say whether or not they hold the following dogmas: that the martyrs by their death performed more for God, and merited more, than was necessary for themselves, and that they have a large surplus of merits which may be applied to others; that, so that this great good may not go to waste, their blood is mingled with the blood of Christ, and out of both is formed the treasury of the Church, for the forgiveness and satisfaction of sins; and that in this sense we must understand the words of Paul: "Who now rejoice in my sufferings, and fill up that which is behind of the afflictions of Christ in my flesh for his body's sake, which is the Church" (Colossians 1:24). What is this but to leave Christ the name only, and at the same time make him a common little saint who can scarcely be picked out of the crowd? He alone ought to be preached, he alone held forth, he alone named, he alone looked to, whenever the subject under consideration is how forgiveness of sins, expiation, and sanctification are obtained. But let us hear their propositions. So that the blood of the martyrs may not be shed without fruit, it must be put to use for the common good of the Church. Is that so? Was there no fruit in glorifying God by their death? In sealing his truth with their blood? In testifying, by their contempt of the present life, that they looked for a better one? In confirming the faith of the Church, and at the same time breaking the stubbornness of the enemy by their constancy? But this is how it is: they acknowledge no fruit if Christ is the only propitiation, if he alone died for our sins, if he alone was offered for our redemption. Nevertheless, they say, Peter and Paul would have gained the crown of victory even if they had died a natural death in their beds. But since they contended even to blood, it would not agree with the justice of God to leave this barren and unfruitful. As if God were unable to increase the glory of his servants in proportion to the measure of his gifts! The benefit the Church receives in common is great enough when she is fired with zeal for the fight by their triumphs.

4¶

How maliciously they twist the passage in which Paul says that he fills up in his body what was lacking in the sufferings of Christ! (Colossians 1:24). That lack, or filling up, refers not to the work of redemption, satisfaction, or expiation, but to those afflictions with which the members of Christ, that is, all believers, must be exercised as long as they are in the flesh. He says, therefore, that part of the sufferings of Christ still remains, namely, that what he suffered in himself he suffers daily in his members. Christ so honors us as to regard and count our afflictions as his own. By the added words, for the Church, Paul means not for the redemption or reconciliation or satisfaction of the Church, but for its edification and progress. As he says elsewhere, "I endure all things for the elect's sakes, that they may also obtain the salvation which is in Christ Jesus with eternal glory" (2 Timothy 2:10). He also writes to the Corinthians: "Whether we be afflicted, it is for your consolation and salvation, which is effectual in the enduring of the same sufferings which we also suffer" (2 Corinthians 1:6). In the same place he immediately explains his meaning by adding that he was made a minister of the Church, not for redemption, but according to the stewardship he received to preach the gospel of Christ. But if they still want another interpreter, let them hear Augustine: "The sufferings of Christ are in Christ alone, as in the head; in Christ and the Church, as in the whole body. Hence Paul, being one member, says, 'I fill up in my body that which is behind of the sufferings of Christ.' Therefore, O hearers, whoever you are, if you are among the members of Christ, whatever you suffer from those who are not members of Christ was lacking to the sufferings of Christ" (Augustine, On Psalm 16). Elsewhere he explains the purpose of the sufferings the Apostles undertook for Christ: "Christ is my door to you, because ye are the sheep of Christ purchased by his blood: acknowledge your price, which is not paid by me, but preached by me" (Augustine, Tractates on John, 47). He afterward adds, "As he laid down his life, so ought we to lay down our lives for the brethren, to build up peace and maintain faith." So far Augustine. Far be it from us to imagine that Paul thought anything was lacking to the sufferings of Christ with respect to the complete fullness of righteousness, salvation, and life, or that he wished to add anything to it, after showing so clearly and eloquently that the grace of Christ was poured out in such rich abundance as far to exceed all the power of sin (Romans 5:15). All the saints have been saved by it alone, not by the merit of their own life or death, as Peter plainly testifies (Acts 15:11); so that it is an insult to God and his Anointed to place the worthiness of any saint in anything except the mercy of God alone. But why dwell on this any longer, as if the matter were obscure, when to mention these monstrous dogmas is to refute them?

5¶

Moreover, to say nothing of these abominations, who taught the Pope to enclose in lead and parchment the grace of Jesus Christ, which the Lord is pleased to dispense by the word of the Gospel? Undoubtedly either the Gospel of God or indulgences must be false. That Christ is offered to us in the Gospel with all the abundance of heavenly blessings, with all his merits, all his righteousness, wisdom, and grace, without exception, Paul bears witness when he says, "Now then we are ambassadors for Christ, as though God did beseech you by us: we pray you in Christ's stead, be ye reconciled to God. For he has made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him" (2 Corinthians 5:20, 21). And what is meant by the fellowship (κοινονία) of Christ, which according to the same Apostle (1 Corinthians 1:9) is offered to us in the Gospel, all believers know. Indulgences, on the contrary, bringing out some portion of the grace of God from the Pope's armory, fasten it to lead, parchment, and a particular place, but tear it away from the word of God. When we inquire into the origin of this abuse, it appears to have arisen from this: when in old times the satisfactions imposed on penitents were too severe to be borne, those who felt burdened beyond measure by the penance laid on them petitioned the Church for relief. The remission given in this way was called an indulgence. But as they transferred satisfactions to God, and called them compensations by which men redeem themselves from the justice of God, so in the same way they transferred indulgences, representing them as expiatory remedies that free us from deserved punishment. The blasphemies we have mentioned have been invented with such effrontery that there is not the least pretext for them.

6¶

Their purgatory cannot now give us much trouble, since with this ax we have struck it, thrown it down, and overturned it from its very foundations. I cannot agree with some who think that we ought to keep quiet on this matter and make no mention of purgatory, from which (as they say) fierce disputes arise while very little edification can be gained. I myself would think it right to ignore their follies if they did not lead to serious consequences. But since purgatory has been built up on many blasphemies, and is daily propped up by new ones, and since it produces many grievous offenses, it certainly is not to be overlooked, however it might have been disguised for a time, that it was devised, without any authority from the word of God, by prying and audacious rashness; that credit was won for it by fictitious revelations, the wiles of Satan; and that certain passages of Scripture were ignorantly twisted to support it. And yet the Lord does not allow human presumption to force its way in this manner into the hidden recesses of his judgments; he has strictly forbidden us to neglect his voice and to make inquiry of the dead (Deuteronomy 18:11), and he does not permit his word to be so erroneously corrupted. Let us grant, however, that all this might have been tolerated for a time as a thing of no great importance; yet when the expiation of sins is sought elsewhere than in the blood of Christ, and satisfaction is transferred to others, silence would be most perilous. We are bound, therefore, to raise our voice to its highest pitch and cry aloud that purgatory is a deadly device of Satan; that it makes the cross of Christ void; that it offers intolerable insult to the divine mercy; that it undermines and overthrows our faith. For what is this purgatory but the satisfaction for sin paid after death by the souls of the dead? Hence, once this idea of satisfaction is refuted, purgatory itself is at once completely overturned. French, "Tellement que si on ote la fantasie de satisfaire, leur purgatorie s'en va bas;" so that if the fancy of satisfying is taken away, down goes their purgatory.

7¶

Those passages of Scripture to which they are accustomed to fasten falsely and wickedly, it may be worthwhile to wrench out of their hands. Matthew 12:32; Mark 3:28; Luke 12:10; Matthew 5:25.

The French adds the following sentence: "Brief, que le passage soit regardé et prins en sa simple intelligence, et il n'y sera rien trouvé de ce qu'ils pretendent;" in short, let the passage be looked at and taken in its simple meaning, and nothing of what they claim will be found in it.

8¶

They look for an argument in the passage where Paul declares that all things shall bow the knee to Christ, "things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth" (Philippians 2:10). They take it for granted that "things under the earth" cannot mean those who are doomed to eternal damnation, and that the only conclusion remaining is that they must be souls suffering in purgatory. They would not reason very badly if by the bending of the knee the Apostle meant true worship; but since he simply says that Christ has received a dominion to which all creatures are subject, what prevents us from understanding those "under the earth" to mean the devils, who will certainly be made to appear before the judgment seat of God, there to acknowledge their Judge with fear and trembling? In this way Paul himself elsewhere interprets the same prophecy: "We shall all stand before the judgment-seat of Christ. For it is written, As I live, saith the Lord, every knee shall bow to me, and every tongue shall confess to God" (Romans 14:10, 11). But, they say, we cannot interpret in this way what is said in the Apocalypse: "Every creature which is in heaven, and on the earth, and under the earth, and such as are in the sea, heard I saying, Blessing, and honor, and glory, and power, be unto him that sitteth upon the throne, and unto the Lamb, for ever and ever" (Revelation 5:13). This I readily admit; but what kinds of creatures do they suppose are listed here? It is absolutely certain that both irrational and inanimate creatures are included. All that is affirmed, then, is that every part of the universe, from the highest summit of heaven to the very center of the earth, proclaims in its own way the glory of the Creator.

To the passage they bring forward from the history of the Maccabees (1 Maccabees 12:43), I will not stoop to reply, lest I should seem to count that work among the canonical books. But Augustine. See Augustine, Against the Second Letter of Gaudentius, chapter 23.

9¶

But, it seems, they find in Paul an invincible support, which cannot be so easily overthrown. His words are, "Now if any man build upon this foundation gold, silver, precious stones, wood, hay, stubble; every man's work shall be made manifest: for the day shall declare it, because it shall be revealed by fire; and the fire shall try every man's work of what sort it is. If any man's work shall be burnt, he shall suffer loss: but he himself shall be saved; yet so as by fire" (1 Corinthians 3:12-15). What fire (they ask) can that be but the fire of purgatory, by which the defilements of sin are wiped away so that we may enter pure into the kingdom of God? But most of the Fathers. Chrysostom, Augustine, and others; see Augustine, Enchiridion to Laurentius, chapter 68.

The French adds, "auquel nombre universel sont enclos les Apostres;" in which universal number the Apostles are included.

French, "l'exposition que font aujourdhui nos adversaires;" the interpretation which our opponents give today.

10¶

But, they say, the observance of it in the Church is of the highest antiquity. This objection is answered by Paul when, including even his own age in the sentence, he declares that all who in building the Church have laid on it something that does not agree with the foundation must suffer the loss of their work. When, therefore, my opponents object that it has been the practice for thirteen hundred years to offer prayers for the dead, I ask them in return by what word of God, by what revelation, by what example it was done. For here not only are passages of Scripture lacking, but in the examples of all the saints of whom we read, nothing of the kind is seen. We have numerous, and sometimes long, accounts of their mourning and burial rites, but not one word is said of prayers. French, "L'Escriture raconte souventesfois et bien au long, comment les fideles ont pleuré la mort de leurs parens, et comment ils les ont ensevelis; mais qu'ils ayent prié plour eux, il n'en est nouvelles;" Scripture relates many times and at great length how the faithful mourned the death of their relatives and how they buried them; but that they prayed for them is never even hinted at.

Received custom, too, was a kind of torch by which the minds of many were set on fire. We know that among all the Gentiles, and in all ages, certain rites were paid to the dead, and that every year purifications were performed for their departed spirits. Although Satan deceived foolish mortals by these impostures, yet the means of deceiving were borrowed from a sound principle, namely, that death is not destruction, but a passage from this life to another. And there can be no doubt that superstition itself always left the Gentiles without excuse before the judgment seat of God, because they neglected to prepare for that future life which they professed to believe. So, that Christians might not seem worse than heathens, they were ashamed to pay no duty to the dead, as if the dead had been utterly annihilated. Hence their ill-advised diligence; for they thought they would expose themselves to great disgrace if they were slow in providing funeral feasts and offerings. What was introduced in this way through perverse rivalry kept receiving new additions, until the highest holiness of the Papacy consisted in giving help to the suffering dead. But Scripture furnishes far better and more solid comfort when it declares, "Blessed are the dead that die in the Lord," and adds the reason, "for they rest from their labors" (Revelation 14:13). We ought not to indulge our love so far as to set up a perverse form of prayer in the Church. Surely anyone with the least prudence easily sees that whatever we meet with on this subject in ancient writers was in deference to public custom and the ignorance of the common people. I admit that they themselves were also carried away into error, since the usual effect of rash credulity is to destroy judgment. Meanwhile the passages themselves show that when they recommended prayer for the dead, it was with hesitation. Augustine relates in his Confessions that his mother, Monica, earnestly asked to be remembered when the solemn rites at the altar were performed; no doubt an old woman's wish, which her son did not bring to the test of Scripture, but out of natural affection wished others to approve. His book De Cura pro Mortals Agenda, On Showing Care for the Dead, is so full of doubt that its coldness may well put out the heat of a foolish zeal. If anyone, while pretending to be a patron of the dead, deals merely in probabilities, the only effect will be to make indifferent those who were formerly anxious. French, "Le liure qu'il à composé tout expres de cest argument, et qu'il a intitule. Du soin pour les morts, est envellopée en tant de doutes, qu'il doit suffire pour refroidir ceux qui y auroyent devotion; pour le moins en voyant qu'il ne s'aide que de conjectures bien legeres et foibles, on verra qu'on ne se doit point fort empescher d'une chose o˜ il n'y a nulle importance;" the book which he composed expressly on this subject, and which he entitled Of Care for the Dead, is wrapped in so many doubts that it should be enough to cool those who are devoted to the practice; at least, since he supports his view only by very slight and feeble conjectures, it will be seen that we ought not to trouble ourselves much over a matter of no importance.

The only support of this dogma is that, since a custom of praying for the dead prevailed, the duty ought not to be despised. But granting that ancient church writers thought it a pious thing to help the dead, the rule that can never deceive must always be observed, namely, that we must not bring anything of our own into our prayers, but must keep all our wishes subordinate to the word of God, because it belongs to him to prescribe what he wishes us to ask. Now, since the whole Law and Gospel do not contain one syllable that supports the right of praying for the dead, it is a profanation of prayer to go one step further than God commands. But, lest our opponents boast that they share their error with the ancient Church, I say that there is a wide difference between the two. The latter made a commemoration of the dead so as not to seem to have cast off all concern for them; but at the same time they acknowledged that they were in doubt about their state; certainly they made no such assertion about purgatory as would imply that they did not hold it to be uncertain. The former insist that their dream of purgatory be received without question as an article of faith. The latter only sparingly and in a perfunctory way commended their dead to the Lord in the communion of the holy supper. The former are constantly urging the care of the dead, and by their importunate preaching of it make out that it is to be preferred to all the duties of charity. But it would not be difficult for us to produce some passages from ancient writers. See Augustine, Homilies on John, 49; The City of God, book 21, chapters 13-24.

The French of the latter clause of this sentence is "et toutesfois il y aura matiere assez ample de les pourmener en cette campagne, veu qu'ils n'ont nulle couleur pour jamais;" and yet there is ample room to drive them across this field, seeing that they have no color of excuse, but must be convicted of being the most villainous deceivers that ever were.

Indulgences and purgatory, Calvin argues, are the bitter fruit of one error — that Christ's satisfaction was incomplete and must be supplemented by our payments, even beyond the grave. He answers with the whole Gospel: Christ alone is propitiation, expiation, and satisfaction; to seek the cleansing of sin anywhere but in his blood is to make void the cross and insult the divine mercy. We do not market grace in lead and parchment, nor consign the dead to a fire Scripture never names — for the dead who die in the Lord rest from their labors. For the believer in 2026 the open trafficking is gone, but the instinct survives wherever we imagine our suffering or our giving buys what only Christ secured. Hold every custom, however ancient or comforting, to the word of God, and refuse to go one step beyond what he enjoins. Let your hope at the graveside be plain and unshaken: it is finished, and those who sleep in Christ are already at rest.
