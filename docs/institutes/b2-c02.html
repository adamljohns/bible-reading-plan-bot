Man Now Deprived of Freedom of Will, and Miserably Enslaved

Having in the first chapter treated of the fall of man, and the corruption of the human race, it becomes necessary to inquire, Whether the sons of Adam are deprived of all liberty; and if any particle of liberty remains, how far its power extends? The four next chapters are devoted to this question. This second chapter may be reduced to three general heads: I. The foundation of the whole discussion. II. The opinions of others on the subject of human freedom, see. 2–9. III. The true doctrine on the subject, see. 10–27.

1¶

Having seen that the dominion of sin, ever since the first man was brought under it, not only extends to the whole race but has complete possession of every soul, it now remains to consider more closely whether, from the time we were thus enslaved, we have been deprived of all liberty; and, if any portion of it still remains, how far its power extends. To make the answer to this question easier, it may be well in passing to point out the course our inquiry ought to take. The best way of avoiding error is to consider the dangers that beset us on either side. When man is stripped of all uprightness, he at once takes the fact as an occasion for indulging in sloth; and because he has no ability in himself for the pursuit of righteousness, he treats the whole subject as if it did not concern him. On the other hand, man cannot claim anything for himself, however small, without robbing God of his honor and, through rash confidence, exposing himself to a fall. To keep clear of both these rocks, See Calvin, Against the Theologians of Paris, Article 2. These two rocks are pointed out by Augustine, Epistle 47, and On John, chapter 12.

The French is, "Laquelle toutefois nous cognoistrons etre très-utile et qui plue est, etre un des fondemens de la religion;" — which, however, we shall know to be very useful, and what is more, to be one of the fundamentals of religion.

2¶

Having just observed that the faculties of the soul are seated in the mind and the heart, let us now consider how far the power of each extends. Philosophers generally hold that reason dwells in the mind like a lamp, throwing light on all its counsels, and like a queen, governing the will; that it is so filled with divine light as to be able to take counsel for the best, and so endowed with vigor as to be able to command perfectly; that, on the other hand, sense is dull and shortsighted, always creeping on the ground, groveling among lower objects, and never rising to true vision; that the appetite, when it obeys reason and does not allow itself to be subdued by sense, is carried to the pursuit of virtue, holds a straight course, and is transformed into will; but that when it is enslaved by sense, it is corrupted and depraved so as to degenerate into lust. In a word, since in their opinion the faculties I mentioned above, namely, intellect, sense, and appetite, or will (the latter being the term in ordinary use), are seated in the soul, they hold that the intellect is endowed with reason, the best guide to a virtuous and happy life, provided it makes proper use of its excellence and exerts the power with which it is naturally endowed; and that, at the same time, the lower movement, which is called sense, and by which the mind is led away into error and delusion, is of such a nature that it can be tamed and gradually subdued by the power of reason. To the will, moreover, they give a middle place between reason and sense, regarding it as possessing full power and freedom, whether to obey the former or to give itself up to be carried away by the latter.

3¶

Sometimes, indeed, convinced by their own experience, they do not deny how difficult it is for man to establish the rule of reason within himself, since at one time he is enticed by the allurements of pleasure, at another deceived by a false appearance of good, and at another driven by unruly passions and pulled away (to use Plato's expression) as if by ropes or sinews (Plato, De Legibus, book 1). For this reason Cicero says that the sparks given off by nature are immediately put out by false opinions and depraved habits (Cicero, Tusculan Questions, book 3). They confess that once diseases of this kind have seized the mind, their course is too violent to be easily checked, and they do not hesitate to compare them to fiery horses which, having thrown off the charioteer, run wild without restraint. At the same time, they lay it down as beyond dispute that virtue and vice are in our own power. For, they say, if it is in our choice to do this thing or that, it must also be in our choice not to do it; again, if it is in our choice not to act, it must also be in our choice to act; but both in doing and in refraining we seem to act from free choice; and therefore, if we do good when we please, we can also refrain from doing it, and if we commit evil, we can also avoid committing it (Aristotle, Ethics, book 3, chapter 5). Indeed, some have gone so far as to boast (Seneca, throughout) that it is the gift of the gods that we live, but our own doing that we live well and purely. Hence Cicero says, in the person of Cotta, that since everyone acquires virtue for himself, no wise man ever thanked the gods for it. "We are praised," he says, "for virtue, and glory in virtue, but this could not be if virtue were the gift of God, and not from ourselves" (Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods). A little later he adds, "The opinion of all mankind is that fortune must be sought from God, wisdom from ourselves." In short, then, all philosophers maintain that human reason is sufficient for right government; and that the will, which is beneath it, may indeed be solicited to evil by sense, but, having a free choice, there is nothing to prevent it from following reason as its guide in all things.

4¶

Among church writers, although there is none who did not acknowledge that sound reason in man was seriously injured by sin and the will greatly entangled by sinful desires, yet many of them came too close to the philosophers. Some of the most ancient writers seem to me to have exalted human strength out of fear that a plain acknowledgment of its impotence might expose them to the ridicule of the philosophers with whom they were disputing, and might also furnish the flesh, already too much disinclined to good, with a new pretext for sloth. Therefore, to avoid teaching anything that most of mankind might consider absurd, they made it their aim, in some measure, to reconcile the doctrine of Scripture with the dogmas of philosophy, while taking special care not to give any occasion to sloth. This is obvious from their words. Chrysostom says, "God, having placed good and evil in our power, has given us full freedom of choice; he does not hold back the unwilling, but embraces the willing" (Homily on the Betrayal of Judas). Again, "He who is wicked is often, when he so chooses, changed into good, and he who is good falls through sluggishness and becomes wicked. For the Lord has made our nature free. He does not lay us under necessity, but, furnishing suitable remedies, allows the whole to depend on the will of the patient" (Homily 18, on Genesis). Again, "As we can do nothing rightly until we are aided by the grace of God, so, until we bring forward what is our own, we cannot obtain favor from above" (Homily 52). He had said earlier, "Since the whole is not done by divine assistance, we ourselves must of necessity bring something." Accordingly, one of his common sayings is, "Let us bring what is our own; God will supply the rest." In agreement with this, Jerome says, "It is ours to begin, God's to finish; it is ours to offer what we can, his to supply what we cannot" (Dialogue 3 Against the Pelagians).

From these sayings you see that they have granted man more than he possesses for the pursuit of virtue, because they thought they could not shake off our inborn sluggishness unless they argued that we sin by ourselves alone. With what skill they have argued this we shall see later. Surely we shall soon be able to show that the opinions just quoted are most inaccurate. 6¶

All this being granted, it will be beyond dispute that free will does not enable any man to do good works unless he is helped by grace; indeed, by the special grace that the elect alone receive through regeneration. For I do not stop to consider the extravagance of those who say that grace is offered equally and indiscriminately to all (Lombard, book 2, distinction 26). But it has not yet been shown whether man is entirely deprived of the power of doing good, or whether he still possesses it in some degree, though a very feeble and limited one, so feeble and limited that it can do nothing of itself, but when helped by grace is able also to do its part. The Master of the Sentences (Lombard, in the same place), wishing to explain this, teaches that a twofold grace is necessary to fit us for any good work. The one he calls Operating. To it we owe that we effectually will what is good. The other, which follows this good will and aids it, he calls Co-operating. My objection to this division (see below, chapter 3, section 10, and chapter 7, section 9) is that, while it attributes the effectual desire of good to divine grace, it insinuates that man by his own nature desires good in some degree, though ineffectually. So Bernard, while maintaining that a good will is the work of God, concedes this much to man, namely, that of his own nature he longs for such a good will. This differs widely from the view of Augustine, though Lombard claims to have taken the division from him. Besides, there is an ambiguity in the second part of the division, which has led to an erroneous interpretation. For it has been thought that we co-operate with subsequent grace, inasmuch as it rests with us either to nullify the first grace by rejecting it, or to confirm it by obediently yielding to it. The author of the work De Vocatione Gentium puts it this way: those who enjoy the faculty of reason are free to depart from grace, so that not departing is a reward, and what cannot be done without the co-operation of the Spirit is imputed as merit to those whose will might have made it otherwise (book 2, chapter 4). It seemed proper to make these two observations in passing, so that the reader may see how far I differ from the sounder of the Schoolmen. I differ still further from the more modern sophists, who have departed even more widely than the Schoolmen from the ancient doctrine. The division, however, shows in what respect free will is attributed to man. For Lombard finally declares (book 2, distinction 25) that our freedom does not extend to leaving us equally inclined to good and evil in act or in thought, but only to freeing us from compulsion. This liberty is compatible with our being depraved, the slaves of sin, able to do nothing but sin.

7¶

In this way, then, man is said to have free will, not because he has a free choice of good and evil, but because he acts voluntarily and not by compulsion. This is perfectly true; but why should so small a matter have been honored with so proud a title? An admirable freedom indeed! that man is not forced to be the servant of sin, while he is nevertheless ejthelodou'lo" (a voluntary slave), his will being bound by the fetters of sin. I detest mere disputes over words, by which the Church is harassed to no purpose; but I think we ought scrupulously to avoid terms that imply some absurdity, especially in subjects where error has destructive consequences. How few there are who, when they hear free will attributed to man, do not at once imagine that he is master of his mind and will in such a sense that he can of himself turn himself either to good or to evil! It may be said that such dangers are removed by carefully explaining the meaning to the people. But the human mind is so prone to go astray that it will more quickly draw error from one little word than truth from a long discourse. Of this the very term in question furnishes all too strong a proof. For once the explanation given by the ancient Christian writers was lost sight of, almost all who came after them, attending only to the derivation of the term, were led to indulge a fatal confidence.

12¶

I am pleased with the well-known saying, borrowed from the writings of Augustine, that man's natural gifts were corrupted by sin and his supernatural gifts withdrawn; meaning by supernatural gifts the light of faith and righteousness, which would have been sufficient for attaining heavenly life and everlasting happiness. When man withdrew his allegiance from God, he was deprived of the spiritual gifts by which he had been raised to the hope of eternal salvation. It follows that he is now an exile from the kingdom of God, so that all things belonging to the blessed life of the soul are extinguished in him until he recovers them by the grace of regeneration. Among these are faith, love to God, charity toward our neighbor, and the pursuit of righteousness and holiness. All these, when restored to us by Christ, are to be regarded as added from outside and above nature. If so, we infer that they had previously been abolished. On the other hand, soundness of mind and integrity of heart were withdrawn at the same time, and it is this that constitutes the corruption of the natural gifts. For although there is still some remnant of understanding and judgment, as well as of will, we cannot call a mind sound and whole that is both weak and sunk in darkness. As for the will, its depravity is only too well known. Therefore, since reason, by which man distinguishes between good and evil, and by which he understands and judges, is a natural gift, it could not be entirely destroyed; but being partly weakened and partly corrupted, all that remains of it is a shapeless ruin. In this sense it is said (John 1:5) that "the light shineth in darkness, and the darkness comprehended it not"; these words clearly express both points, namely, that in the perverted and degenerate nature of man there are still some sparks which show that he is a rational animal and differs from the brutes, since he is endowed with understanding, and yet that this light is so smothered by clouds of darkness that it cannot shine out to any good effect. In the same way the will, because it is inseparable from the nature of man, did not perish, but was so enslaved by depraved lusts as to be incapable of a single righteous desire. The definition now given is complete, but there are several points that need to be explained. Therefore, following that primary distinction (Book 1, chapter 15, sections 7 and 8) by which we divided the soul into intellect and will, we will now inquire into the power of the intellect.

To charge the intellect with perpetual blindness, so as to leave it no understanding of any kind whatever, is contrary not only to the Word of God but to common experience. We see that there has been implanted in the human mind a certain desire to search out truth, to which it would never aspire unless some taste for truth already existed. There is, therefore, even now in the human mind discernment to this extent, that it is naturally moved by the love of truth, the lack of which in the lower animals is a proof of their gross and irrational nature. Still, it is true that this love of truth fails before it reaches the goal, quickly falling away into vanity. Since the human mind, through its dullness, is unable to keep to the right path of inquiry, and after various wanderings, stumbling every now and then like someone groping in darkness, at last becomes completely bewildered, its whole course of proceeding proves how unfit it is to search for the truth and find it. Then it labors under another serious defect, in that it often fails to discern what knowledge it should strive to acquire. Hence, under the influence of an idle curiosity, it torments itself with superfluous and useless discussions, either not attending at all to the things that need to be known, or casting only a hasty and contemptuous glance at them. At any rate, it hardly ever studies them in sober earnest. Secular writers constantly complain of this perverse way of proceeding, and yet almost all of them are found following it. Hence Solomon, throughout the Book of Ecclesiastes, after listing all the studies in which men think they attain the highest wisdom, pronounces them vain and frivolous.

13¶

Still, man's efforts are not always so utterly fruitless as to lead to no result, especially when his attention is turned to lower objects. Indeed, even with regard to higher objects, though he is more careless in investigating them, he makes some little progress. Here, however, his ability is more limited, and he is never made more aware of his weakness than when he tries to soar above the sphere of the present life. It may therefore be proper, in order to make it plainer how far our ability extends with regard to these two classes of objects, to draw a distinction between them. The distinction is that we have one kind of understanding of earthly things and another of heavenly things. By earthly things I mean those that do not relate to God and his kingdom, to true righteousness and future blessedness, but have some connection with the present life and are in a way confined within its boundaries. By heavenly things I mean the pure knowledge of God, the way of true righteousness, and the mysteries of the heavenly kingdom. To the former belong matters of government and household management, all the mechanical arts, and the liberal studies. To the latter (on which see the eighteenth and following sections) belong the knowledge of God and of his will, and the means of shaping our life in accordance with them. As to the former, the view to be taken is this. Since man is by nature a social animal, he is disposed by natural instinct to cherish and preserve society; and accordingly we see that the minds of all men bear impressions of civil order and honesty. This is why every individual understands that human societies must be regulated by laws, and is also able to grasp the principles of those laws. Hence the universal agreement on such subjects, both among nations and among individuals, since the seeds of them are implanted in the hearts of all without a teacher or lawgiver. The truth of this fact is not affected by the wars and dissensions that immediately arise, when some, such as thieves and robbers, would overturn the rules of justice, loosen the bonds of law, and give free rein to their lust; and when others (a very common vice) think that unjust which is elsewhere regarded as just, and, on the contrary, hold that praiseworthy which is elsewhere forbidden. For such people do not hate the laws because they do not know them to be good and sacred, but, inflamed with headlong passion, they quarrel with what is clearly reasonable and willfully hate what their mind and understanding approve. Quarrels of this latter kind do not destroy the primary idea of justice. For while men dispute with one another about particular enactments, their ideas of equity agree in substance. This, no doubt, proves the weakness of the human mind, which, even when it seems to be on the right path, limps and hesitates. Still, it is true that some principle of civil order is impressed on all. And this is ample proof that, with regard to the ordering of the present life, no man is without the light of reason.

14¶

Next come the manual and liberal arts, in learning which, since all have some degree of aptitude, the full force of human sharpness is displayed. But though not all are equally able to learn all the arts, we have sufficient evidence of a common capacity in the fact that there is hardly an individual who does not show intelligence in some particular art. And this capacity extends not merely to learning the art, but to devising something new, or improving what had been learned before. This led Plato to adopt the mistaken idea that such knowledge was nothing but recollection. The French adds, "de ce que l'ame savoit avant qu'etre mis dedlans le corps;" — of what the soul knew before it was placed within the body.

15¶

Therefore, in reading secular authors, the admirable light of truth displayed in them should remind us that the human mind, however much fallen and perverted from its original integrity, is still adorned and clothed with admirable gifts from its Creator. If we reflect that the Spirit of God is the only fountain of truth, we will be careful, as we would avoid insulting him, not to reject or condemn truth wherever it appears. In despising the gifts, we insult the Giver. How, then, can we deny that truth must have shone on those ancient lawgivers who arranged civil order and discipline with such great equity? Shall we say that the philosophers, in their exquisite researches and skillful descriptions of nature, were blind? Shall we deny understanding to those who drew up rules for discourse and taught us to speak in accordance with reason? Shall we say that those who, by cultivating the art of medicine, spent their labor on our behalf were only raving? What shall we say of the mathematical sciences? Shall we consider them the dreams of madmen? No; we cannot read the writings of the ancients on these subjects without the highest admiration, an admiration that their excellence will not allow us to withhold. But shall we consider anything noble and praiseworthy without tracing it to the hand of God? Far from us be such ingratitude, an ingratitude not chargeable even on the heathen poets, who acknowledged that philosophy and laws and all useful arts were the inventions of the gods. Therefore, since it is evident that men whom the Scriptures call carnal are so sharp and clear-sighted in the investigation of lower things, their example should teach us how many gifts the Lord has left in the possession of human nature, even though it has been stripped of the true good.

16¶

Moreover, let us not forget that there are most excellent blessings that the Divine Spirit dispenses to whom he wills for the common benefit of mankind. For if the skill and knowledge required for building the Tabernacle had to be imparted to Bezaleel and Aholiab by the Spirit of God (Exodus 31:2; 35:30), it is not strange that the knowledge of those things that are of the highest excellence in human life is said to be communicated to us by the Spirit. Nor is there any ground for asking what fellowship the Spirit can have with the ungodly, who are altogether estranged from God. For what is said about the Spirit dwelling in believers only is to be understood of the Spirit of holiness, by whom we are consecrated to God as temples. Notwithstanding this, he fills, moves, and invigorates all things by the power of the Spirit, and does so according to the particular nature that each class of beings has received by the Law of Creation. But if the Lord has been pleased to help us by the work and ministry of the ungodly in physics, logic, mathematics, and other similar sciences, let us make use of it, lest by neglecting the gifts of God freely offered to us we be justly punished for our sloth. But lest anyone should imagine a man to be very happy merely because, with respect to the elements of this world, he has been endowed with great talents for the investigation of truth, we ought to add that the whole power of intellect thus bestowed is, in the sight of God, fleeting and vain whenever it is not based on a solid foundation of truth. Augustine (above, sections 4 and 12), to whom, as we have observed, the Master of the Sentences (book 2, distinction 25) and the Schoolmen are forced to subscribe, says most correctly that, as the free gifts bestowed on man were withdrawn, so the natural gifts that remained were corrupted after the fall. Not that they can be polluted in themselves, insofar as they proceed from God, but that they have ceased to be pure to polluted man, so that he should not by their means obtain any praise.

17¶

The sum of the whole is this. From a general survey of the human race, it appears that one of the essential properties of our nature is reason, which distinguishes us from the lower animals, just as they by means of sense are distinguished from inanimate objects. For although some individuals are born without reason, that defect does not lessen the general kindness of God, but rather serves to remind us that whatever we retain ought rightly to be ascribed to the Divine indulgence. Had God not spared us in this way, our revolt would have carried with it the entire destruction of nature. In that some excel in sharpness and some in judgment, while others have greater readiness in learning some particular art, God by this variety commends his favor toward us, lest anyone should presume to claim for himself what flows from His mere generosity. For why is one more excellent than another, except that in a common nature the grace of God is specially displayed in passing by many, and so proclaiming that it is under obligation to none? We may add that each individual is brought under particular influences according to his calling. Many examples of this occur in the Book of Judges, in which the Spirit of the Lord is said to have come upon those whom he called to govern his people (Judges 6:34). In short, in every distinguished act there is a special inspiration. Thus it is said of Saul that "there went with him a band of men whose hearts the Lord had touched" (1 Samuel 10:26). And when his installation in the kingdom is foretold, Samuel addresses him thus: "The Spirit of the Lord will come upon thee, and thou shalt prophesy with them, and shalt be turned into another man" (1 Samuel 10:6). This extends to the whole course of his government, as it is said later of David, "The Spirit of the Lord came upon David from that day forward" (1 Samuel 16:13). The same thing is said elsewhere with reference to particular movements. Indeed, even in Homer men are said to excel in genius not only according as Jupiter has distributed to each, but according as he leads them day by day, oJion ejp e\ma" a[geisi. And certainly experience shows, when those who were most skillful and ingenious stand stupefied, that the minds of men are entirely under the control of God, who rules them every moment. Hence it is said that "He poureth contempt upon princes, and causeth them to wander in the wilderness where there is no way" (Psalm 107:40). Still, in this diversity we can trace some remains of the divine image distinguishing the whole human race from other creatures.

18¶

We must now explain what the power of human reason is with regard to the kingdom of God and spiritual discernment, which consists chiefly of three things: the knowledge of God, the knowledge of his fatherly favor toward us, which constitutes our salvation, and the way of ordering our conduct in accordance with the Divine Law. With regard to the first two, but more properly the second, men who are otherwise the most ingenious are blinder than moles. I do not deny, indeed, that in the writings of philosophers we occasionally meet with shrewd and apt remarks on the nature of God, though they always have something of a giddy imagination about them. As observed above, the Lord has given them some slight perception of his Godhead, so that they might not plead ignorance as an excuse for their impiety, and has at times moved them to utter some truths, the confession of which should be their own condemnation. Still, though seeing, they did not see. Their discernment was not such as to direct them to the truth, much less to enable them to attain it, but was like that of the bewildered traveler who sees the flash of lightning glance far and wide for a moment and then vanish into the darkness of the night before he can advance a single step. So far is such help from enabling him to find the right path. Besides, how many monstrous falsehoods are mixed in with those tiny particles of truth scattered here and there in their writings as if by chance! In short, not one of them ever made the least approach to that assurance of the divine favor without which the mind of man must always remain a mere chaos of confusion. To the great truths, what God is in himself and what he is in relation to us, human reason makes not the least approach. (See Book 3, chapter 2, sections 14, 15, 16.)

19¶

But since we are intoxicated with a false opinion of our own discernment, and can hardly be persuaded that in divine things it is altogether dull and blind, I believe the best course will be to establish the fact not by argument but by Scripture. Most admirable to this effect is the passage I quoted a little earlier from John, where he says, "In him was life; and the life was the light of men. And the light shineth in darkness; and the darkness comprehended it not" (John 1:4, 5). He indicates that the human soul is indeed lit by a beam of divine light, so that it is never left entirely without some small flame, or rather spark, though not such as to enable it to comprehend God. And why is this? Because its sharpness, with respect to the knowledge of God, is mere blindness. When the Spirit describes men by the term darkness, he declares them empty of all power of spiritual understanding. For this reason it is said that believers, in embracing Christ, are "born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God" (John 1:13); in other words, the flesh has no capacity for wisdom so lofty as to grasp God and the things of God unless it is enlightened by His Spirit. In the same way our Savior, when he was acknowledged by Peter, declared that this came by special revelation from the Father (Matthew 16:17).

20¶

If we were persuaded of a truth that ought to be beyond dispute, namely, that human nature possesses none of the gifts that the elect receive from their heavenly Father through the Spirit of regeneration, there would be no room here for hesitation. For so speaks the congregation of the faithful by the mouth of the prophet: "With thee is the fountain of life: in thy light shall we see light" (Psalm 36:9). To the same effect is the testimony of the Apostle Paul when he declares that "no man can say that Jesus is the Lord, but by the Holy Ghost" (1 Corinthians 12:3). And John the Baptist, seeing the dullness of his disciples, exclaims, "A man can receive nothing, unless it be given him from heaven" (John 3:27). That the gift he refers to here must be understood not of ordinary natural gifts but of special illumination is clear from this, that he was complaining of how little his disciples had profited by all he had said to them in commendation of Christ. "I see," he says, "that my words are of no effect in filling the minds of men with divine things unless the Lord enlightens their understandings by His Spirit." Indeed, Moses too, while rebuking the people for their forgetfulness, at the same time observes that they could not become wise in the mysteries of God without his help. "Ye have seen all that the Lord did before your eyes in the land of Egypt, unto Pharaoh, and unto all his servants, and unto all his land; the great temptations which thine eyes have seen, the signs, and these great miracles: yet the Lord has not given you an heart to perceive, and eyes to see, and ears to hear, unto this day" (Deuteronomy 29:2, 3, 4). Would the expression have been stronger had he called us mere blocks with regard to the contemplation of divine things? Hence the Lord, by the mouth of the Prophet, promises the Israelites as a singular favor, "I will give them an heart to know me" (Jeremiah 24:7), indicating that in spiritual things the human mind is wise only so far as he enlightens it. Our Savior also clearly confirmed this when he said, "No man can come to me, except the Father which has sent me draw him" (John 6:44). Indeed, is not he himself the living image of his Father, in which the full brightness of his glory is shown to us? Therefore, how far our faculty of knowing God extends could not be better shown than by the declaration that, though his image is so plainly displayed, we have no eyes to perceive it. What? Did not Christ come down into the world to make the will of his Father known to men, and did he not faithfully carry out that office? True, he did; but nothing is accomplished by his preaching unless the inner teacher, the Spirit, opens the way into our minds. Only those, therefore, come to him who have heard and learned from the Father. And what is the manner of this hearing and learning? It is when the Spirit, with a wonderful and special power, forms the ear to hear and the mind to understand. Lest this should seem new, our Savior refers to the prophecy of Isaiah, which contains a promise of the renewal of the Church: "For a small moment have I forsaken thee; but with great mercies will I gather thee" (Isaiah 54:7). If the Lord here foretells some special blessing for his elect, it is plain that the teaching he refers to is not that which they share with the ungodly and profane.

It thus appears that none can enter the kingdom of God except those whose minds have been renewed by the enlightening of the Holy Spirit. On this subject the clearest explanation is given by Paul, who, when dealing with it expressly, after condemning the whole wisdom of the world as foolishness and vanity, and so declaring man's utter destitution, concludes thus: "The natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God: for they are foolishness unto him: neither can he know them, for they are spiritually discerned" (1 Corinthians 2:14). Whom does he mean by the "natural man"? The man who relies on the light of nature. Such a man has no understanding of the spiritual mysteries of God. Why is this? Is it because he neglects them through sloth? No; even if he exerts himself, it is of no use; they are "spiritually discerned." And what does this mean? That, being altogether hidden from human discernment, they are made known only by the revelation of the Spirit, so that they are counted foolishness wherever the Spirit does not give light. The Apostle had previously declared that "Eye has not seen, nor ear heard, neither have entered into the heart of man, the things which God has prepared for them that love him"; indeed, that the wisdom of the world is a kind of veil by which the mind is kept from beholding God (1 Corinthians 2:9). What more do we want? The Apostle declares that God has "made foolish the wisdom of this world" (1 Corinthians 1:20); and shall we attribute to it a sharpness capable of penetrating to God and to the hidden mysteries of his kingdom? Far from us be such presumption!

21¶

What the Apostle here denies to man, he ascribes in another place to God alone, when he prays "that the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory, may give unto you the spirit of wisdom and revelation" (Ephesians 1:17). You now hear that all wisdom and revelation is the gift of God. What follows? "The eyes of your understanding being enlightened." Surely, if they need a new enlightening, they must in themselves be blind. The next words are, "that ye may know what is the hope of his calling" (Ephesians 1:18). In other words, the minds of men do not have capacity enough to know their calling. Let no chattering Pelagian claim here that God remedies this crudeness or dullness when, by the doctrine of his word, he directs us to a path we could not have found without a guide. David had the law, containing in it all the wisdom that could be desired, and yet, not content with this, he prays, "Open thou mine eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of thy law" (Psalm 119:18). By this expression he certainly indicates that it is like sunrise on the earth when the word of God shines forth, but that men do not gain much benefit from it until he himself, who for this reason is called the Father of lights (James 1:17), either gives eyes or opens them; because whatever is not enlightened by his Spirit is wholly darkness. The Apostles had been properly and fully instructed by the best of teachers. Still, since they needed the Spirit of truth to complete their education in the very doctrine they had already heard, they were commanded to wait for him (John 14:26). If we confess that what we ask of God is lacking in us, and He, by the very thing he promises, shows our need, no one can hesitate to acknowledge that he is able to understand the mysteries of God only so far as he is enlightened by his grace. He who ascribes more understanding than this to himself is all the blinder for not acknowledging his blindness.

22¶

It remains to consider the third branch of the knowledge of spiritual things, namely, the way of rightly ordering our conduct. This is correctly called the knowledge of the works of righteousness, a branch in which the human mind seems to have somewhat more discernment than in the other two, since an Apostle declares, "When the Gentiles, which have not the law, do by nature the things contained in the law, these, having not the law, are a law unto themselves: which show the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness, and their thoughts the meantime accusing or else excusing one another" (Romans 2:14, 15). If the Gentiles have the righteousness of the law naturally engraved on their minds, we certainly cannot say that they are altogether blind as to the rule of life. Nothing, indeed, is more common than for man to be sufficiently instructed in a right course of conduct by the natural law of which the Apostle speaks here. Let us consider, however, for what purpose this knowledge of the law was given to men. For from this it will at once appear how far it can lead them in the way of reason and truth. This is plain even from the words of Paul, if we pay attention to their order. He had said a little before that those who have sinned in the law will be judged by the law, and those who have sinned without the law will perish without the law. Since it might seem inexplicable that the Gentiles should perish without any prior judgment, he immediately adds that conscience served them in place of the law, and was therefore sufficient for their righteous condemnation. The purpose of the natural law, therefore, is to make man inexcusable, and it may fairly be defined as the judgment of conscience, distinguishing sufficiently between just and unjust, and, by convicting men on their own testimony, depriving them of every pretext of ignorance. Man is so indulgent toward himself that, while doing evil, he always tries as hard as he can to suppress the thought of sin. It was this, apparently, that led Plato (in his Protagoras) to suppose that sins were committed only through ignorance. There might be some ground for this if hypocrisy were so successful in hiding vice as to keep the conscience clear in the sight of God. But since the sinner, when he tries to evade the judgment of good and evil implanted in him, is again and again dragged forward, and is not allowed to shut his eyes so effectively as not to be forced at times, whether he will or not, to open them, it is false to say that he sins only through ignorance.

23¶

Themistius is more accurate in teaching (Paraphrase on Book 3 De Anima, chapter 46) that the intellect is very seldom mistaken in the general definition or essence of a matter, but that deception begins as it goes further, namely, when it comes down to particulars. That homicide, taking the case in the abstract, is an evil, no one will deny; and yet one who is plotting the death of his enemy deliberates on it as if the thing were good. The adulterer will condemn adultery in the abstract, and yet flatter himself while committing it in private. The ignorance lies here: that man, when he comes to the particular, forgets the rule he had laid down in the general case. Augustine treats this subject most admirably in his exposition of the first verse of the fifty-seventh Psalm. The doctrine of Themistius, however, does not always hold true; for the foulness of the crime sometimes presses so hard on the conscience that the sinner does not deceive himself with a false appearance of good, but rushes into sin knowingly and willingly. Hence the saying: I see the better course, and approve it; I follow the worse (Ovid's Medea). For this reason Aristotle seems to me to have made a very shrewd distinction between incontinence and intemperance (Ethics, book 7, chapter 3). Where incontinence (ajkrasiva) reigns, he says, particular knowledge is suppressed through the passion (pavtho"), so that the individual does not see in his own misdeed the evil that he sees generally in similar cases; but when the passion is over, repentance immediately follows. Intemperance (ajkolasiva), on the other hand, is not extinguished or lessened by a sense of sin, but on the contrary persists in the evil choice it has once made.

24¶

Moreover, when you hear of a universal judgment in man distinguishing between good and evil, you must not suppose that this judgment is in every respect sound and whole. For if the hearts of men are imbued with a sense of justice and injustice so that they may have no pretext for pleading ignorance, it is by no means necessary for this purpose that they should discern the truth in particular cases. It is more than enough if they understand so far as to be unable to resort to evasion without being convicted by their own conscience, and to begin even now to tremble at the judgment seat of God. Indeed, if we were to test our reason by the Divine Law, which is a perfect standard of righteousness, we should find how blind it is in many respects. It certainly does not attain to the principal heads of the First Table, such as trust in God, the ascribing to him of all praise for virtue and righteousness, the calling on his name, and the true observance of his day of rest. Did any soul, under the guidance of natural sense, ever imagine that these and the like make up the lawful worship of God? When profane men would worship God, however often they may be drawn away from their vain trifling, they constantly fall back into it. They admit, indeed, that sacrifices are not pleasing to God unless accompanied by sincerity of mind; and by this they testify that they have some notion of spiritual worship, though they immediately pervert it by false inventions; for it is impossible to persuade them that everything the law commands on the subject is true. Shall I, then, praise the discernment of a mind that can neither gain wisdom by itself nor listen to advice? The French adds, "Or l'entendement humaiu a eté tel en cest endroit. Nous appercevons donques qu'il est du tout stupide;" now, the understanding has proved so in this matter. We see, therefore, that it is quite stupid.

25¶

As we have criticized above Plato's error in ascribing all sins to ignorance, so we must reject the opinion of those who hold that all sins proceed from deliberate gravity and malice. We know only too well from experience how often we fall even when our intention is good. Our reason is exposed to so many forms of delusion, is liable to so many errors, stumbles on so many obstacles, and is entangled in so many snares, that it is always wandering from the right direction. Paul shows how little it is worth in the sight of God with regard to all the parts of life when he says that we are not "sufficient of ourselves to think any thing as of ourselves" (2 Corinthians 3:5). He is not speaking of the will or the affections; he denies us the power even of thinking rightly how anything can be properly done. Is it really true that all our thought, understanding, discernment, and industry are so defective that in the sight of the Lord we cannot think of or aim at anything that is right? To us, who can hardly bear to part with sharpness of intellect (in our estimation a most precious endowment), this seems hard to admit; yet it is regarded as entirely just by the Holy Spirit, who "knoweth the thoughts of man, that they are vanity" (Psalm 94:11), and plainly declares that "every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually" (Genesis 6:5; 8:21). If everything our mind conceives, meditates, plans, and resolves is always evil, how can it ever think of doing what is pleasing to God, to whom righteousness and holiness alone are acceptable? It is plain, then, that our mind, in whatever direction it turns, is miserably exposed to vanity. David was aware of its weakness when he prayed, "Give me understanding, and I shall keep thy law" (Psalm 119:34). By desiring to obtain a new understanding, he indicates that his own was by no means sufficient. And he does this not once only, but repeats the same prayer almost ten times in one psalm, the repetition showing how strong was the need that urged him to pray. What he thus asked for himself alone, Paul prays for the churches in general: "For this cause," he says, "we also, since the day we heard it, do not cease to pray for you, and to desire that ye might be filled with the knowledge of his will, in all wisdom and spiritual understanding; that you might walk worthy of the Lord," and so on (Colossians 1:9, 10). Whenever he represents this as a blessing from God, we should remember that he testifies at the same time that it is not in the power of man. Accordingly, Augustine, speaking of this inability of human reason to understand the things of God, says that he considers the grace of illumination no less necessary to the mind than the light of the sun to the eye (Augustine, De Peccat. Merit. et Remiss., book 2, chapter 5). And, not content with this, he qualifies his expression, adding that we open our eyes to see the light, whereas the eye of the mind remains shut until it is opened by the Lord. Nor does Scripture say that our minds are enlightened in a single day so as to see by themselves afterward. The passage I quoted a little earlier from the Apostle Paul refers to continual progress and growth. David, too, expresses this plainly in these words: "With my whole heart have I sought thee: O let me not wander from thy commandments" (Psalm 119:10). Though he had been regenerated, and so had made no ordinary progress in true godliness, he confesses that he needed direction every moment, so that he might not turn aside from the knowledge with which he had been endowed. Hence elsewhere he prays for the renewal of a right spirit, which he had lost by his sin, Calvin, in his Commentary on the passage, says, "Lost in part or appearance, or deserved to lose."

26¶

We must now examine the will, on which the question of freedom chiefly turns, since the power of choice belongs to it rather than to the intellect, as we have already seen (above, section 4). And at the outset, to guard against the idea that the doctrine taught by philosophers and generally accepted, namely, that all things by natural instinct have a desire for good, is any proof of the uprightness of the human will, let us observe that the power of free will is not to be looked for in any of those desires that proceed more from instinct than from deliberation of the mind. Even the Schoolmen admit (Thomas, Part 1, Question 83, article 3) that there is no act of free will except when reason considers opposites. By this they mean that the things desired must be such as can be made the object of choice, and that, to prepare the way for choice, deliberation must come first. And undoubtedly, if you look at what this natural desire of good in man is, you will find that he shares it with the brutes. They too desire what is good; and when any appearance of good capable of moving the senses presents itself, they follow after it. Here, however, man does not, in keeping with the excellence of his immortal nature, rationally choose and diligently pursue what is truly for his good. He does not admit reason to his counsel, nor exert his intellect, but without reason and without counsel follows the bent of his nature like the lower animals. The question of freedom, therefore, has nothing to do with the fact that man is led by natural instinct to desire good. The question is this: Does man, after determining by right reason what is good, choose what he thus knows, and pursue what he thus chooses? So that no doubt may remain on this point, we must attend to the double misuse of terms. For this appetite is not properly a movement of the will but a natural inclination; and this good is not one of virtue or righteousness but of condition, namely, that the individual may be comfortable. In short, however much man may desire to obtain what is good, he does not follow after it. There is no one who would not be pleased with eternal blessedness; and yet without the impulse of the Spirit no one aspires to it. Since, then, the natural desire for happiness in man no more proves the freedom of the will than the tendency in metals and stones to attain the perfection of their nature, let us consider in other respects whether the will is so utterly corrupted and depraved in every part as to produce nothing but evil, or whether it keeps some portion unharmed and productive of good desires.

27¶

Those who ascribe our effectual willing to the primary grace of God (above, section 6) seem, conversely, to insinuate that the soul has in itself a power of aspiring to good, though a power too feeble to rise to firm affection or active effort. There is no doubt that this opinion, taken over from Origen and certain of the ancient Fathers, has been generally embraced by the Schoolmen, who are accustomed to apply to man in his natural state (in puris naturalibus, as they put it) the following description of the apostle: "For that which I do I allow not: for what I would, that do I not; but what I hate, that do I." "To will is present with me; but how to perform that which is good I find not" (Romans 7:15, 18). But in this way the whole drift of Paul's discourse is turned upside down. He is speaking of the Christian struggle (touched on more briefly in the Epistle to the Galatians), which believers constantly experience from the conflict between the flesh and the Spirit. But the Spirit comes not from nature but from regeneration. That the apostle is speaking of the regenerate is clear from this, that after saying, "in me dwells no good thing," he immediately adds the explanation, "in my flesh." Accordingly, he declares, "It is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me." What is the meaning of the correction, "in me (that is, in my flesh)"? It is just as if he had spoken thus: No good thing dwells in me of myself, for in my flesh nothing good can be found. Hence follows the kind of excuse: It is not I myself that do evil, but sin that dwells in me. This applies to none but the regenerate, who with the leading powers of the soul tend toward what is good. The whole is made plain by the conclusion, "I delight in the law of God after the inward man: but I see another law in my members, warring against the law of my mind" (Romans 7:22, 23). Who has this struggle within himself except those who, regenerated by the Spirit of God, carry about with them the remains of the flesh? Accordingly, Augustine, who had at one time thought that the discourse related to the natural man (Augustine, To Boniface, book 1, chapter 10), afterward retracted that exposition as unsound and inconsistent. And indeed, if we grant that men without grace have any motions toward good, however feeble, what answer shall we give to the apostle, who declares that "we are incapable of thinking a good thought" (2 Corinthians 3:6)? What answer shall we give to the Lord, who declares by Moses that "every imagination of man's heart is only evil continually" (Genesis 8:21)? Since the blunder has thus arisen from a mistaken view of a single passage, it seems unnecessary to dwell on it. Let us rather give due weight to our Savior's words, "Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin" (John 8:34). We are all sinners by nature; therefore we are held under the yoke of sin. But if the whole man is subject to the dominion of sin, surely the will, which is its principal seat, must be bound with the tightest chains. And indeed, if divine grace were preceded by any will of ours, Paul could not have said that "it is God which worketh in us both to will and to do" (Philippians 2:13). Away, then, with all the absurd trifling that many have indulged in on the subject of preparation. Although believers sometimes ask to have their heart trained to obey the divine law, as David does in several passages (Psalm 51:12), it is to be observed that even this longing in prayer is from God. This is clear from the language used. When he prays, "Create in me a clean heart," he certainly does not attribute the beginning of the creation to himself. Let us therefore rather adopt the view of Augustine: "God will go before you in all things; but do you sometimes go before his anger. How? Confess that you have all these things from God, that all the good you have is from him, all the evil from yourself" (Augustine, De Verbis Apost., Sermon 10). Shortly afterward he says, "Of our own we have nothing but sin."

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin clears away the philosophers' flattering fictions and the schoolmen's hairsplitting to leave us with a plain and humbling word: fallen man is not free but enslaved, the will bound toward evil though it sins willingly. We retain choice; what we have lost is the power to choose God. This cuts against the deepest assumption of 2026 — that the autonomous self, properly motivated, can pull itself toward the good — and it explains why willpower alone never finally reforms the heart. For the believer this is not despair but the doorway to grace, for what we cannot free God freely frees. Do not raise children on the gospel of self-improvement; teach them that the chains are real and that Christ alone strikes them off. The honest admission of bondage is the beginning of true liberty, and the soul that stops boasting in its own will is the soul prepared to be redeemed by another's.
