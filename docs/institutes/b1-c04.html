Book 1 · Chapter 4 · USMC modern English

The Knowledge of God Stifled or Corrupted, Ignorantly or Maliciously

▶ Listen — AI narration by Mr. Pemberton (modernized for 2026)
Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. The knowledge of God suppressed by ignorance, many falling away into superstition. Such persons, however, inexcusable, because their error is accompanied with pride and stubbornness.
  2. 2. Stubbornness the companion of impiety.
  3. 3. No pretext can justify superstition. This proved, first, from reason; and, secondly, from Scripture.
  4. 4. The wicked never willingly come into the presence of God. Hence their hypocrisy. Hence, too, their sense of Deity leads to no good result.
1¶

Experience shows that God has planted a seed of religion in everyone. Yet scarcely one person in a hundred truly cherishes it in the heart, and not one in whom it grows to full maturity, so that it never yields fruit in its season. Some people lose themselves in superstition, and others deliberately and wickedly turn away from God. As a result, when it comes to the true knowledge of him, everyone is so corrupted that genuine godliness cannot be found anywhere in the world.

When I say that some fall away into superstition, I do not mean to suggest that their sheer absurdity excuses them from guilt. The blindness they suffer almost always goes hand in hand with empty pride and stubbornness. Their mixture of vanity and pride shows itself like this: when these wretched people do seek after God, instead of rising higher than themselves as they ought, they measure him by their own dull and earthly minds. They abandon serious inquiry and fly off to indulge their curiosity in pointless speculation. So they do not conceive of God as he has revealed himself, but imagine him to be whatever their own recklessness has invented.

Once this pit lies open, they cannot take a single step without rushing headlong toward destruction. With such an idea of God, nothing they try to offer in worship or obedience can have any value in his sight, because it is not him they worship. Instead of him, they worship the dream and fiction of their own heart. Paul describes this corrupt practice perfectly when he says that, "thinking themselves wise, they became fools" (Romans 1:22). He had said earlier that "they became futile in their thinking," but so that no one would think them blameless, he goes on to add that they were justly blinded. Not content with sober inquiry, claiming for themselves far more than they had any right to claim, they willingly court darkness; indeed, they bewitch themselves with twisted, empty illusions. That is why their folly cannot be excused. It is the result not only of vain curiosity, but of unrestrained desire and overconfidence in their pursuit of forbidden knowledge.

Rom 1:22
2¶

David's saying, "The fool has said in his heart, There is no God" (Psalm 14:1; 53:1), applies first of all to those who, as will soon become clearer, smother the light of nature and deliberately stupefy themselves. We see many people who, once they have grown hardened in a bold course of sin, madly drive out every remembrance of God, even though their own natural sense suggests it to them from within. To show how detestable this madness is, the Psalmist presents them as flatly denying that there is a God. They do not actually deny his existence, but they strip him of his justice and his providence and picture him sitting idle in heaven.

Nothing is less in keeping with the nature of God than to cast off the government of the world, leaving it to chance, and to wink at the crimes of men so that they may run wild in evil without punishment. It follows that every person who lives in false security, after putting out all fear of divine judgment, is in effect denying that there is a God. As a just punishment of the wicked, after they have shut their own eyes, God makes their hearts dull and heavy, so that, though they see, they do not truly see. David is the best interpreter of his own meaning when he says elsewhere that the wicked have "no fear of God before their eyes" (Psalm 36:1), and again, "He has said in his heart, God has forgotten; he hides his face; he will never see it."

So although they are forced to admit that there is some God, they rob him of his glory by denying his power. As Paul declares, "If we are faithless, he remains faithful; he cannot deny himself" (2 Timothy 2:13). In the same way, those who fashion for themselves a dead and dumb idol are rightly said to deny God. We should also notice this: even though they wrestle against their own convictions, and would gladly banish God not only from their minds but from heaven as well, their numbness is never so complete that they are entirely safe from being dragged, now and then, before God's judgment seat. Still, since no fear holds them back from violently defying God as long as that blind impulse drives them on, it cannot be denied that their usual state of mind toward him is a brutish forgetfulness.

3¶

In this way the empty excuse that many use to dress up their superstition is overthrown. They think it is enough that they have some kind of zeal for religion, however misguided it may be. But they fail to see that true religion must conform to the will of God as its unerring standard, that he can never deny himself, and that he is no ghost or phantom to be reshaped according to each person's whim. It is easy to see how superstition, with its false pretenses, actually mocks God while it tries to please him. It usually fastens onto the very things he has declared he sets no value on, while it either carelessly overlooks, or openly rejects, the very things he expressly commands and in which we are assured he takes pleasure.

So those who set up a made-up worship are really only worshiping and adoring their own deluded fantasies. They would never dare to trifle with God this way if they had not first shaped him to fit their own childish notions. That is why an apostle calls such a vague and wandering opinion of God a state of not knowing God at all: "Formerly, when you did not know God, you were enslaved to those that by nature are not gods." And elsewhere he declares that the Ephesians were "without God" (Ephesians 2:12) at the time when they wandered without any true knowledge of him.

In this respect it makes little difference whether you hold to one God or to many gods, since either way, by departing from the true God, you have nothing left but a detestable idol. And so we are left to conclude, with Lactantius, "No religion is genuine that is not in accordance with truth."

4¶

To this fault they add a second: when they do think about God, they do so against their will. They never come into his presence without being dragged there, and once there, they feel none of the willing fear that flows from reverence for the divine majesty. They feel only that forced and slavish fear which the thought of divine judgment wrings out of them. Because they cannot escape it, they are compelled to dread that judgment; yet even while they dread it, they also hate it. To impiety, and to it alone, the saying of Statius truly applies: "Fear first brought gods into the world." Those whose desires run against the justice of God know that his judgment seat has been set up to punish wrongdoing, and so they desperately wish it could be torn down.

Driven by this feeling, they are actually waging war against God, since justice is one of his essential attributes. Seeing that they are always within reach of his power, and that both resistance and escape are impossible, they fear and tremble. So, to avoid looking as if they openly defied a majesty that overawes everyone, they take up some form of religious observance. Yet all the while they never stop defiling themselves with every kind of vice, piling crime upon crime, until they have broken the holy law of the Lord in every one of its demands and treated his whole righteousness as nothing. At the very least, their pretense of fear does not restrain them from reveling and delighting in wickedness, since they would rather indulge their bodily cravings than rein them in with the bridle of the Holy Spirit.

But since this shadow of religion (it scarcely deserves even to be called a shadow) is false and empty, it is easy to see how far this confused knowledge of God differs from the godliness that is poured into the hearts of believers, the godliness from which alone true religion springs. And yet hypocrites would love, by all sorts of twisting and turning, to put on a show of drawing near to God at the very moment they are running away from him. Their whole life ought to be one continuous course of obedience, yet they rebel without fear in nearly everything they do, and then try to appease him with a few worthless sacrifices. They ought to serve him with integrity of heart and holiness of life, yet they try to win his favor by means of trivial gimmicks and petty observances of no value.

Indeed, they take even greater liberty in their base indulgences, because they imagine they can fulfill their duty to him by absurd acts of atonement. In short, when their confidence should have been fixed on him, they push him aside and rest instead in themselves or in created things. In the end they lose themselves in such a maze of error that the darkness of ignorance obscures, and finally puts out, the very sparks that were meant to show them the glory of God. Even so, the conviction that there is some God lives on, like a plant that can never be completely uprooted, though it is so corrupt that it can only produce the worst kind of fruit.

In fact, we have even stronger proof of the point I am now making, namely, that a sense of God is naturally engraved on the human heart. The proof is this: even the worst of the reprobate are forced to acknowledge it. When they are at ease, they can joke about God and talk flippantly and endlessly to belittle his power. But should despair, from any cause, suddenly seize them, it will stir them to seek him and prompt desperate, broken prayers. This proves that they were not entirely ignorant of God, but had stubbornly suppressed feelings that should have shown themselves long before.

The seed of religion is sown in all, Calvin grants, yet scarcely one in a hundred lets it grow — some smother it in superstition, others wickedly revolt, and all are left without excuse, for their error is wedded to pride and stubbornness. Men measure God by their own carnal stupidity and worship the figment their own hearts devise; zeal alone is no defense, since true religion must conform to his revealed will, not our caprice. This is the besetting sin of a spiritual but not religious age — a homemade God tailored to private preference, fashioned to bless whatever the worshiper already loves. Calvin strips the comfort from it: to worship a deity of your own design is to worship an idol and flee the living God even while pretending nearness. Guard your home against this drift, where Scripture is edited to taste and obedience traded for sentiment. Teach that we do not get to invent God; we receive him as he has made himself known, and bow.
