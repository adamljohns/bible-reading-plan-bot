The Knowledge of God Stifled or Corrupted, Ignorantly or Maliciously

Listen — AI narration by Mr. Pemberton (modernized for 2026)
Download the MP3
Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. The knowledge of God suppressed by ignorance, many falling away into superstition. Such persons, however, inexcusable, because their error is accompanied with pride and stubbornness.
  2. 2. Stubbornness the companion of impiety.
  3. 3. No pretext can justify superstition. This proved, first, from reason; and, secondly, from Scripture.
  4. 4. The wicked never willingly come into the presence of God. Hence their hypocrisy. Hence, too, their sense of Deity leads to no good result.
1¶

But though experience testifies that a seed of religion has been sown by God in all, scarcely one in a hundred is found who cherishes it in his heart, and not one in whom it grows to maturity; so far is it from yielding fruit in its season. Moreover, while some lose themselves in superstitious observances, and others deliberately and wickedly revolt from God, the result is that, with regard to the true knowledge of him, all have so degenerated that nowhere in the world can genuine godliness be found. In saying that some fall away into superstition, I do not mean to suggest that their extreme absurdity frees them from guilt; for the blindness under which they labor is almost always accompanied by vain pride and stubbornness. Vanity and pride mingled together appear in this: that when miserable men do seek after God, instead of rising higher than themselves, as they ought to do, they measure him by their own carnal dullness, and, neglecting solid inquiry, fly off to indulge their curiosity in empty speculation. Hence they do not conceive of him as he has revealed himself, but imagine him to be whatever their own rashness has devised. With this abyss standing open, they cannot take a single step without rushing headlong to destruction. With such an idea of God, nothing they may attempt to offer by way of worship or obedience can have any value in his sight, because it is not he whom they worship, but, in his place, the dream and invention of their own heart. Paul describes this corrupt course admirably when he says that "thinking to be wise, they became fools" (Romans 1:22). He had said before that "they became vain in their imaginations"; but lest anyone should suppose them blameless, he goes on to add that they were justly blinded, because, not content with sober inquiry, and claiming for themselves more than they have any right to, they of their own accord court the darkness; indeed, they bewitch themselves with perverse and empty show. Hence their folly, which is the result not only of vain curiosity but of unbridled desire and overweening confidence in the pursuit of forbidden knowledge, cannot be excused.

2¶

The saying of David (Psalm 14:1; 53:1), "The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God," applies first of all to those who, as will soon appear more fully, stifle the light of nature and deliberately make themselves senseless. We see many who, after they have become hardened in a reckless course of sin, madly banish all remembrance of God, though it is spontaneously suggested to them from within by natural sense. To show how detestable this madness is, the Psalmist presents them as flatly denying that there is a God; for although they do not disown his being, they rob him of his justice and providence, and picture him as sitting idle in heaven. Since nothing is less in keeping with the nature of God than to cast off the government of the world and leave it to chance, and so to overlook the crimes of men that they may run riot in evil with impunity, it follows that every man who indulges in careless security, having put out all fear of divine judgment, in effect denies that there is a God. As a just punishment of the wicked, after they have closed their own eyes, God makes their hearts dull and heavy, and so, seeing, they do not see. David, indeed, is the best interpreter of his own meaning when he says elsewhere that the wicked man has "no fear of God before his eyes" (Psalm 36:1); and again, "He has said in his heart, God has forgotten; he hideth his face; he will never see it." Thus, although they are forced to acknowledge that there is some God, they nevertheless rob him of his glory by denying his power. For as Paul declares, "If we believe not, he abideth faithful; he cannot deny himself" (2 Timothy 2:13); so those who invent for themselves a dead and dumb idol are truly said to deny God. It is to be observed, moreover, that though they struggle against their own convictions, and would gladly banish God not only from their minds but from heaven as well, their senselessness is never so complete as to keep them from being dragged now and then before the divine tribunal. Still, since no fear restrains them from rushing violently in the face of God so long as they are swept along by that blind impulse, it cannot be denied that their prevailing state of mind toward him is a brutish forgetfulness.

3¶

In this way the empty pretext which many use to cloak their superstition is overthrown. They think it enough to have some kind of zeal for religion, however absurd it may be, not observing that true religion must conform to the will of God as its unerring standard; that he can never deny himself, and is no specter or phantom to be transformed at each person's whim. It is easy to see how superstition, with its false glosses, mocks God even while it tries to please him. Usually fastening only on things on which he has declared he sets no value, it either scornfully overlooks, or even openly rejects, the things he expressly commands, or in which we are assured that he takes pleasure. Those, therefore, who set up an invented worship are merely worshiping and adoring their own delirious fancies; indeed, they would never dare to trifle with God in this way had they not first fashioned him after their own childish notions. Hence that vague and wandering opinion of deity is declared by an apostle to be ignorance of God: "Howbeit, then, when ye knew not God, ye did service unto them which by nature are no gods." And elsewhere he declares that the Ephesians were "without God" (Ephesians 2:12) at the time when they wandered without any right knowledge of him. It makes little difference, at least in this respect, whether you hold that there is one God or a plurality of gods, since in both cases alike, by departing from the true God, you have nothing left but an accursed idol. It remains, therefore, to conclude with Lactantius (Divine Institutes, book 1:2, 6): "No religion is genuine that is not in accordance with truth."

4¶

To this fault they add a second, namely, that when they do think of God, it is against their will. They never approach him without being dragged into his presence, and when they are there, instead of the willing fear that flows from reverence for the divine majesty, they feel only that forced and servile fear which divine judgment wrings from them: a judgment which, since escape is impossible, they are compelled to dread, but which, even while they dread it, they also hate. To ungodliness, and to it alone, the saying of Statius properly applies: "Fear first brought gods into the world" (Thebaid, book 1). Those whose desires are at odds with the justice of God, knowing that his tribunal has been set up for the punishment of transgression, earnestly wish that tribunal were overthrown. Under the influence of this feeling they are actually waging war against God, since justice is one of his essential attributes. Seeing that they are always within reach of his power, and that resistance and escape are equally impossible, they fear and tremble. Accordingly, to avoid the appearance of despising a majesty by which all are overawed, they resort to some kind of religious observance, while never ceasing in the meantime to defile themselves with every kind of vice, adding crime to crime until they have broken the holy law of the Lord in every one of its requirements and set his whole righteousness at nothing. At any rate, they are not so restrained by their show of fear as to keep from wallowing and taking pleasure in iniquity, choosing rather to indulge their carnal inclinations than to curb them with the bridle of the Holy Spirit. But since this shadow of religion (it hardly even deserves to be called a shadow) is false and vain, it is easy to infer how far this confused knowledge of God differs from that piety which is instilled into the hearts of believers, and from which alone true religion springs. And yet hypocrites would like, by twisting and winding paths, to make a show of being near to God at the very moment they are fleeing from him. For while the whole of life ought to be one unbroken course of obedience, they rebel without fear in almost all their actions, and try to appease him with a few paltry sacrifices; while they ought to serve him with integrity of heart and holiness of life, they try to win his favor by frivolous devices and worthless niceties of ceremony. Indeed, they allow themselves greater license in their groveling indulgences, because they imagine they can fulfill their duty to him by absurd expiations; in short, while their confidence ought to have been fixed on him, they set him aside and rest in themselves or in the creatures. At length they entangle themselves in such a maze of error that the darkness of ignorance obscures, and finally extinguishes, those sparks which were meant to show them the glory of God. Still, the conviction that there is some deity continues to exist, like a plant that can never be completely rooted out, though so corrupt that it can produce only the worst of fruit. Indeed, we have still stronger evidence for the point I am now contending for, namely, that a sense of deity is naturally engraved on the human heart, in the fact that even the reprobate are forced to acknowledge it. When they are at ease, they can joke about God and talk glibly and at length in disparagement of his power; but should despair, from any cause, overtake them, it will drive them to seek him and prompt them to sudden, brief prayers, proving that they were not entirely ignorant of God, but had perversely suppressed feelings which ought to have shown themselves earlier.

The seed of religion is sown in all, Calvin grants, yet scarcely one in a hundred lets it grow — some smother it in superstition, others wickedly revolt, and all are left without excuse, for their error is wedded to pride and stubbornness. Men measure God by their own carnal stupidity and worship the figment their own hearts devise; zeal alone is no defense, since true religion must conform to his revealed will, not our caprice. This is the besetting sin of a spiritual but not religious age — a homemade God tailored to private preference, fashioned to bless whatever the worshiper already loves. Calvin strips the comfort from it: to worship a deity of your own design is to worship an idol and flee the living God even while pretending nearness. Guard your home against this drift, where Scripture is edited to taste and obedience traded for sentiment. Teach that we do not get to invent God; we receive him as he has made himself known, and bow.
