Book 1 · Chapter 18 · USMC modern English

The Instrumentality of the Wicked Employed by God, While He Continues Free From Every Taint

This last chapter of the First Book consists of three parts: I. It having been said above that God bends all the reprobate, and even Satan himself, at his will, three objections are started. First, that this happens by the permission, not by the will of God. To this objection there is a twofold reply, the one, that angels and men, good and bad, do nothing but what is appointed by God; the second, that all movements are secretly directed to their end by the hidden inspiration of God, sec. 1, 2. II. A second objection is, that there are two contrary wills in God, if by a secret counsel he decrees what he openly prohibits by his law. This objection refuted, sec. 3. III. The third objection is, that God is made the author of all wickedness, when he is said not only to use the agency of the wicked, but also to govern their counsels and affections, and that therefore the wicked are unjustly punished. This objection refuted in the last section.

Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. The carnal mind the source of the objections which are raised against the Providence of God. A primary objection, making a distinction between the permission and the will of God, refuted. Angels and men, good and bad, do nought but what has been decreed by God. This proved by examples.
  2. 2. All hidden movements directed to their end by the unseen but righteous instigation of God. Examples, with answers to objections.
  3. 3. These objections originate in a spirit of pride and blasphemy. Objection, that there must be two contrary wills in God, refuted. Why the one simple will of God seems to us as if it were manifold.
  4. 4. Objection, that God is the author of sin, refuted by examples. Augustine’s answer and admonition.
1¶

A more difficult question arises from other passages, in which God is said to draw or bend Satan himself, and all the reprobate, to his will. For the carnal mind can scarcely grasp how, when he acts through them, he contracts no stain from their impurity; indeed, how, in a common operation, he is free from all guilt and can justly condemn his own ministers. Hence a distinction has been invented between doing and permitting, because to many it seemed altogether inexplicable how Satan and all the wicked are so under the hand and authority of God that he directs their malice to whatever end he pleases and uses their iniquities to carry out his judgments. The modesty of those who are alarmed in this way at the appearance of absurdity might perhaps be excused, if they did not try to clear the justice of God of every appearance of reproach by defending an untruth. It seems absurd that a man should be blinded by the will and command of God, and then be promptly punished for his blindness. Hence recourse is had to the evasion that this is done only by the permission, and not also by the will, of God. God himself, however, rejects the evasion by openly declaring that he does this. That men do nothing except at the secret prompting of God, and do not discuss and deliberate on anything but what he has already decreed with himself and brings to pass by his secret direction, is proved by countless clear passages of Scripture. What we quoted earlier from the Psalms, to the effect that he does whatever pleases him, certainly extends to all the actions of men. If God is the arbiter of peace and war, as is said there, and that without any exception, who will venture to say that men are carried along at random by a blind impulse while he is unaware or idle? But the matter will be made clearer by particular examples. From the first chapter of Job we learn that Satan appears in the presence of God to receive his orders, just as the angels do who obey willingly. The manner and the end are different, but the fact remains that he cannot attempt anything without the will of God. And though his power to afflict the saint afterward seems to be only a bare permission, yet since the saying is true, "The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away; as it pleased the Lord, so it has been done," we infer that God was the author of that trial, of which Satan and the wicked robbers were merely the instruments. Satan's aim is to drive the saint to madness through despair. The Sabeans cruelly and wickedly make a sudden raid to rob another man of his goods. Job acknowledges that he was stripped of all his property and brought to poverty because such was the pleasure of God. Therefore, whatever men or Satan himself may devise, God holds the helm and makes all their efforts serve the execution of his judgments. God wills that the faithless Ahab should be deceived; the devil offers his services for that purpose and is sent with a definite command to be a lying spirit in the mouth of all the prophets (2 Kings 22:20). If the blinding and infatuation of Ahab is a judgment from God, the fiction of bare permission is at an end; for it would be ridiculous for a judge only to permit, and not also to decree, what he wishes to be done, at the very time that he commits its execution to his ministers. The Jews intended to destroy Christ. Pilate and the soldiers gave way to their fury; yet the disciples confess in solemn prayer that all the wicked did nothing but what the hand and counsel of God had decreed (Acts 4:28), just as Peter had said earlier in his sermon, that Christ was delivered to death by the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God (Acts 2:23); in other words, that God, to whom all things are known from the beginning, had determined what the Jews carried out. He repeats the same thing elsewhere: "Those things, which God before had showed by the mouth of all his prophets, that Christ should suffer, he has so fulfilled" (Acts 4:18). Absalom, incestuously defiling his father's bed, commits a detestable crime. God, however, declares that it was his work; for the words are, "You did it secretly, but I will do this thing before all Israel, and before the sun." 2 Samuel 12:12; Jeremiah 1:25; Isaiah 5:26; 10:5; 19:25; 2 Samuel 16:10; 1 Kings 11:31; 1 Samuel 2:34.

2¶

With regard to secret movements, what Solomon says of the heart of a king, that it is turned this way and that as God sees fit, certainly applies to the whole human race, and has the same force as if he had said that whatever we conceive in our minds is directed to its end by the secret inspiration of God. And certainly, if he did not work inwardly in the minds of men, it could not rightly have been said that he takes away the lip from the truthful and prudence from the aged, and takes away the heart from the princes of the earth, so that they wander in trackless paths. To the same effect, we often read that men are made afraid when he fills their hearts with terror. So David left the camp of Saul without anyone knowing it, because a sleep from God had fallen on them all. But nothing can be clearer than the many passages declaring that he blinds the minds of men and strikes them with giddiness, makes them drunk with a spirit of stupor, makes them senseless, and hardens their hearts. Even these expressions many would limit to permission, as if, by forsaking the reprobate, he allowed them to be blinded by Satan. But since the Holy Spirit plainly says that the blindness and infatuation are inflicted by the just judgment of God, this solution is altogether inadmissible. He is said to have hardened the heart of Pharaoh, to have hardened it still more, and to have confirmed it. Some evade these forms of expression with a silly quibble: because Pharaoh is elsewhere said to have hardened his own heart, they make his will the cause of its hardening; as if the two things did not agree perfectly with each other, though in different senses, namely, that man, while he is acted upon by God, at the same time also acts. But I turn the objection back on those who make it. If to harden means only bare permission, the stubbornness will not properly belong to Pharaoh. Now, could anything be weaker and more insipid than to interpret it as if Pharaoh had only allowed himself to be hardened? We may add that Scripture cuts off every handle for such quibbles: "I," says the Lord, "will harden his heart" (Exodus 4:21). So also Moses says of the inhabitants of the land of Canaan that they went out to battle because the Lord had hardened their hearts (Joshua 11:20). The same thing is repeated by another prophet: "He turned their hearts to hate his people" (Psalm 105:25). In the same way, in Isaiah, he says of the Assyrian, "I will send him against a hypocritical nation, and against the people of my wrath will I give him a charge, to take the spoil, and to take the prey" (Isaiah 10:6); not that he means to teach wicked and obstinate men to obey willingly, but because he bends them to carry out his judgments, just as if they carried their orders engraved on their minds. And from this it appears that they are driven by the sure appointment of God. I admit, indeed, that God often acts in the reprobate by interposing the agency of Satan, but in such a way that Satan himself performs his part only as he is driven, and succeeds only so far as he is permitted. The evil spirit that troubled Saul is said to be from the Lord (1 Samuel 16:14), to indicate that Saul's madness was a just punishment from God. Satan is also said to blind the minds of those who do not believe (2 Corinthians 4:4). But how is this so, except that a spirit of error is sent from God himself, making those who refuse to obey the truth believe a lie? According to the first view it is said, "If the prophet be deceived when he has spoken a thing, I the Lord have deceived that prophet" (Ezekiel 14:9). According to the second view, he is said to have given men over to a reprobate mind (Romans 1:28), because he is the principal author of his own just vengeance, while Satan is only his minister (see Calvin on Psalm 141:4). But since this subject will be considered again in the Second Book (chapter 4, sections 3, 4), in discussing the question of man's freedom, the little that has now been said seems to be all that the occasion requires. The sum of the whole is this: since the will of God is said to be the cause of all things, all the counsels and actions of men must be held to be governed by his providence; so that he not only exerts his power in the elect, who are guided by the Holy Spirit, but also compels the reprobate to do him service.

3¶

Since I have so far stated only what is plainly and unambiguously taught in Scripture, those who do not hesitate to brand with reproach what is taught in this way by the sacred oracles had better take care what kind of censure they use. If, under a pretense of ignorance, they seek the praise of modesty, what greater arrogance can be imagined than to utter one word against the authority of God, to say, for instance, "I think otherwise," or "I would not have this subject touched"? But if they openly blaspheme, what will they gain by assaulting heaven? Such insolence, indeed, is not new. In every age there have been wicked and profane men who have furiously attacked this part of doctrine. But what the Spirit declared long ago by the mouth of David (Psalm 51:6), they will find by experience to be true: God will prevail when he is judged. David indirectly rebukes the folly of those whose license is so unbridled that, from their lowly patch of earth, they not only plead against God but claim for themselves the right to censure him. At the same time he briefly indicates that the blasphemies they belch out against heaven, instead of reaching God, only make his justice shine the more when the mists of their slanders are scattered. Even our faith, because it is founded on the sacred word of God, is superior to the whole world, and is able from its height to look down on such mists.

Their first objection, that if nothing happens without the will of God he must have two contrary wills, decreeing by a secret counsel what he has openly forbidden in his law, is easily disposed of. But before I answer it, I would again remind my readers that this quibble is directed not against me, but against the Holy Spirit, who certainly dictated this confession to that holy man Job: "The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away," when, after being plundered by robbers, he acknowledges that their injustice and mischief were a just chastisement from God. And what does Scripture say elsewhere? The sons of Eli "hearkened not unto the voice of their father, because the Lord would slay them" (1 Samuel 2:25). Another prophet also exclaims, "Our God is in the heavens: he has done whatsoever he has pleased" (Psalm 115:3). I have already shown clearly enough that God is the author of all those things that, according to these objectors, happen only by his idle permission. He testifies that he creates light and darkness and forms good and evil (Isaiah 45:7), and that no evil happens which he has not done (Amos 3:6). Let them tell me whether God carries out his judgments willingly or unwillingly. As Moses teaches that the man who is accidentally killed by the blow of an axe is delivered by God into the hand of the one who strikes him (Deuteronomy 19:5), so the Gospel, by the mouth of Luke, declares that Herod and Pontius Pilate conspired "to do whatsoever your hand and your counsel determined before to be done" (Acts 4:28). And in truth, if Christ was not crucified by the will of God, where is our redemption? Yet the will of God is not at variance with itself. It undergoes no change. He does not pretend not to will what he wills; but while in himself his will is one and undivided, to us it appears manifold, because, from the weakness of our understanding, we cannot grasp how, though in a different way, he wills and does not will the very same thing. Paul calls the calling of the Gentiles a hidden mystery, and shortly afterward adds that in it the manifold wisdom of God was made known (Ephesians 3:10). Since, because of the dullness of our perception, the wisdom of God seems manifold (or, as an old interpreter rendered it, multiform), are we therefore to dream of some variation in God, as if he either changed his counsel or disagreed with himself? On the contrary, when we cannot grasp how God can will that to be done which he forbids us to do, let us call to mind our weakness, and remember that the light in which he dwells is not without reason called inaccessible (1 Timothy 6:16), because it is shrouded in darkness. Hence all godly and modest men will readily rest in the judgment of Augustine: "Man sometimes with a good will wishes something which God does not will, as when a good son wishes his father to live, while God wills him to die. Again, it may happen that man with a bad will wishes what God wills righteously, as when a bad son wishes his father to die, and God also wills it. The former wishes what God does not will; the latter wishes what God also wills. And yet the filial affection of the former is more in keeping with the good will of God, though it wills differently, than the unnatural affection of the latter, though it wills the same thing; so much do approval and condemnation depend on what it is fitting for man to will and what for God, and on the end to which the will of each looks. For the things that God rightly wills, he accomplishes through the evil wills of bad men" (Augustine, Enchiridion to Laurentius, chapter 101). He had said a little earlier (chapter 100) that the apostate angels by their revolt, and all the reprobate, did, as far as they themselves were concerned, what God did not will; but with regard to his omnipotence it was impossible for them to do so, for while they act against the will of God, his will is accomplished in them. Hence he exclaims: "Great is the work of God, exquisite in all that he wills! so that, in a manner wonderful and ineffable, that which is done contrary to his will is not done without his will, because it could not be done if he did not permit it; nor does he permit it unwillingly, but willingly; nor would he who is good permit evil to be done, were he not omnipotent to bring good out of evil" (Augustine on Psalm 111:2).

4¶

In the same way another objection is resolved, or rather vanishes of itself, namely: If God not only uses the agency of the wicked, but also governs their counsels and affections, he is the author of all their sins; and therefore men are unjustly condemned for carrying out what God has decreed, because they are obeying his will. Here will is wrongly confused with precept, though it is plain from countless examples that there is the greatest difference between them. The French is, "Car ils meslent perversment le commandement de Dieu avec son vouloir secret, veu qu'il appert par exemples infinis qu'il y a bien longue distance et diversité de l'un à l'autre"; that is, for they perversely confuse the command of God with his secret will, though it appears from an infinite number of examples that there is a great distance and difference between them.

How the things that men do perversely are of God, and are ruled by his secret providence, is strikingly shown in the election of King Jeroboam (1 Kings 12:20), in which the rashness and folly of the people are severely condemned for perverting the order God had sanctioned and treacherously revolting from the house of David. And yet we know it was God's will that Jeroboam should be anointed. Hence the apparent contradiction in the words of Hosea (Hosea 8:4; 13:11): while God complained that that kingdom was set up without his knowledge and against his will, he declares elsewhere that he had given King Jeroboam in his anger. How shall we reconcile the two statements, that Jeroboam's reign was not of God, and yet that God appointed him king? In this way: the people could not revolt from the house of David without throwing off a yoke that God had laid on them, and yet God himself was not deprived of the power of punishing the ingratitude of Solomon in this way. We see, therefore, how God, while not willing treachery, with another end in view justly wills the revolt; and so Jeroboam, by unexpectedly receiving the sacred anointing, is urged on to aspire to the kingdom. For this reason the sacred history says that God stirred up an enemy to deprive the son of Solomon of part of the kingdom (1 Kings 11:23). Let the reader carefully ponder both points: how, since it was the will of God that the people should be ruled by the hand of one king, their being torn into two parties was contrary to his will; and yet how this same will gave rise to the revolt. For certainly, when Jeroboam, who had no such thought, is urged by the prophet, both by word and by the oil of anointing, to hope for the kingdom, the thing was not done without the knowledge or against the will of God, who had expressly commanded it; and yet the rebellion of the people is justly condemned, because it was against the will of God that they revolted from the descendants of David. For this reason it is added afterward that when Rehoboam haughtily spurned the requests of the people, "the cause was from the Lord, that he might perform his saying, which the Lord spoke by Ahijah" (1 Kings 12:15). See how sacred unity was violated against the will of God, while at the same time, by his will, the ten tribes were separated from the son of Solomon. To this might be added another similar example, namely, the murder of the sons of Ahab and the extermination of his whole family by the consent, or rather the active agency, of the people. Jehu says truly, "There shall fall unto the earth nothing of the word of the Lord, which the Lord spoke concerning the house of Ahab: for the Lord has done that which he spoke by his servant Elijah" (2 Kings 10:10). And yet with good reason he reproaches the citizens of Samaria for having lent their help: "You are righteous: behold, I conspired against my master, and slew him: but who slew all these?"

If I am not mistaken, I have already shown clearly how the same act at once reveals the guilt of man and displays the righteousness of God. Modest minds will always be satisfied with Augustine's answer: "Since the Father delivered up the Son, Christ his own body, and Judas his Master, how in such a case is God just and man guilty, except that in the one act which they did, the reasons for which they did it are different?" (Augustine, Letter 48, to Vincentius). If any are not fully satisfied with this explanation, namely, that there is no concurrence between God and man when, by God's righteous impulse, man does what he ought not to do, let them pay attention to what Augustine observes elsewhere: "Who can keep from trembling at those judgments, when God does according to his pleasure even in the hearts of the wicked, while at the same time rendering to them according to their deeds?" (On Grace and Free Will, to Valentinus, chapter 20). And certainly, in regard to the treachery of Judas, there is just as little ground for throwing the blame for the crime on God, because he both willed that his Son should be delivered up to death and did deliver him, as there is for ascribing to Judas the praise of our redemption. Hence Augustine rightly observes in another place that when God makes his examination, he looks not to what men could do, or to what they did, but to what they wished to do, so taking account of their will and purpose. Those to whom this seems harsh had better consider how far their captiousness deserves any toleration, when, on the ground that it exceeds their capacity, they reject a matter clearly taught by Scripture, and complain of the setting forth of truths which God would never have ordered his prophets and apostles to teach if they were not useful to know. Our true wisdom is to embrace with meek teachableness, and without reservation, whatever the Holy Scriptures have delivered. Those who give rein to their insolence, an insolence plainly directed against God, do not deserve a longer refutation.

END OF THE FIRST BOOK.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin holds two truths the carnal mind cannot reconcile and dares not let go: God bends even Satan and the reprobate to his will, yet he remains free from every taint of their sin. The wicked act by their own evil intent; God ordains the end in perfect righteousness — one deed, two wills wholly unlike. For the church in 2026, this guards us against the lazy blasphemy that makes God the author of sin and the equally lazy fatalism that excuses our own. When evil men prosper and corrupt schemes seem to carry the day, we are not to conclude that God has lost the reins or soiled his hands; he is wringing good from their malice without sharing it. Teach your children to hate sin as their own and to trust God as untainted Lord over it. This is sober conviction, not speculation — we adore where we cannot fathom, and we never accuse the holy One who works all things, even the worst things, well.
