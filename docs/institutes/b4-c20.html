Of Civil Government

This chapter consists of two principal heads,—I. General discourse on the necessity, dignity, and use of Civil Government, in opposition to the frantic proceedings of the Anabaptists, sec. 1-3. II. A special exposition of the three leading parts of which Civil Government consists, sec. 4-32. The first part treats of the function of Magistrates, whose authority and calling is proved, sec. 4-7. Next, the three Forms of civil government are added, sec. 8. Thirdly, Consideration of the office of the civil magistrate in respect of piety and righteousness. Here, of rewards and punishments—viz. punishing the guilty, protecting the innocent, repressing the seditious, managing the affairs of peace and war, sec. 9-13. The second part treats of Laws, their utility, necessity, form, authority, constitution, and scope, sec. 14-16. The last part relates to the People, and explains the use of laws, courts, and magistrates, to the common society of Christians, sec. 17-21. 1¶

Having shown above that there is a twofold government in man, and having fully considered the one which is placed in the soul, or inward man, and relates to eternal life, we are here called to say something of the other, which pertains only to civil institutions and the outward regulation of conduct. For although this subject seems by its nature to be unconnected with the spiritual doctrine of faith which I have undertaken to treat, it will appear as we proceed that I have rightly joined them; indeed, that I am under the necessity of doing so, especially since, on the one hand, frantic and barbarous men are furiously striving to overturn the order established by God, and, on the other, the flatterers of princes, extolling their power without measure, do not hesitate to set it against the government of God. Unless we meet both extremes, the purity of the faith will perish. We may add that it concerns us in no small degree to know how kindly God has provided here for the human race, so that godly zeal may urge us the more strongly to testify our gratitude. And first, before entering on the subject itself, we must attend to the distinction which we laid down earlier (Book 3, chapter 19, section 16, and above, chapter 10), lest, as often happens to many, we unwisely confound these two things, whose nature is altogether different. For some, on hearing that liberty is promised in the gospel, a liberty which acknowledges no king and no magistrate among men but looks to Christ alone, think that they can receive no benefit from their liberty so long as they see any power placed over them. Accordingly, they think that nothing will be safe until the whole world is changed into a new form, in which there will be neither courts, nor laws, nor magistrates, nor anything of the kind to interfere, as they suppose, with their liberty. But he who knows how to distinguish between the body and the soul, between this present fleeting life and that which is future and eternal, will have no difficulty in understanding that the spiritual kingdom of Christ and civil government are things very widely separated. Since, therefore, it is a Jewish vanity to seek the kingdom of Christ and enclose it within the elements of this world, let us consider, as Scripture clearly teaches, that the blessings we derive from Christ are spiritual, and remember to confine the liberty which is promised and offered to us in him within its proper limits. For why is it that the very same apostle who bids us "stand fast in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not again entangled with the yoke of bondage" (Galatians 5:1), in another passage forbids slaves to be anxious about their condition (1 Corinthians 7:21), unless it is that spiritual liberty is perfectly compatible with civil servitude? In this sense the following passages are to be understood: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female" (Galatians 3:28). Again, "There is neither Greek nor Jew, circumcision nor uncircumcision, barbarian, Scythian, bond nor free: but Christ is all and in all" (Colossians 3:11). By this it is indicated that it does not matter what your condition is among men, nor under what laws you live, since the kingdom of Christ does not consist in these things at all.

2¶

Still, the distinction does not go so far as to justify us in supposing that the whole scheme of civil government is a thing of pollution, with which Christian men have nothing to do. Fanatics, indeed, delighting in unbridled license, insist and loudly proclaim that, since we are dead through Christ to the elements of this world, and, being transferred into the kingdom of God, sit among the heavenly ones, it is unworthy of us and far beneath our dignity to be occupied with those profane and impure cares which relate to matters foreign to a Christian man. To what end, they say, are laws without courts and tribunals? But what has a Christian man to do with courts? Indeed, if it is unlawful to kill, what have we to do with laws and courts? But as we have just taught that this kind of government is distinct from the spiritual and inward kingdom of Christ, so we ought to know that the two are not opposed to each other. The former in some measure begins the heavenly kingdom in us even now upon earth, and in this mortal and fleeting life commences an immortal and incorruptible blessedness; while to the latter it is assigned, so long as we live among men, to foster and maintain the outward worship of God, to defend sound doctrine and the condition of the Church, to adapt our conduct to human society, to form our manners to civil justice, to reconcile us to one another, and to cherish common peace and tranquility. All these things I confess to be superfluous, if the kingdom of God, as it now exists within us, extinguishes the present life. But if it is the will of God that, while we aspire to true godliness, we are pilgrims upon the earth, and if such a pilgrimage stands in need of such aids, then those who take them away from man rob him of his humanity. As to their claim that there ought to be such perfection in the Church of God that her guidance should suffice for law, they foolishly imagine her to be such as she can never be found in any community of men. For while the insolence of the wicked is so great, and their iniquity so stubborn, that it can scarcely be curbed by any severity of laws, what do we expect would be done by those whom force can scarcely restrain from doing evil, if they were to see perfect impunity for their wickedness?

3¶

But we shall have a fitter opportunity of speaking about the use of civil government. All we wish to be understood at present is that it is sheer barbarism to think of abolishing it, since its use among men is no less than that of bread and water, light and air, while its dignity is far more excellent. Its object is not merely, like those things, to enable men to breathe, eat, drink, and be kept warm (though it certainly includes all these, since it enables them to live together); this, I say, is not its only object. Its object is also that no idolatry, no blasphemy against the name of God, no slanders against his truth, nor other offenses against religion, break out and spread among the people; that the public peace be not disturbed; that every man's property be kept secure; that men may carry on honest commerce with one another; that honesty and modesty be cultivated; in short, that a public form of religion may exist among Christians, and humanity among men. Let no one be surprised that I now assign to human government the task of rightly establishing religion, though I seem above to have placed it beyond the will of man. For I no more than before allow men to enact laws at their pleasure concerning religion and the worship of God, when I approve of a civil order which is directed to this end, namely, to prevent the true religion, which is contained in the law of God, from being openly violated and polluted with impunity by public blasphemy. But the reader, with the help of a clear arrangement, will better understand what view is to be taken of the whole order of civil government if we treat each of its parts separately. Now these are three: the Magistrate, who is the president and guardian of the laws; the Laws, according to which he governs; and the People, who are governed by the laws and obey the magistrate. Let us consider, then, first, what is the function of the magistrate? Is it a lawful calling approved by God? What is the nature of his duty? What is the extent of his power? Secondly, what are the laws by which a Christian commonwealth is to be regulated? And lastly, what is the use of laws as regards the people? And what obedience is due to the magistrate?

4¶

With regard to the function of magistrates, the Lord has not only declared that he approves of it and is pleased with it, but has, moreover, strongly commended it to us by the very honorable titles which he has conferred upon it. To mention a, few. Exodus 22:8, 9; Psalm 82:1, 6; John 10:34, 35; Deuteronomy 1:16, 17; 2 Chronicles 19:6, 7; Proverbs 8:15.

5¶

Those who are desirous to introduce anarchy French, "Ceux qui voudroyent que les hommes vesquissent pesle mesle comme rats en paille;" that is, Those who would have men live pell-mell like rats among straw.

6¶

This consideration ought to be constantly present to the minds of magistrates, since it is fitted to supply a strong spur to the discharge of their duty, and also to afford them singular consolation, smoothing the difficulties of their office, which are certainly many and weighty. What zeal for integrity, prudence, gentleness, self-control, and innocence ought to govern those who know that they have been appointed ministers of the divine justice! How will they dare to admit iniquity to their tribunal, when they are told that it is the throne of the living God? How will they venture to pronounce an unjust sentence with that mouth which they understand to be an ordained instrument of divine truth? With what conscience will they sign wicked decrees with that hand which they know has been appointed to write the acts of God? In a word, if they remember that they are the vicegerents of God, it is their duty to watch with all care, diligence, and industry, so that they may display in themselves a kind of image of the divine providence, guardianship, goodness, benevolence, and justice. And let them constantly keep this further thought in view: that if a curse is pronounced on him that "doeth the work of the Lord deceitfully," a much heavier curse must lie on him who deals deceitfully in a righteous calling. Therefore, when Moses and Jehoshaphat wished to urge their judges to the discharge of their duty, they had nothing by which they could more powerfully stir their minds than the consideration to which we have already referred: "Take heed what ye do: for ye judge not for man, but for the Lord, who is with you in the judgment. Wherefore now let the fear of the Lord be upon you; take heed and do it: for there is no iniquity with the Lord our God, nor respect of persons, nor taking of gifts" (2 Chronicles 19:6, 7, compared with Deuteronomy 1:16, and following). And in another passage it is said, "God standeth in the congregation of the mighty; he judgeth among the gods" (Psalm 82:1; Isaiah 3:14), so that they may be roused to duty when they hear that they are the ambassadors of God, to whom they must one day render an account of the charge committed to them. This admonition ought rightly to have the greatest effect upon them; for if they sin in any respect, not only is injury done to the men whom they wickedly torment, but they also insult God himself, whose sacred tribunals they pollute. On the other hand, they have an admirable source of comfort when they reflect that they are not engaged in profane occupations unfitting for a servant of God, but in a most sacred office, inasmuch as they are the ambassadors of God.

7¶

As for those who are not restrained by all these passages of Scripture from presuming to rail against this sacred ministry, as if it were a thing repugnant to religion and Christian godliness, what else do they do but assail God himself, who cannot but be insulted when his servants are disgraced? These men not only speak evil of dignities, but would not even have God reign over them (1 Samuel 7:7). For if this was truly said of the people of Israel when they rejected the authority of Samuel, how can it be less truly said in the present day of those who allow themselves to break loose against all the authority established by God? But it seems that when our Lord said to his disciples, "The kings of the Gentiles exercise lordship over them; and they that exercise authority upon them are called benefactors. But ye shall not be so: but he that is greatest among you, let him be as the younger; and he that is chief, as he that doth serve" (Luke 22:25, 26), he by these words forbade all Christians to become kings or governors. Skillful interpreters! A dispute had arisen among the disciples as to which of them should be greatest. To suppress this vain ambition, our Lord taught them that their ministry was not like the power of earthly sovereigns, among whom one greatly surpasses another. What, I ask, is there in this comparison that disparages royal dignity? Indeed, what does it prove at all, except that the royal office is not the apostolic ministry? Besides, though among the offices of magistrates themselves there are different forms, there is no difference in this respect, that they are all to be received by us as ordinances of God. For Paul includes them all together when he says that "there is no power but of God"; and that form which was by no means the most pleasing of all was honored with the highest testimony, I mean the rule of one. This form, since it carries with it the public servitude of all (except the one to whose despotic will everything is subject), was in ancient times disliked by heroic and more excellent natures. But Scripture, to forestall these unjust judgments, expressly affirms that it is by divine wisdom that "kings reign," and gives a special command "to honor the king" (1 Peter 2:17).

8¶

And certainly it would be a very idle occupation for private men to debate what would be the best form of government in the place where they live, since these deliberations cannot have any influence in deciding any public matter. Then, too, the question itself could not be settled absolutely without rashness, since the nature of the discussion depends on circumstances. And if you compare the different forms of state with one another, apart from circumstances, it is not easy to determine which of them has the advantage in usefulness, so equal are the terms on which they meet. Monarchy is prone to tyranny. In an aristocracy, again, the tendency is no less toward the faction of a few, while in popular rule there is the strongest tendency to sedition. French "On conte trois especes de regime civil: c'est assavoir Monarchie, qui est la domination d'un seul, soit qu'on le nomme Roy ou Due, ou autrement: Aristoeratie qui est une domination gouvernee par les principaux et gens d'apparence: et Democratie, qui est une domination populaire, en laquelle chacun du peuple a puissance." That is, There are three kinds of civil government; namely, Monarchy, which is the rule of one only, whether he is called King or Duke, or otherwise; Aristocracy, which is a government composed of the chief men and people of note; and Democracy, which is a popular government, in which each of the people has power.

9¶

The duty of magistrates, its nature as described by the word of God, and the things in which it consists, I will here indicate in passing. That it extends to both tables of the law, we might learn from pagan writers even if Scripture did not teach it; for no man has discussed the duty of magistrates, the making of laws, and the common good without beginning with religion and divine worship. Thus all have confessed that no government can be successfully established unless godliness is its first care, and that those laws are absurd which disregard the rights of God and provide only for men. Since, then, religion holds the first place among the philosophers, and the same thing has always been observed by the universal consent of nations, Christian princes and magistrates may be ashamed of their indifference if they do not make it their care. We have already shown that this office is specially assigned to them by God; and indeed it is right that they should exert themselves in asserting and defending the honor of him whose vicegerents they are, and by whose favor they rule. Hence in Scripture holy kings are especially praised for restoring the worship of God when it had been corrupted or overthrown, or for taking care that religion flourished under them in purity and safety. On the other hand, the sacred history counts anarchy among the vices when it states that there was no king in Israel, and that therefore everyone did as he pleased (Judges 21:25). This rebukes the folly of those who would neglect the care of divine things and devote themselves merely to the administration of justice among men; as if God had appointed rulers in his own name to decide earthly disputes, and had omitted what was of far greater moment, his own pure worship as prescribed by his law. Such views are adopted by turbulent men who, in their eagerness to make all kinds of innovations with impunity, would gladly get rid of all the defenders of violated godliness. In regard to the second table of the law, Jeremiah addresses rulers: "Thus saith the Lord, Execute ye judgment and righteousness, and deliver the spoiled out of the hand of the oppressor: and do no wrong, do no violence to the stranger, the fatherless, nor the widow, neither shed innocent blood" (Jeremiah 22:3). To the same effect is the exhortation in the Psalm: "Defend the poor and fatherless; do justice to the afflicted and needy. Deliver the poor and needy; rid them out of the hand of the wicked" (Psalm 82:3, 4). Moses also declared to the princes whom he had appointed in his place, "Hear the causes between your brethren, and judge righteously between every man and his brother, and the stranger that is with him. Ye shall not respect persons in judgment; but ye shall hear the small as well as the great: ye shall not be afraid of the face of man, for the judgment is God's" (Deuteronomy 1:16). I say nothing of such passages as these: "He shall not multiply horses to himself, nor cause the people to return to Egypt"; "neither shall he multiply wives to himself; neither shall he greatly multiply to himself silver and gold"; "he shall write him a copy of this law in a book"; "and it shall be with him, and he shall read therein all the days of his life, that he may learn to fear the Lord his God"; "that his heart be not lifted up above his brethren" (Deuteronomy 17:16-20). In explaining here the duties of magistrates, my exposition is intended not so much for the instruction of magistrates themselves as to teach others why there are magistrates, and to what end they have been appointed by God. We say, therefore, that they are the appointed guardians and defenders of public innocence, modesty, honor, and tranquility, so that their only concern should be to provide for the common peace and safety. Of these things David declares that he will set an example when he has ascended the throne: "A froward heart shall depart from me: I will not know a wicked person. Whoso privily slandereth his neighbor, him will I cut off: him that hath an high look and a proud heart will not I suffer. Mine eyes shall be upon the faithful of the land, that they may dwell with me: he that walketh in a perfect way, he shall serve me" (Psalm 101:4-6). But since rulers cannot do this unless they protect the good against the injuries of the bad and give aid and protection to the oppressed, they are armed with power to curb open evildoers and criminals, by whose misconduct the public tranquility is disturbed or harassed. For we have full experience of the truth of Solon's saying, that all public matters depend on reward and punishment, and that where these are lacking, the whole discipline of states totters and falls to pieces. For in the minds of many the love of equity and justice grows cold if due honor is not paid to virtue; and the license of the wicked cannot be restrained without strict discipline and the infliction of punishment. The two things are included by the prophet when he commands kings and other rulers to execute "judgment and righteousness" (Jeremiah 21:12; 22:3). It is righteousness (justice) to take charge of the innocent, to defend and avenge them, and to set them free; it is judgment to withstand the audacity of the wicked, to repress their violence, and to punish their crimes.

10¶

But here a difficult and, as it seems, a perplexing question arises. If all Christians are forbidden to kill, and the prophet predicts concerning the holy mountain of the Lord, that is, the Church, Exodus 20:13; Deuteronomy 5:17; Matthew 5:21; Isaiah 11:9; 65:25.

Tout ainsi que quand un prince defend à tou sses sujets de porter baston ou blesser aucun, il n'empeehe pas neantmoins ses officiers d'executer la justice, laquelie il leur a specialement commise." 11¶

Since it is sometimes necessary for kings and states to take up arms in order to execute public vengeance, the reason already given supplies us with the means of judging how far the wars thus undertaken are lawful. For if power has been given them to maintain the tranquility of their subjects, to repress the seditious movements of the turbulent, to help those who are violently oppressed, and to punish crimes, can they use it more fittingly than in repressing the fury of him who disturbs both the ease of individuals and the common tranquility of all, who stirs up seditious tumult, and commits acts of violent oppression and gross wrong? If it is their part to be the guardians and upholders of the laws, they must repress the attempts of all alike by whose criminal conduct the discipline of the laws is impaired. Indeed, if they justly punish those robbers whose injuries have been inflicted on only a few, will they allow the whole country to be robbed and laid waste with impunity? Since it makes no difference whether it is a king or the lowest of the people who makes a hostile and devastating raid into a district over which he has no authority, all alike are to be regarded and punished as robbers. Natural equity and duty, therefore, demand that princes be armed not only to repress private crimes by judicial penalties, but also to defend the subjects committed to their guardianship whenever they are attacked by an enemy. Such wars even the Holy Spirit, in many passages of Scripture, declares to be lawful.

12¶

But if it is objected that in the New Testament there is no passage or example teaching that war is lawful for Christians, I answer, first, that the reason for waging war which existed in ancient times still exists in the present day, and that, on the other hand, there is no ground for barring magistrates from the defense of those under them; secondly, that in the apostolic writings we are not to look for a distinct exposition of those matters, since their object is not to frame a civil government but to establish the spiritual kingdom of Christ; and lastly, that there also it is indicated in passing that our Savior, by his coming, made no change in this respect. For (to use the words of Augustine) "if Christian discipline condemned all wars, then when the soldiers asked counsel about the way of salvation, they would have been told to cast away their arms and withdraw altogether from military service. Whereas it was said (Luke 3:14), Extort from no one, do injury to no one, be content with your pay. Those whom he orders to be content with their pay he certainly does not forbid to serve" (Augustine, Letter 5, to Marcellinus). But all magistrates must here be particularly careful not to give way in the slightest degree to their passions. Rather, if punishments are to be inflicted, they must not be swept along by anger, nor carried away by hatred, nor burn with implacable severity; they must, as Augustine says (City of God, book 5, chapter 24), "even pity a common nature in him in whom they punish an individual fault." Or if they have to take up arms against an enemy, that is, an armed robber, they must not readily seize the opportunity; indeed, they must not take it even when it is offered, unless compelled by the strongest necessity. For if we are to do far more than that heathen demanded who wished war to appear as peace sought for, then assuredly all other means must be tried before having recourse to arms. In short, in both cases they must not allow themselves to be carried away by any private feeling, but must be guided solely by regard for the public good. If they act otherwise, they wickedly abuse their power, which was given them not for their own advantage but for the good and service of others. On this right of war depends the right of garrisons, leagues, and other civil defenses. By garrisons I mean those troops which are stationed in states for the defense of the frontiers; by leagues, the alliances which are made by neighboring princes on the understanding that, if any disturbance arises within their territories, they will assist one another and combine their forces to repel the common enemies of the human race; under civil defenses I include everything pertaining to the military art.

13¶

Lastly, we think it proper to add that taxes and duties are the lawful revenues of princes, which they are chiefly to employ in bearing the public burdens of their office. These, however, they may also use for the maintenance of their household state, which is in a manner joined with the dignity of the authority they exercise. Thus we see that David, Hezekiah, Josiah, Jehoshaphat, and other holy kings, and Joseph and Daniel also, spent liberally from the public funds in proportion to the office they held, without offending against godliness; and we read in Ezekiel that a very large extent of territory was assigned to kings (Ezekiel 48:21). In that passage, indeed, he is depicting the spiritual kingdom of Christ, but he still borrows his picture from lawful dominion among men. Princes, however, must remember in their turn that their revenues are not so much private chests as treasuries of the whole people (as Paul testifies, Romans 13:6), which they cannot squander or waste without manifest injustice; or rather, that they are almost the very blood of the people, which it would be the harshest inhumanity not to spare. They should also consider that their levies and contributions, and other kinds of taxes, are merely aids for the public necessity, and that it is tyrannical greed to burden the poor people with them without cause. These things are not meant to stir princes to lavishness and luxurious spending (there is certainly no need to inflame their desires, when these are already, of their own accord, inflamed more than enough). But since it is of the greatest consequence that whatever they venture to do, they should do with a pure conscience, it is necessary to teach them how far they can lawfully go, lest by wicked presumption they incur the divine displeasure. Nor is this doctrine superfluous for private individuals, so that they may not rashly and insolently condemn the expenditure of princes, even if it should exceed the ordinary limits.

14¶

In states, the thing next in importance to the magistrates is the laws, the strongest sinews of government, or, as Cicero calls them after Plato, its soul, without which the office of the magistrate cannot exist; just as, on the other hand, laws have no force without the magistrate. Hence nothing could be said more truly than that the law is a silent magistrate, and the magistrate a living law. Although I have undertaken to describe the laws by which a Christian commonwealth is to be governed, there is no reason to expect from me a long discussion of the best kind of laws. The subject is of vast extent and does not belong to this place. I will only observe briefly, in passing, what laws may be used with godliness before God and rightly administered among men. This I would rather have passed over in silence, were I not aware that many dangerous errors are committed here. For there are some who deny that any commonwealth is rightly framed which neglects the law of Moses and is ruled by the common laws of nations. How perilous and seditious these views are, let others consider; for me it is enough to show that they are foolish and false. We must keep in mind the well-known division which distributes the whole law of God, as published by Moses, into the moral, the ceremonial, and the judicial law; and we must consider each of these parts, in order to understand how far they do or do not pertain to us. Meanwhile, let no one be troubled by the thought that the judicial and ceremonial laws also relate to morals. For the ancients who adopted this division, though they were not unaware that the two latter classes had to do with morals, did not give them the name of moral, because they might be changed and abrogated without affecting morals. They give this name specially to the first class, without which true holiness of life and an unchangeable rule of conduct cannot exist.

15¶

The moral law, then (to begin with it), being contained under two heads, of which the one simply commands us to worship God with pure faith and godliness, and the other to embrace men with sincere love, is the true and eternal rule of righteousness, prescribed to men of all nations and of all times who would frame their lives in agreement with the will of God. For his eternal and unchangeable will is that we all worship him and love one another. The ceremonial law of the Jews was a tutelage by which the Lord was pleased to train, as it were, the childhood of that people, until the fullness of the time should come, when he was fully to manifest his wisdom to the world and to display the reality of those things which were then foreshadowed by figures (Galatians 3:24; 4:4). The judicial law, given them as a kind of civil constitution, laid down certain forms of equity and justice by which they might live together innocently and peaceably. And just as that exercise in ceremonies properly belonged to the doctrine of godliness, inasmuch as it kept the Jewish Church in the worship and religion of God, yet was still distinguishable from godliness itself, so the judicial form, though it looked only to the best method of preserving that love which is commanded by the eternal law of God, was still something distinct from the precept of love itself. Therefore, as the ceremonies might be abrogated without at all interfering with godliness, so also, when these judicial arrangements are removed, the duties and precepts of love can still remain perpetual. But if it is true that each nation has been left at liberty to enact the laws which it judges to be beneficial, these are still always to be tested by the rule of love, so that while they vary in form, they must proceed on the same principle. Those barbarous and savage laws, for instance, which conferred honor on thieves, permitted the promiscuous intercourse of the sexes, and allowed other things even fouler and more absurd, I do not think deserve to be considered laws, since they are altogether abhorrent not only to justice but to humanity and civilized life.

16¶

What I have said will become plain if we attend, as we ought, to two things connected with all laws, namely, the enactment of the law, and the equity on which the enactment is founded and rests. Equity, being natural, can only be one and the same for all, and therefore all laws ought to aim at it, each according to the nature of the thing enacted. Since constitutions have certain circumstances on which they partly depend, there is nothing to prevent their diversity, provided they all alike aim at equity as their end. Now, since it is evident that the law of God which we call moral is nothing else than the testimony of natural law and of that conscience which God has engraved on the minds of men, the whole of this equity of which we now speak is prescribed in it. Hence it alone ought to be the aim, the rule, and the end of all laws. Wherever laws are framed according to this rule, directed to this aim, and limited to this end, there is no reason why we should disapprove of them, however much they may differ from the Jewish law or from one another (Augustine, City of God, book 19, chapter 17). The law of God forbids stealing. The punishment appointed for theft in the civil government of the Jews may be seen in Exodus 22. Very ancient laws of other nations punished theft by exacting double the amount stolen, while later laws made a distinction between manifest theft and theft not manifest. Other laws went as far as punishing it with exile or with branding, while others made the punishment capital. Among the Jews, the punishment of the false witness was to "do unto him as he had thought to have done unto his brother" (Deuteronomy 19:19). In some countries the punishment is public disgrace, in others hanging, in others crucifixion. All laws alike avenge murder with blood, but the kinds of death differ. In some countries adultery was punished more severely, in others more leniently. Yet we see that amid this diversity they all tend to the same end. For with one voice they all declare against those crimes which are condemned by the eternal law of God, namely, murder, theft, adultery, and false witness, though they do not agree as to the manner of punishment. Such agreement is not necessary, nor even expedient. There may be a country which, if murder were not visited with fearful punishments, would immediately become a prey to robbery and slaughter. There may be an age which requires that the severity of punishments be increased. If the state is in a troubled condition, those things from which disturbances usually arise must be corrected by new edicts. In time of war, civilization would disappear amid the noise of arms unless men were overawed by an unusual severity of punishment. In times of famine or pestilence, unless a stricter discipline were employed, all things would grow worse. One nation might be more prone to a particular vice unless it were most severely repressed. How spiteful it would be, and how hostile to the public good, to take offense at this diversity, which is admirably suited to maintain the observance of the divine law! The claim that insult is offered to the law of God enacted by Moses when it is abrogated and other new laws are preferred to it is utterly absurd. Other laws are not preferred to it when they are more approved, not absolutely, but with regard to time and place and the condition of the people; nor is it insulted when those things are abrogated which were never enacted for us. The Lord did not give it by the hand of Moses to be published in all countries and enforced everywhere; but having taken the Jewish nation under his special care, patronage, and guardianship, he was pleased to be in a special way its lawgiver, and, as became a wise lawgiver, he had special regard to that nation in enacting its laws.

17¶

It now remains to see, as was proposed in the last place, what use the common society of Christians derives from laws, judicial proceedings, and magistrates. With this is connected another question, namely, what deference private individuals ought to pay to magistrates, and how far obedience ought to go. To very many it seems that among Christians the office of magistrate is superfluous, because they cannot with godliness implore his aid, inasmuch as they are forbidden to take revenge, to summon anyone before a judge, or to go to law. But when Paul, on the contrary, clearly declares that he is the minister of God to us for good (Romans 13:4), we understand by this that he was so ordained by God that, being defended by his hand and aid against the dishonesty and injustice of wicked men, we may live quiet and secure. But if he would have been appointed over us in vain unless we were to use his aid, it is plain that it cannot be wrong to appeal to it and implore it. Here, indeed, I have to deal with two classes of men. For there are very many who boil with such a rage for litigation that they can never be at peace with themselves unless they are fighting with others. They prosecute lawsuits with the bitterness of deadly hatred and with an insane eagerness to hurt and take revenge, and they persist in them with implacable obstinacy, even to the ruin of their adversary. Meanwhile, so that they may be thought to do nothing but what is legal, they use this pretext of judicial proceedings as a defense of their perverse conduct. But if it is lawful for brother to go to law with brother, it does not follow that it is lawful to hate him and obstinately pursue him with a furious desire to do him harm.

18¶

Let such persons, then, understand that judicial proceedings are lawful to him who makes a right use of them. And the right use, both for the plaintiff and for the defendant, is this: for the defendant, to present himself on the appointed day and, without bitterness, to urge what he can in his defense, but only with the desire of justly maintaining his right; and for the plaintiff, when he is undeservedly attacked in his life or fortunes, to throw himself on the protection of the magistrate, state his complaint, and demand what is just and good, while, far from any wish to hurt or take vengeance, far from bitterness or hatred, far from the heat of strife, he is rather disposed to yield and suffer something than to cherish hostile feelings toward his opponent. On the contrary, when minds are filled with malice, corrupted by envy, burning with anger, breathing revenge, or, in short, so inflamed by the heat of the contest that they in some measure lay aside love, the whole pleading, even of the justest cause, cannot but be ungodly. For it ought to be an axiom among all Christians that no lawsuit, however equitable, can be rightly conducted by anyone who does not feel as kindly toward his opponent as if the matter in dispute were being settled and arranged on friendly terms. Someone, perhaps, may break in here and say that such moderation in judicial proceedings is so far from being seen that an instance of it would be a kind of marvel. I confess that in these times it is rare to meet with an example of an honest litigant; but the thing itself, untainted by the addition of evil, does not cease to be good and pure. When we hear that the assistance of the magistrate is a sacred gift from God, we ought the more carefully to beware of polluting it by our own fault.

19¶

Let those who flatly condemn all judicial distinction know that they reject the holy ordinance of God, and one of those gifts which to the pure are pure, unless indeed they would charge Paul with a crime, Acts 22, 24:12; 16:37; 22:25; 25:10; Leviticus 19:18; Matthew 5:39; Deuteronomy 32:35; Romans 12:19.

20¶

Nor are we at variance with the words of Christ, who forbids us to resist evil, and adds, "Whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also. And if any man will sue thee at the law, and take away thy coat, let him have thy cloak also" (Matthew 5:39, 40). He would have the minds of his followers so averse to everything like retaliation that they would sooner allow the injury to be doubled than desire to repay it. From this patience we do not dissuade them. For truly Christians were to be a class of men born to endure insults and injuries, and to be exposed to the iniquity, deceit, and mockery of abandoned men; and not only so, but they were to be patient under all these evils, that is, so composed in the whole frame of their minds that, on receiving one offense, they were to prepare themselves for another, promising themselves nothing during the whole of life but the bearing of a perpetual cross. Meanwhile, they must do good to those who injure them and pray for those who curse them, and (this is their only victory) strive to overcome evil with good (Romans 12:20, 21). Thus disposed, they will not seek eye for eye and tooth for tooth (as the Pharisees taught their disciples to long for vengeance), but (as we are instructed by Christ) they will allow their body to be maimed and their goods to be maliciously taken from them, ready to forgive and freely pardon those injuries the moment they have been inflicted. This equity and moderation, however, will not prevent them, while keeping entire friendship toward their enemies, from using the aid of the magistrate for the preservation of their goods, or, from zeal for the public interest, from calling for the punishment of the wicked and pestilent man whom they know nothing but death will reform. All these precepts are truly expounded by Augustine as tending to prepare the just and godly man to bear patiently the malice of those whom he desires to become good, so that he may thus increase the number of the good, and not add himself to the number of the bad by imitating their wickedness. Moreover, it pertains more to the preparation of the heart, which is within, than to the work which is done openly, that patience and goodwill be kept within the secret of the heart, and that what we see may do good to those to whom we ought to wish well be done openly (Augustine, Letter 5, to Marcellinus).

21¶

The common objection that lawsuits are universally condemned by Paul (1 Corinthians 6:6) is false. It may easily be understood from his words that a rage for litigation prevailed in the Church of Corinth to such a degree that they exposed the gospel of Christ, and the whole religion which they professed, to the slanders and cavils of the ungodly. Paul rebukes them, first, for bringing reproach on the gospel among unbelievers by the intemperance of their quarrels; and, secondly, for striving so with one another while they were brethren. For so far were they from bearing injury from one another that they greedily coveted each other's possessions, and of their own accord provoked and injured one another. He inveighs, therefore, against that madness for litigation, and not absolutely against all kinds of disputes. He declares it to be altogether a fault or weakness that they do not submit to the loss of their possessions rather than strive, even to the point of contention, to preserve them. In other words, since they were so easily moved by every kind of loss, and on every occasion, however slight, ran off to the court and to lawsuits, he says that in this way they showed that they were of too irritable a temper and not prepared for patience. Christians should always be disposed rather to give up part of their right than to go into court, from which they can scarcely come out without a troubled mind, a mind inflamed with hatred of their brother. But when someone sees that he is able, without any loss of love, to defend his property, the lack of which he would grievously feel, then if he does so, he in no respect offends against that passage of Paul. In short, as we said at first, every man's best counselor is love. Everything in which we engage without love, and all the disputes which carry us beyond it, are unquestionably unjust and ungodly.

22¶

The first duty of subjects toward their rulers is to hold the most honorable views of their office, recognizing it as a jurisdiction delegated by God, and on that account receiving and reverencing them as the ministers and ambassadors of God. For you will find some who show themselves very obedient to magistrates, and would be unwilling that there should be no magistrates to obey, because they know this is expedient for the public good; and yet the opinion which these persons hold of magistrates is that they are a kind of necessary evil. But Peter requires something more of us when he says, "Honor the king" (1 Peter 2:17); and so does Solomon when he says, "My son, fear thou the Lord and the king" (Proverbs 24:21). For under the term honor, the former includes a sincere and candid esteem; and the latter, by joining the king with God, shows that he is invested with a kind of sacred veneration and dignity. We have also the remarkable command of Paul, "Be subject not only for wrath, but also for conscience' sake" (Romans 13:5). By this he means that subjects, in submitting to princes and governors, are not to be moved merely by fear (as those submit to an armed enemy who see vengeance ready to be carried out if they resist), but because the obedience which they render is rendered to God himself, inasmuch as their power is from God. I do not speak of the men themselves, as if the mask of dignity could cloak folly, or cowardice, or cruelty, or wicked and shameful conduct, and so win for vice the praise of virtue; but I say that the station itself is deserving of honor and reverence, and that those who rule should, with respect to their office, be held by us in esteem and veneration.

23¶

From this follows a second consequence: that we must prove our obedience to them with ready minds, whether in complying with edicts, or in paying taxes, or in undertaking public offices and burdens which relate to the common defense, or in carrying out any other orders. "Let every soul," says Paul, "be subject unto the higher powers." "Whosoever, therefore, resisteth the power, resisteth the ordinance of God" (Romans 13:1, 2). Writing to Titus, he says, "Put them in mind to be subject to principalities and powers, to obey magistrates, to be ready to every good work" (Titus 3:1). Peter also says, "Submit yourselves to every human creature" (or rather, as I understand it, "ordinance of man"), "for the Lord's sake: whether it be to the king, as supreme; or unto governors, as unto them that are sent by him for the punishment of evildoers, and for the praise of them that do well" (1 Peter 2:13). Moreover, to testify that they do not feign subjection but are sincerely and heartily subject, Paul adds that they are to commend to God the safety and prosperity of those under whom they live. "I exhort, therefore," he says, "that, first of all, supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men; for kings, and for all that are in authority: that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and honesty" (1 Timothy 2:1, 2). Let no man deceive himself here, since we cannot resist the magistrate without resisting God. For although an unarmed magistrate may seem to be despised with impunity, yet God is armed and will signally avenge this contempt. Under this obedience I include the restraint which private men ought to impose on themselves in public matters: not meddling with public business, or rashly encroaching on the province of the magistrate, or attempting anything at all of a public nature. If anything in a public ordinance needs to be corrected, let them not act tumultuously, or put their hands to a work where they ought to feel that their hands are tied, but let them leave it to the judgment of the magistrate, whose hand alone is free here. My meaning is, let them not dare to do it without being ordered. For when the command of the magistrate is given, they too are invested with public authority. For as, according to the common saying, the eyes and ears of the prince are his counselors, so one may not improperly say that those who, by his command, have the charge of managing affairs are his hands.

24¶

But since we have so far described the magistrate who truly is what he is called, namely, the father of his country, and (as the Poet says) the shepherd of the people, the guardian of peace, the president of justice, the defender of innocence, he is rightly to be judged a madman who disapproves of such authority. But in almost every age we see that some princes, careless of all the duties to which they ought to have been attentive, live without concern in luxurious sloth; others, bent on their own interest, sell for gain all rights, privileges, judgments, and enactments; others plunder the poor people of their money and afterward squander it in insane largesse; others act as mere robbers, plundering houses, violating wives, and slaying the innocent. And so many cannot be persuaded to recognize such persons as princes whose commands, as far as they are lawful, they are bound to obey. For while, in such unworthy conduct and amid atrocities so foreign not only to the duty of the magistrate but also to that of a man, they behold no appearance of the image of God, which ought to shine in the magistrate; while they see not a trace of that minister of God who was appointed to be a praise to the good and a terror to the bad, they cannot recognize the ruler whose dignity and authority Scripture commends to us. And undoubtedly the natural feeling of the human mind has always been no less to assail tyrants with hatred and execration than to look up to just kings with love and veneration.

25¶

But if we have regard to the word of God, it will lead us further, and make us subject not only to the authority of those princes who honestly and faithfully perform their duty toward us, but to all princes, by whatever means they have come to be such, even though there is nothing they perform less than the duty of princes. For though the Lord declares that a ruler to maintain our safety is the highest gift of his kindness, and prescribes to rulers themselves their proper sphere, he at the same time declares that, of whatever sort they may be, they derive their power from none but him. Those, indeed, who rule for the public good are true examples and specimens of his kindness, while those who domineer unjustly and tyrannically are raised up by him to punish the people for their iniquity. Still, all alike possess that sacred majesty with which he has invested lawful power. I will not proceed further without adding some distinct passages to this effect. Job 34:30; Hosea 13:11; Isaiah 3:4; 10:5; Deuteronomy 28:29.

26¶

And first, I would have the reader attend carefully to that divine providence which, not without cause, is so often set before us in Scripture, and to that special act of distributing kingdoms and setting up as kings whomever he pleases. In Daniel it is said, "He changeth the times and the seasons: he removeth kings, and setteth up kings" (Daniel 2:21, 37). Again, "That the living may know that the Most High ruleth in the kingdom of men, and giveth it to whomsoever he will" (Daniel 4:17, 25). Similar statements occur throughout Scripture, but they abound particularly in the prophetic books. What kind of king Nebuchadnezzar was, he who stormed Jerusalem, is well known. He was a vigorous invader and destroyer of other countries. Yet the Lord declares in Ezekiel that he had given him the land of Egypt as his wages for the devastation which he had carried out. Daniel also said to him, "You, O king, are a king of kings: for the God of heaven hath given you a kingdom, power, and strength, and glory. And wheresoever the children of men dwell, the beasts of the field and the fowls of the heaven hath he given into your hand, and hath made you ruler over them all" (Daniel 2:37, 38). Again, he says to his son Belshazzar, "The most high God gave Nebuchadnezzar your father a kingdom, and majesty, and glory, and honor: and for the majesty that he gave him, all people, nations, and languages, trembled and feared before him" (Daniel 5:18, 19). When we hear that the king was appointed by God, let us at the same time call to mind those heavenly commands about honoring and fearing the king, and we shall have no doubt that we are to regard the most wicked tyrant as occupying the place with which the Lord has honored him. When Samuel declared to the people of Israel what they would suffer from their kings, he said, "This will be the manner of the king that shall reign over you: He will take your sons, and appoint them for himself, for his chariots, and to be his horsemen; and some shall run before his chariots. And he will appoint him captains over thousands, and captains over fifties; and will set them to ear his ground, and to reap his harvest, and to make his instruments of war, and instruments of his chariots. And he will take your daughters to be confectioneries, and to be cooks, and to be bakers. And he will take your fields, and your vineyards, and your oliveyards, even the best of them, and give them to his servants. And he will take the tenth of your seed, and of your vineyards, and give to his officers, and to his servants. And he will take your menservants, and your maidservants, and your goodliest young men, and your asses, and put them to his work. He will take the tenth of your sheep: and ye shall be his servants" (1 Samuel 8:11-17). Certainly these things could not be done lawfully by kings, whom the law trained most admirably to every kind of restraint; but it was called justice with regard to the people, because they were bound to obey and could not lawfully resist; as if Samuel had said, To such a degree will kings indulge in tyranny, and it will not be for you to restrain it. The only thing left for you will be to receive their commands and be obedient to their words.

27¶

But the most remarkable and memorable passage is in Jeremiah. Though it is rather long, I am not unwilling to quote it, because it most clearly settles this whole question. "I have made the earth, the man and the beast that are upon the ground, by my great power, and by my outstretched arm, and have given it unto whom it seemed meet unto me. And now have I given all these lands into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar the king of Babylon, my servant: and the beasts of the field have I given him also to serve him. And all nations shall serve him, and his son, and his son's son, until the very time of his land come: and then many nations and great kings shall serve themselves of him. And it shall come to pass, that the nation and kingdom which will not serve the same Nebuchadnezzar the king of Babylon, and that will not put their neck under the yoke of the king of Babylon, that nation will I punish, saith the Lord, with the sword, and with famine, and with pestilence, until I have consumed them by his hand" (Jeremiah 27:5-8). Therefore "bring your necks under the yoke of the king of Babylon, and serve him and his people, and live" (verse 12). We see how great an obedience the Lord was pleased to demand for this dreadful and ferocious tyrant, for no other reason than that he held the kingdom. In other words, the divine decree had placed him on the throne of the kingdom and admitted him to royal majesty, which could not lawfully be violated. If we constantly keep before our eyes and minds the fact that even the most wicked kings are appointed by the same decree which establishes all royal authority, we will never entertain the seditious thought that a king is to be treated according to his deserts, and that we are not bound to act the part of good subjects toward him who does not in his turn act the part of a king toward us.

28¶

It is vain to object that this command was given specially to the Israelites. For we must attend to the ground on which the Lord bases it: "I have given the kingdom to Nebuchadnezzar; therefore serve him and live." Let us not doubt that on whomever the kingdom has been conferred, him we are bound to serve. Whenever God raises anyone to royal honor, he declares it to be his pleasure that he should reign. To this effect we have general declarations in Scripture. Solomon says, "For the transgression of a land many are the princes thereof" (Proverbs 28:2). Job says, "He looseth the bond of kings, and girdeth their loins with a girdle" (Job 12:18). This being admitted, nothing remains for us but to serve and live. There is in Jeremiah another command in which the Lord orders his people thus: "Seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the Lord for it: for in the peace thereof shall ye have peace" (Jeremiah 29:7). Here the Israelites, plundered of all their property, torn from their homes, driven into exile, and thrown into miserable bondage, are ordered to pray for the prosperity of the conqueror; not as we are elsewhere ordered to pray for our persecutors, but that his kingdom may be preserved in safety and tranquility, so that they too may live prosperously under him. Thus David, when he was already king elect by the appointment of God and anointed with his holy oil, though he was attacked by Saul without cause and unjustly, holds the life of one who was seeking his life to be sacred, because the Lord had invested him with royal honor. "The Lord forbid that I should do this thing unto my master, the Lord's anointed, to stretch forth mine hand against him, seeing he is the anointed of the Lord." "Mine eyes spare you; and I said, I will not put forth mine hand against my lord; for he is the Lord's anointed" (1 Samuel 24:6, 11). Again, "Who can stretch forth his hand against the Lord's anointed, and be guiltless?" "As the Lord liveth, the Lord shall smite him, or his day shall come to die, or he shall descend into battle, and perish. The Lord forbid that I should stretch forth mine hand against the Lord's anointed" (1 Samuel 24:9-11).

29¶

This feeling of reverence, and even of godliness, we owe in the fullest measure to all our rulers, whatever their characters may be. I repeat this the more often so that we may learn not to look at the individuals themselves, but to hold it enough that by the will of the Lord they bear an office on which he has impressed and engraved an inviolable majesty. But rulers, you will say, owe mutual duties to those under them. This I have already admitted. But if from this you conclude that obedience is to be rendered to none but just governors, you reason absurdly. Husbands are bound by mutual duties to their wives, and parents to their children. Should husbands and parents neglect their duty; should parents be harsh and severe toward the children whom they are commanded not to provoke to anger, and by their severity harass them beyond measure; should husbands treat with the greatest contempt the wives whom they are commanded to love and to spare as the weaker vessels; would children be any less bound in duty to their parents, and wives to their husbands? They are made subject even to the perverse and undutiful. Indeed, since the duty of all is not to look behind them, that is, not to inquire into the duties of one another, but each to attend to his own duty, this ought especially to be shown in the case of those who are placed under the power of others. Therefore, if we are cruelly tormented by a savage prince, if we are greedily plundered by an avaricious or luxurious one, if we are neglected by a sluggish one, if, in short, we are persecuted for righteousness' sake by an ungodly and sacrilegious one, let us first call to mind our own faults, which the Lord is doubtless chastising by such scourges. In this way humility will curb our impatience. And let us reflect that it does not belong to us to cure these evils; that all that remains for us is to implore the help of the Lord, in whose hands are the hearts of kings and the inclinations of kingdoms. Daniel 9:7; Proverbs 21:1; Psalm 82:1; 2:10; Isaiah 10:1.

30¶

Herein the goodness, power, and providence of God are wonderfully displayed. At one time he raises up open avengers from among his own servants, and gives them his command to punish accursed tyranny and to deliver his people from calamity when they are unjustly oppressed; at another time he employs for this purpose the fury of men who have other thoughts and other aims. Thus he rescued his people Israel from the tyranny of Pharaoh by Moses; from the violence of Chusa, king of Syria, by Othniel; and from other bondage by other kings or judges. Thus he tamed the pride of Tyre by the Egyptians; the insolence of the Egyptians by the Assyrians; the ferocity of the Assyrians by the Chaldeans; the confidence of Babylon by the Medes and Persians, Cyrus having first subdued the Medes; while the ingratitude of the kings of Judah and Israel, and their wicked obstinacy after all his kindness, he subdued and punished, at one time by the Assyrians, at another by the Babylonians. All these things, however, were not done in the same way. The former class of deliverers, being brought forward by the lawful call of God to perform such deeds, did not at all violate, when they took up arms against kings, that majesty with which kings are invested by divine appointment; but, armed from heaven, they curbed a lesser power by a greater, just as kings may lawfully punish their own officers. The latter class, though they were directed by the hand of God as seemed good to him, and did his work without knowing it, had nothing but evil in their thoughts.

31¶

But whatever may be thought of the acts of the men themselves, The French adds, "Car les uns les faisoyent estans asseurez qu'ils faisoyent bien, et les autres par autre zele (comme nous avons dit)." That is: For the former acted in the full conviction that they were doing right, and the latter from a different motive, as we have said.

32¶

But in that obedience which we hold to be due to the commands of rulers, we must always make this exception, and indeed must be particularly careful, that it is not incompatible with obedience to Him to whose will the wishes of all kings should be subject, to whose decrees their commands must yield, and to whose majesty their scepters must bow. And indeed, how absurd it would be, in pleasing men, to incur the displeasure of Him for whose sake you obey men! The Lord, therefore, is King of kings. When he opens his sacred mouth, he alone is to be heard, instead of all and above all. We are subject to the men who rule over us, but subject only in the Lord. If they command anything against Him, let us not pay the least regard to it, nor be moved by all the dignity which they possess as magistrates, a dignity to which no injury is done when it is made subordinate to the special and truly supreme power of God. On this ground Daniel denies that he had sinned in any respect against the king when he refused to obey his ungodly decree (Daniel 6:22), because the king had exceeded his limits, and had not only wronged men but, by raising his horn against God, had in effect abrogated his own power. On the other hand, the Israelites are condemned for having too readily obeyed the ungodly edict of the king. For when Jeroboam made the golden calf, they forsook the temple of God and, in submission to him, defected to new superstitions (1 Kings 12:28). With the same readiness their descendants had bowed before the decrees of their kings. For this they are severely rebuked by the Prophet (Hosea 5:11). So far is the praise of modesty from being due to that pretense with which flattering courtiers cloak themselves and deceive the simple, when they deny that it is lawful to refuse anything imposed by their kings; as if the Lord had resigned his own rights to mortals by appointing them to rule over their fellows, or as if earthly power were diminished when it is subjected to its author, before whom even the principalities of heaven tremble as suppliants. I know the imminent peril to which subjects expose themselves by this firmness, since kings are most indignant when they are defied. As Solomon says, "The wrath of a king is as messengers of death" (Proverbs 16:14). But since Peter, one of heaven's heralds, has published the edict, "We ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29), let us console ourselves with the thought that we are rendering the obedience which the Lord requires when we endure anything rather than turn aside from godliness. And so that our courage may not fail, Paul spurs us on by this further consideration (1 Corinthians 7:23): that we were redeemed by Christ at the great price which our redemption cost him, in order that we might not yield a slavish obedience to the depraved wishes of men, much less do homage to their ungodliness.

END OF THE INSTITUTES.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin closes the Institutes by defending civil government as God's ordinance — magistrates are his ministers for our good, the order that restrains evil and preserves peace is no necessary evil but a gift, and the Christian owes honor, obedience, and prayer to those in authority even as his ultimate allegiance rests with God alone. A word for 2026, when many despise the office while idolizing the officeholder, or else withdraw entirely in disgust: receive earthly authority as a divine appointment, render what is Caesar's, and labor as a sober citizen for the good of your city. Yet remember the limit Calvin set — we must obey God rather than men when rulers command what he forbids. Teach your children both halves: honor toward authority and a conscience captive to the Word above it. Pray for those who govern, vote and serve as faithful stewards, and hold your hope steady, for the kingdom that cannot be shaken is not built by any election but kept by the Lord who reigns over all.
