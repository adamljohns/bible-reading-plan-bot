Of the Popish Mass — How It Not Only Profanes, But Annihilates the Lord's Supper

The principal heads of this chapter are,—I. The abomination of the Mass, sec. 1. Its manifold impiety included under five heads, sec. 2-7. Its origin described. sec. 8, 9. II. Of the name of sacrifice which the ancients gave to the holy Supper, sec. 10-12. An apposite discussion on sacrifice, refuting the arguments of the Papists for the sacrifice of the Mass, sec. 13-18. III. A summary of the doctrine of the Christian Church respecting sacraments, paving the way for the subsequent discussion of the five sacraments, falsely so called, sec. 19, 20. 1. The chief of all the abominations set up in opposition to the Lord’s Supper is the Papal Mass. A description of it. 2. Its impiety is five-fold. 1. Its intolerable blasphemy in substituting priests to him the only Priest. Objections of the Papists answered. 3. Impiety of the Mass continued. 2. It overthrows the cross of Christ by setting up an altar. Objections answered. 4. Other objections answered. 5. Impiety of the Mass continued. 3. It banishes the remembrance of Christ’s death. It crucifies Christ afresh. Objections answered. 6. Impiety of the Mass continued. 4. It robs us of the benefit of Christ’s death. 7. Impiety of the Mass continued. 5. It abolishes the Lord’s Supper. In the Supper the Father offers Christ to us; in the Mass, priestlings offer Christ to the Father. The Supper is a sacrament common to all Christians; the Mass confined to one priest. 8. The origin of the Mass. Private masses an impious profanation of the Supper. 9. This abomination unknown to the purer Church. It has no foundation in the word of God. 10. Second part of the chapter. Some of the ancients call the Supper a sacrifice, but not propitiatory, as the Papists do the Mass. This proved by passages from Augustine. 11. Some of the ancients seem to have declined too much to the shadows of the law. 12. Great distinction to be made between the Mosaic sacrifices and the Lord’s Supper, which is called a eucharistic sacrifice. Same rule in this discussion. 13. The terms sacrifice and priest . Different kinds of sacrifices. 1. Propitiatory. 2. Eucharistic. None propitiatory but the death of Christ. 14. The Lord’s Supper not properly called a propitiatory sacrifice, still less can the Popish Mass be so called. Those who mutter over the mass cannot be called priests. 15. Their vanity proved even by Plato. 16. To the eucharistic class of sacrifice belong all offices of piety and charity. This species of sacrifice has no connection with the appeasing of God. 17. Prayer, thanksgiving, and other exercises of piety, called sacrifices. In this sense the Lord’s Supper called the eucharist. In the same sense all believers are priests. 18. Conclusion. Names given to the Mass. 19. Last part of the chapter, recapitulating the views which ought to be held concerning baptism and the Lord’s Supper. Why the Lord’s Supper is, and Baptism is not, repeated. 20. Christians should be contented with these two sacraments. They are abolished by the sacraments decreed by men.

1¶

By these and similar inventions Satan has tried to corrupt the sacred Supper of Christ and to wrap it as in thick darkness, so that its purity might not be preserved in the Church. But the height of this horrible abomination came when he raised up a sign by which the Supper was not only obscured and perverted, but altogether blotted out and abolished, so that it vanished and disappeared from the memory of men — namely, when, by a most deadly error, he blinded almost the whole world into believing that the Mass was a sacrifice and oblation for obtaining the remission of sins. I say nothing about the way in which the sounder Schoolmen at first received this dogma. The French adds, "qui ont parlé un petit plus passablement que leur successeurs qui sont venus depuis;" — who have spoken somewhat more tolerably than their successors who have come since.

2¶

Let us show, therefore, as was proposed in the first place, that in the mass intolerable blasphemy and insult are offered to Christ. For he was not appointed Priest and Pontiff by the Father (Hebrews 5:5-10; 7:17; 21; 9:11; 10:21; Psalm 110:4; Genesis 14:18).

3¶

Another wickedness chargeable to the mass is that it sinks and buries the cross and passion of Christ. This much, indeed, is most certain: the cross of Christ is overthrown the moment an altar is set up. For if on the cross he offered himself in sacrifice so that he might sanctify us forever and purchase eternal redemption for us (Hebrews 9:11, 12, 26; 10:10, 14, 16).

4¶

But it is worthwhile to hear on what other foundation they build up their sacrifice of the mass. For this purpose they drag in the prophecy of Malachi, in which the Lord promises that "in every place incense shall be offered unto my name, and a pure offering" (Malachi 1:11). As if it were new or unusual for the prophets, when they speak of the calling of the Gentiles, to describe the spiritual worship of God to which they call them by the outward rites of the law, so as to convey more familiarly to the men of their own age that they were to be called into the true fellowship of religion; just as in general they are accustomed to describe the truth displayed by the gospel through the types of their own age. Thus they use going up to Jerusalem for conversion to the Lord, the bringing of all kinds of gifts for the worship of God, and dreams and visions for the fuller knowledge with which believers were to be endowed in the kingdom of Christ. The passage they quote from Malachi resembles one in Isaiah, in which the prophet speaks of three altars to be set up in Assyria, Egypt, and Judea. First, I ask whether or not they grant that this prophecy is fulfilled in the kingdom of Christ. Secondly, where are those altars, or when were they ever set up? Thirdly, do they suppose that a single temple is appointed for a single kingdom, as that of Jerusalem was? If they weigh these things, they will confess, I think, that the prophet, under types suited to his age, prophesied the spread of the spiritual worship of God over the whole world. This is the answer we give them; but since obvious examples occur everywhere in Scripture, I am not anxious to list more of them. Yet they are miserably deluded in this also, that they acknowledge no sacrifice but that of the mass, whereas in truth believers now sacrifice to God and offer him a pure offering, of which we shall speak shortly.

5¶

I now come to the third part of the mass, in regard to which we are to explain how it blots out the true and only death of Christ and drives it from the memory of men. For as among men the confirmation of a testament depends on the death of the testator, so also our Lord has confirmed by his death the testament by which he has bequeathed to us the remission of sins and eternal righteousness. Those who dare to make any change or innovation in this testament deny his death and treat it as of no account. Now, what is the mass but a new and altogether different testament? What? Does not each mass promise a new forgiveness of sins, a new purchase of righteousness, so that there are now as many testaments as there are masses? Therefore let Christ come again and by another death make this new testament; or rather, by countless deaths ratify the countless testaments of the mass. Did I not say truly, then, at the outset, that the only true death of Christ is blotted out by the mass? For what is the direct aim of the mass but simply to put Christ to death again, if that were possible? For, as the apostle says, "Where a testament is, there must also of necessity be the death of the testator" (Hebrews 9:16). The novelty of the mass presents itself on its face as a testament of Christ, and therefore demands his death. Besides, the victim that is offered must be slain and sacrificed. If Christ is sacrificed at each mass, he must be cruelly slain every moment in a thousand places. This is not my argument but the apostle's: "Nor yet that he should offer himself often"; "for then must he often have suffered since the foundation of the world" (Hebrews 9:25, 26). I admit that they have an answer ready, by which they even charge us with slander; for they say that we accuse them of what they never thought and could not even think. We know that the life and death of Christ are not at all in their hands. Whether they mean to slay him does not concern us; our purpose is only to show the absurdity that follows from their ungodly and accursed dogma. This I demonstrate from the mouth of the apostle. Though they insist a hundred times that this sacrifice is bloodless (ἀναίμακτον), I will reply that it does not depend on the will of man to change the nature of sacrifice, for in that way the sacred and inviolable institution of God would fall. Hence it follows that the principle of the apostle stands firm: "without shedding of blood is no remission" (Hebrews 9:22).

6¶

The fourth property of the mass that we are to consider is that it robs us of the benefit that came to us from the death of Christ, by preventing us from recognizing it and thinking on it. For who can think that he has been redeemed by the death of Christ when he sees a new redemption in the mass? Who can feel confident that his sins have been forgiven when he sees a new forgiveness? It will not do to say that the only reason we obtain forgiveness of sins in the mass is that it has already been purchased by the death of Christ. For this is simply the same as saying that we are redeemed by Christ on condition that we redeem ourselves. For the doctrine spread abroad by the ministers of Satan, which at the present day they defend by clamor, fire, and sword, is that when we offer Christ to the Father in the mass, we obtain remission of sins by this work of oblation and become partakers of the sufferings of Christ. What is now left for the sufferings of Christ, but to be an example of redemption, from which we may learn to be our own redeemers? Christ himself, when he seals our assurance of pardon in the Supper, does not bid his disciples stop short at that act, but sends them on to the sacrifice of his death; indicating that the Supper is the memento, or, as it is commonly called, the memorial, from which they may learn that the atoning victim by which God was to be appeased was to be offered only once. For it is not enough to hold that Christ is the only victim, without adding that his is the only immolation, so that our faith may be fixed to his cross.

7¶

I come now to the crowning point — namely, that the sacred Supper, on which the Lord left the memorial of his passion formed and engraved, was taken away, hidden, and destroyed when the mass was set up. While the Supper itself is a gift of God, to be received with thanksgiving, the sacrifice of the mass pretends to pay a price to God, to be received by him as satisfaction. As far as giving differs from receiving, so far does sacrifice differ from the sacrament of the Supper. And here the wretched ingratitude of man appears: when the generosity of the divine goodness ought to have been acknowledged and thanks returned, he makes God his debtor. The sacrament promised that by the death of Christ we were not only restored to life once, but are continually made alive, because all the parts of our salvation were then completed. The sacrifice of the mass speaks a very different language — namely, that Christ must be sacrificed daily in order that he may do us some good. The Supper was to be administered at the public assembly of the Church, to remind us of the communion by which we are all united in Christ Jesus. This communion the sacrifice of the mass dissolves and tears apart. For after the heresy prevailed that there had to be priests to sacrifice for the people, as if the Supper had been handed over to them, it ceased to be shared with the assembly of the faithful according to the command of the Lord. Entrance was given to private masses, which resemble a kind of excommunication more than the communion ordained by the Lord, since the petty priest, about to devour his victim apart, separates himself from the whole body of the faithful. So that there may be no mistake, I call it a private mass whenever there is no partaking of the Lord's Supper among believers, even though a great multitude of people may be present at the same time.

8¶

The origin of the name Mass I have never been able to determine with certainty. It seems probable that it was derived from the offerings that were collected. That is why the ancients usually speak of it in the plural. But without raising any dispute about the name, I hold that private masses are diametrically opposed to the institution of Christ, and are therefore an ungodly profanation of the sacred Supper. For what did the Lord command? Was it not to take and divide among ourselves? What does Paul teach about keeping this command? Is it not that the breaking of bread is the communion of body and blood (1 Corinthians 10:16)? Therefore, when one person takes without distributing, where is the resemblance? But, they say, that one man acts in the name of the whole Church. By what command? Is it not openly mocking God when one man privately seizes for himself what ought to have been distributed among many? But since the words both of our Savior and of Paul are clear enough, we must briefly conclude that wherever there is no breaking of bread for the communion of the faithful, there is no Supper of the Lord, but a false and absurd imitation of the Supper. But false imitation is corruption. Moreover, the corruption of this high ordinance is not without ungodliness. In private masses, therefore, there is an ungodly abuse. And as in religion one fault continually breeds another, once that custom of offering without communion had crept in, they gradually began to hold countless masses in all the separate corners of the churches, and to draw the people here and there, when they ought to have formed one assembly and so acknowledged the mystery of their unity. Let them go now and deny their idolatry, when they display the bread in their masses so that it may be adored as Christ. In vain do they talk of those promises of the presence of Christ, which, however they may be understood, were certainly not given so that impure and profane men might form the body of Christ as often as they please and for whatever abuse they please; but so that believers, while they follow the command of Christ with religious observance in celebrating the Supper, might enjoy the true participation of it.

9¶

We may add that this perverse practice was unknown to the purer Church. For however the more shameless among our opponents may try to gloss over the matter, it is absolutely certain that all antiquity is against them, as has been shown above in other instances, and as may be known more surely by diligent reading of the Fathers. The French of this sentence is, "Car combien que ceux qui sont les plus effrontées entre les Papistes fassent un bouclier des anciens docteurs, abusant faussement de leurs tesmoignages, toutesfois c'est une chose claire comme le soleil en plein midi, que ce qu'ils font est tout contraire a l'usage ancien: et que c'est un abus qui est venu en avant du temps que tout etoit depravé et corrompu en l'Eglise." — For although the most brazen among the Papists make a shield of the ancient doctors, falsely abusing their testimony, it is as clear as the sun at noonday that what they do is quite contrary to ancient practice, and that it is an abuse which immediately preceded the time when everything was depraved and corrupted in the Church.

10¶

Should anyone here bring forward brief sayings of the ancients, and contend on their authority that the sacrifice performed in the Supper is to be understood differently from the way we have explained it, let this be our brief reply: if the question concerns approval of the fiction of sacrifice as the Papists imagine it in the mass, there is nothing in the Fathers to support the sacrilege. They do indeed use the term sacrifice, but at the same time they explain that they mean nothing more than the commemoration of that one true sacrifice which Christ, our only sacrifice (as they themselves everywhere proclaim), offered on the cross. "The Hebrews," says Augustine (Cont. Faust. Lib. 20 c. 18), "in the victims of beasts which they offered to God, celebrated the prediction of the future victim which Christ offered: Christians now celebrate the commemoration of a finished sacrifice by the sacred oblation and participation of the body of Christ." Here he certainly teaches the same doctrine that is set out at greater length in the Treatise on Faith addressed to Peter the deacon, whoever its author may have been. The words are: "Hold most firmly, and have no doubt at all, that the Only-Begotten became incarnate for us, that he offered himself for us, an offering and sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling savor; to whom, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, in the time of the Old Testament, animals were sacrificed, and to whom now, with the Father and the Holy Spirit (with whom there is one Godhead), the holy Church throughout the whole world does not cease to offer the sacrifice of bread and wine. For in those carnal victims there was a foreshadowing of the flesh of Christ, which he himself was to offer for our sins, and of the blood which he was to shed for the forgiveness of sins. But in that sacrifice there is thanksgiving and commemoration of the flesh of Christ which he offered for us, and of the blood which he shed for us." Hence Augustine himself, in several passages (Ep. 120, ad Honorat. Cont. Advers. Legis.), explains that it is nothing else than a sacrifice of praise. In short, you will find in his writings, passim, that the only reason the Lord's Supper is called a sacrifice is that it is a commemoration, an image, a testimony of that singular, true, and only sacrifice by which Christ atoned for our guilt. For there is a memorable passage (De Trinitate, Lib. 4 c. 24) where, after discussing the only sacrifice, he concludes this way: "Since in a sacrifice four things are considered — namely, to whom it is offered, by whom, what, and for whom — the same one true Mediator, reconciling us to God by the sacrifice of peace, remains one with him to whom he offered, made himself one with those for whom he offered, is himself the one who offered, and the one thing which he offered." Chrysostom speaks to the same effect. They claim the honor of the priesthood so strongly for Christ alone that Augustine declares it would amount to Antichrist for anyone to make a bishop an intercessor between God and man (August. Cont. Parmen. Lib. 2 c. 8). This last sentence forms, in the French, the first of section 11.

11¶

And yet we do not deny that in the Supper the sacrifice of Christ is so vividly displayed as almost to set the spectacle of the cross before our eyes, just as the apostle says to the Galatians that Jesus Christ had been plainly set forth before their eyes when the preaching of the cross was delivered to them (Galatians 3:1). But because I see that those ancient writers twisted this commemoration to a purpose other than the one that agreed with the divine institution (the Supper somehow seemed to them to present the appearance of a repeated, or at least renewed, immolation), nothing can be safer for the godly than to rest content with the pure and simple ordinance of God, whose Supper it is said to be precisely because his authority alone ought to appear in it. Since they kept a godly and orthodox view of the whole ordinance — and I cannot find that they wished to detract in the least from the one sacrifice of the Lord — I cannot charge them with any ungodliness; and yet I think they cannot be excused from having erred somewhat in the manner of the action. They imitated the Jewish manner of sacrificing more closely than either Christ had ordained or the nature of the gospel allowed. The only thing, therefore, for which they may justly be censured is that misguided analogy, by which, not content with the simple and genuine institution of Christ, they leaned too far toward the shadows of the law.

12¶

Anyone who considers the matter carefully will see that the word of the Lord makes this distinction between the Mosaic sacrifices and our eucharist: while the former represented to the Jewish people the same efficacy of the death of Christ that is now displayed to us in the Supper, the form of representation was different. There the Levitical priests were commanded to foreshadow the sacrifice that Christ was to accomplish; a victim was set in place to stand as a substitute for Christ himself; an altar was erected on which it was to be sacrificed; in short, the whole was so conducted as to bring before the eye an image of the sacrifice that was to be offered to God in expiation. But now that the sacrifice has been accomplished, the Lord has prescribed a different method to us — namely, to pass on to his believing people the benefit of the sacrifice offered to him by his Son. The Lord, therefore, has given us a table at which we may feast, not an altar on which a victim may be offered; he has not consecrated priests to sacrifice, but ministers to distribute a sacred feast. The more sublime and holy this mystery is, the more religiously and reverently it ought to be treated. Nothing, therefore, is safer than to banish all the boldness of human sense and to hold solely to what Scripture teaches. And certainly, if we reflect that it is the Supper of the Lord and not of men, why do we allow ourselves to be turned aside a nail's breadth from Scripture by any human authority or long-standing custom? French, "n'ancun authorite humaine, ne longeur de temps, ne toutes autres apparences;" — no human authority, no length of time, nor any other appearances.

13¶

So that no quarrelsome person may raise a dispute with us over the terms sacrifice and priest, I will briefly explain what we mean throughout this discussion by sacrifice, and what by priest. Some, on what rational ground I do not see, extend the term sacrifice to all sacred ceremonies and religious acts. We know that by the consistent usage of Scripture the name of sacrifice is given to what the Greeks call at one time θυσια, at another προσφοπὰ, at another τελετνὴ. In its general sense this includes everything whatever that is offered to God. Therefore we ought to make a distinction, but in such a way that the distinction draws its analogy from the sacrifices of the Mosaic Law, under whose shadows the Lord was pleased to represent to his people the whole reality of sacrifices. Though these were various in form, they may all be referred to two classes. For either an oblation for sin was made by a certain kind of satisfaction, by which the penalty was redeemed before God; or it was a symbol and attestation of religion and divine worship — at one time by way of supplication, to ask for the favor of God; at another by way of thanksgiving, to testify gratitude to God for benefits received; at another as a simple exercise of godliness, to renew the sanction of the covenant. To this latter branch belonged burnt offerings and libations, oblations, firstfruits, and peace offerings. Let us therefore also divide sacrifices into two classes. The one class, for the sake of explanation, let us call λατπευτικὸν and σεβαστιχὸν, as consisting of the veneration and worship that believers both owe and render to God; or, if you prefer, let us call it ευχαριστικὸν, since it is presented to God only by those who, enriched with his boundless benefits, offer themselves and all their actions to him in return. The other class let us call propitiatory or expiatory. A sacrifice of expiation is one whose object is to appease the wrath of God, to satisfy his justice, and so to wipe and wash away sins, so that the sinner, cleansed and restored to purity, may return to favor with God. Hence the name that was given in the Law to the victims offered in expiation of sin (Exodus 29:36); not that they were adequate to regain the favor of God and wipe away guilt, but because they foreshadowed the true sacrifice of this kind, which was at length performed in reality by Christ alone: by him alone, because no other could; and once, because the efficacy and power of the one sacrifice performed by Christ is eternal, as he declared with his own voice when he said, "It is finished." That is, everything necessary to regain the favor of the Father and to procure forgiveness of sins, righteousness, and salvation was performed and completed by his one oblation, so that nothing was lacking. No place was left for another sacrifice.

14¶

Therefore I conclude that it is an abominable insult and an intolerable blasphemy, both against Christ and against the sacrifice which he performed for us by his death on the cross, for anyone to think of repeating the oblation, of purchasing the forgiveness of sins, of propitiating God, and of obtaining justification. But what else is done in the mass than to make us partakers of the sufferings of Christ by means of a new oblation? And so that there might be no limit to their extravagance, they have thought it too little to say that it becomes properly a common sacrifice for the whole Church, without adding that it is in their power to apply it specially to this one or that, as they choose; or rather, to anyone who is willing to buy their merchandise from them for a price paid. Moreover, since they could not come up to the price of Judas, still, so that they might in some way recall their author, they make the resemblance consist in the number. He sold for thirty pieces of silver; they, according to the French method of reckoning, sell for thirty pieces of brass. He did it once; they, as often as a buyer turns up. We deny that they are priests in this sense — namely, that by such oblations they intercede with God for the people, and that by propitiating God they make expiation for sins. Christ is the only Pontiff and Priest of the New Testament: to him all priestly offices were transferred, and in him they were closed and ended. Even if Scripture had made no mention of the eternal priesthood of Christ, yet since God, after abolishing those ancient sacrifices, appointed no new priest, the argument of the apostle remains unanswerable: "No man taketh this honor unto himself, but he that is called of God, as was Aaron" (Hebrews 5:4). How, then, can those sacrilegious men, who by their own account are murderers of Christ, dare to call themselves the priests of the living God?

15¶

There is a most elegant passage in the second book of Plato's Republic. Speaking of ancient expiations, he mocks the foolish confidence of wicked and unjust men, who thought that by these, as by a kind of veil, they hid their crimes from the gods, and who, as if they had made a bargain with the gods, indulged themselves more securely; and in doing so he seems to describe precisely the use of the expiation of the mass as it exists in the world at the present day. All know that it is unlawful to defraud and cheat another. To wrong widows, to plunder orphans, to harass the poor, to seize the goods of others by wicked arts, to get possession of any man's inheritance by fraud and perjury, to oppress by violence and tyrannical terror — all admit these to be ungodly. How, then, do so many dare to do all these things, as if assured that they will go unpunished? Undoubtedly, if we consider the matter properly, we will find that the only thing that gives them such courage is that they trust that by the sacrifice of the mass, as a price paid, they will satisfy God, or at least will easily find a way of striking a deal with him. Plato next goes on to mock the gross stupidity of those who think by such expiations to buy off the punishments they must otherwise suffer after death. And what is the meaning of anniversaries and the greater part of masses at the present day, but simply that those who throughout life have been the cruelest tyrants, or the most greedy plunderers, or experts in every kind of wickedness, may escape the fire of purgatory, as if redeemed at this price?

16¶

Under the other kind of sacrifice, which we have called eucharistic, are included all the duties of love, by which, while we embrace our brothers, we honor the Lord himself in his members; in short, all our prayers, praises, thanksgivings, and every act of worship that we perform to God. All these depend on the greater sacrifice by which we dedicate ourselves, soul and body, to be a holy temple to the Lord. For it is not enough that our outward acts be shaped to obedience; we must dedicate and consecrate, first, ourselves, and secondly, all that we have, so that all that is in us may serve his glory and be stirred up to magnify it. This kind of sacrifice has nothing to do with appeasing God, with obtaining remission of sins, or with procuring justification, but is wholly occupied in magnifying and exalting God; for it cannot be pleasing and acceptable to God except from the hand of those who, having received forgiveness of sins, have already been reconciled and freed from guilt. This is so necessary to the Church that it cannot be dispensed with. Therefore it will endure forever, as long as the people of God shall endure, as we have already seen above from the prophet. For in this sense we may understand the prophecy, "From the rising of the sun, even unto the going down of the same, my name shall be great among the Gentiles; and in every place incense shall be offered unto my name, and a pure offering: for my name shall be great among the heathen, said the Lord of hosts" (Malachi 1:11). So far are we from doing away with this sacrifice. Thus Paul urges us by the mercies of God to present our bodies "a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God," our "reasonable service" (Romans 12:1). Here he speaks very pointedly when he adds that this service is reasonable, for he refers to the spiritual manner of worshiping God, and silently sets it against the carnal sacrifices of the Mosaic Law. Thus to do good and to share are called sacrifices with which God is well pleased (Hebrews 13:16). Thus the kindness of the Philippians in relieving Paul's need is called "an odor of a sweet smell, a sacrifice acceptable, well pleasing to God" (Philippians 4:18); and thus all the good works of believers are called spiritual sacrifices.

17¶

But why do I go on listing them? This form of expression occurs constantly in Scripture. Indeed, even while the people of God were kept under the outward tutelage of the law, the prophets plainly declared that under these carnal sacrifices there was a reality common both to the Jewish people and to the Christian Church. For this reason David prayed, "Let my prayer ascend forth before you as incense" (Psalm 141:2). And Hosea gives the name of "calves of the lips" (Hosea 14:3) to thanksgivings, which David elsewhere calls "sacrifices of praise"; and the apostle, following him, speaks of offering "the sacrifice of praise," which he explains to mean "the fruit of our lips, giving thanks to his name" (Hebrews 13:15). This kind of sacrifice is indispensable in the Lord's Supper, in which, while we show forth his death and give him thanks, we offer nothing but the sacrifice of praise. From this office of sacrificing, all Christians are called "a royal priesthood," because through Christ we offer that sacrifice of praise of which the apostle speaks, the fruit of our lips, giving thanks to his name (1 Peter 2:9; Hebrews 13:15). We do not appear with our gifts in the presence of God without an intercessor. Christ is our Mediator, through whose intervention we offer ourselves and all that we have to the Father; he is our High Priest, who, having entered into the heavenly sanctuary, opens up access for us; he is the altar on which we lay our gifts, so that whatever we attempt, we may attempt in him. It is he, I say, who "hath made us kings and priests unto God and his Father" (Revelation 1:6).

18¶

What remains but for the blind to see, the deaf to hear, and even children to perceive this abomination of the mass, which, held out in a golden cup, The French explains, "c'est à dire, sous le nom de la parole de Dieu;" — that is to say, under the name of the word of God.

19¶

My readers have here a concise view of all that I have thought it important to know concerning these two sacraments, which have been delivered to the Christian Church to be used from the beginning of the new dispensation to the end of the world: Baptism being a kind of entrance into the Church, an initiation into the faith; and the Lord's Supper the continual nourishment by which Christ spiritually feeds his family of believers. Therefore, as there is but one God, one faith, one Christ, one Church, which is his body, so Baptism is one and is not repeated. But the Supper is administered again and again, to signify that those who have once been drawn into the Church are continually fed by Christ. Besides these two, no other has been instituted by God, and no other ought to be recognized by the assembly of the faithful. That sacraments are not to be instituted and set up by the will of men is easily understood by anyone who remembers what has been set out above with sufficient plainness — namely, that the sacraments have been appointed by God to instruct us in his promise and to testify his goodwill toward us — and who also considers that the Lord has no counselor (Isaiah 40:13; Romans 11:34). Who can give us any certainty as to his will, or assure us how he is disposed toward us, what he is disposed to give, and what to deny? From this it follows that no one can put forward a sign that is to be a testimony of his will and of some promise. He alone can give the sign and bear witness to himself. I will put it more briefly, perhaps in plainer but also in clearer terms: there can never be a sacrament without a promise of salvation. All men gathered into one cannot of themselves give us any promise of salvation, and therefore they cannot of themselves issue and set up a sacrament.

20¶

With these two, therefore, let the Christian Church be content, and not only not admit or acknowledge any third at present, but not even desire or expect one until the end of the world. For though the Jews were given, besides his ordinary sacraments, others differing somewhat according to the nature of the times (such as the manna, the water gushing from the rock, the bronze serpent, and the like) (Exodus 16:13-15; 17:6; Numbers 20:8; 21:9; 1 Corinthians 10:4; John 3:14).

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin's verdict on the Mass is severe because the stakes are eternal — to make of the Supper a sacrifice offered afresh by priestly hands is not to honor Christ's death but to deny its sufficiency, for the one offering on the cross was final, and to repeat it is to annihilate the very gospel the Supper was given to proclaim. A word for 2026: the perfect, finished work of Christ is the believer's whole rest, and any teaching that adds our works, our priests, or our repetitions to that cross steals the comfort God meant us to have. Guard this in your home with quiet vigilance — teach your children that nothing remains to be paid, that Christ said 'It is finished' and meant it. The temptation to earn what has already been purchased never dies; it merely changes its dress. Stand on the once-for-all sacrifice, and let no subtler gospel, however reverent its trappings, move you from the finished work that alone saves.
