In the Creation of the World, and All Things in It, the True God Distinguished by Certain Marks From Fictitious Gods

In this chapter commences the second part of Book First—viz. the knowledge of man. Certain things premised. I. The creation of the world generally (s. 1 and 2). II. The subject of angels considered (s. 3-13). III. Of bad angels or devils (s. 13-20); and, IV. The practical use to be made of the history of the creation (s. 20-22).

1¶

Although Isaiah rightly charges the worshipers of false gods with stupidity for not learning from the foundations of the earth and the circle of the heavens who the true God is (Isaiah 40:21), yet our intellect is so sluggish and groveling that it was necessary for him to be more clearly portrayed, so that the faithful might not fall away to the fictions of the Gentiles. The idea that God is the soul of the world, though the most tolerable that the philosophers have suggested, is absurd; and therefore it was important to furnish us with a more intimate knowledge, so that we might not wander to and fro in uncertainty. Hence God was pleased that a history of the creation should exist — a history on which the faith of the Church might lean without seeking any other God than him whom Moses sets forth as the Creator and Architect of the world. First, in that history the span of time is marked, so as to enable the faithful to ascend by an unbroken succession of years to the first origin of their race and of all things. This knowledge is of the highest use, not only as an antidote to the monstrous fables which prevailed in ancient times both in Egypt and in the other regions of the world, but also as a means of displaying more clearly the eternity of God in contrast with the birth of creation, and so of inspiring us with higher admiration. We must not be moved by the profane jeer that it is strange it did not occur to the Deity sooner to create the heavens and the earth, instead of idly allowing an infinite period to pass away, during which thousands of generations might have existed, while the present world is drawing to a close before it has completed its six thousandth year. Why God delayed so long it is neither fitting nor lawful to inquire. Should the human mind presume to do so, it could only fail in the attempt; nor would it be useful for us to know what God, as a test of the modesty of our faith, has purposely been pleased to conceal. It was a shrewd saying of a good old man who, when someone pertly asked in mockery what God was doing before the world was created, answered that he was making a hell for the inquisitive (Augustine, Confessions, book 11, chapter 12). This rebuke, no less weighty than severe, should restrain the itching wantonness which drives many to indulge in vicious and harmful speculation.

In short, let us remember that the invisible God, whose wisdom, power, and justice are incomprehensible, is set before us in the history of Moses as in a mirror, in which his living image is reflected. For just as an eye, whether dimmed by age or weakened by some other cause, sees nothing distinctly without the aid of glasses, so (such is our feebleness) if Scripture does not guide us in our inquiries after God, we immediately become vain in our imaginations. Those who now indulge their insolence and refuse to take warning will learn, when it is too late, how much better it would have been to regard the secret counsels of God with reverence than to belch forth blasphemies which pollute the face of heaven. Augustine rightly complains that God is insulted whenever any higher reason than his will is demanded (Lib. de Gent.). He also wisely reminds us in another place that it is just as improper to raise questions about infinite periods of time as about infinite space (City of God). However wide the circuit of the heavens may be, it is of some definite extent. But should anyone remonstrate with God that empty space remains which exceeds creation a hundredfold, must not every godly mind detest the presumption? Of the same kind is the madness of those who charge God with idleness for not having pleased them by creating the world countless ages sooner than he did. In their greed they affect to go beyond the world, as if the ample circumference of heaven and earth did not contain objects numerous and splendid enough to absorb all our senses; as if, in the span of six thousand years, God had not furnished facts enough to exercise our minds in ceaseless meditation. Therefore let us willingly remain hedged in by those boundaries within which God has been pleased to confine our persons and, as it were, to enclose our minds, so as to keep them from losing themselves by wandering unrestrained.

2¶

With the same aim Moses relates that the work of creation was accomplished not in one moment but in six days. By this statement we are drawn away from fictions to the one God who divided his work into six days in this way, so that we may not be reluctant to devote our whole lives to contemplating it. For though our eyes, in whatever direction they turn, are compelled to behold the works of God, we see how fleeting our attention is, and how quickly godly thoughts, if any arise, vanish away. Here too objection is taken to these progressive steps as inconsistent with the power of God, until human reason is subdued to the obedience of faith and learns to welcome the calm rest to which the sanctification of the seventh day invited us. In the very order of events we ought diligently to ponder the fatherly goodness of God toward the human race, in not creating Adam until he had liberally enriched the earth with all good things. Had he placed him on an earth barren and unfurnished, had he given life before light, he might have seemed to pay little regard to his interest. But now that he has arranged the motions of the sun and stars for man's use, has filled the air, earth, and water with living creatures, and has produced every kind of fruit in abundance for the supply of food, he has shown his wondrous goodness toward us by performing the office of a provident and industrious head of a household. These subjects, which I only touch briefly, will, if pondered more attentively, make it plain that Moses was a sure witness and herald of the one only Creator. I do not repeat what I have already explained — namely, that mention is made here not of the bare essence of God, but that his eternal Wisdom and Spirit are also set before us, so that we may not dream of any other God than him who desires to be recognized in that express image.

3¶

But before I begin to treat more fully of the nature of man (chapter 15, and Book 2, chapter 1), it will be proper to say something about angels. For although Moses, accommodating himself to the ignorance of ordinary people, does not mention in the history of the creation any other works of God than those which meet our eye, yet since he afterward introduces angels as the ministers of God, we easily infer that he whom they serve is their Creator. Hence, though Moses, speaking in popular language, did not at the very beginning list the angels among the creatures of God, nothing prevents us from treating distinctly and explicitly what Scripture teaches about them in other places. For if we desire to know God by his works, we surely cannot pass over this noble and illustrious example. We may add that this branch of doctrine is very necessary for refuting numerous errors. The minds of many are so struck with the excellence of angelic natures that they would think them insulted by being subjected to the authority of God and so made subordinate. Hence an imagined divinity has been assigned to them. Manes too has arisen with his sect, inventing for himself two principles — God and the devil — attributing the origin of good things to God, but assigning all evil natures to the devil as their author. Were this delirium to take possession of our minds, God would be denied his glory in the creation of the world. For since nothing is more peculiar to God than eternity and αὐτουσία, that is, self-existence, or existence of himself, if I may so speak, do not those who attribute it to the devil in some degree invest him with the honor of divinity? And where is the omnipotence of God, if the devil has the power to carry out whatever he pleases against the will of God and in spite of his opposition? But the only sound ground the Manichees have — namely, that it would be impious to ascribe the creation of anything evil to a good God — in no way militates against the orthodox faith, since it is not admitted that there is anything naturally evil in the whole universe. The depravity and wickedness, whether of man or of the devil, and the sins that result from them, come not from nature but from the corruption of nature; nor at the first did anything whatever exist that did not display some manifestation of the divine wisdom and justice. To counter such perverse imaginations, we must raise our minds higher than our eyes can penetrate. It was probably with this aim that the Nicene Creed, in calling God the creator of all things, makes express mention of things invisible. My care, however, must be to keep within the bounds which godliness prescribes, lest by indulging in speculations beyond my reach I bewilder the reader and lead him away from the simplicity of the faith. And since the Holy Spirit always instructs us in what is useful, but altogether omits, or only touches in passing, matters which contribute little to edification, it is certainly our duty to remain in willing ignorance of all such matters.

4¶

Since angels are the ministers appointed to carry out the commands of God, they must of course be acknowledged to be his creatures; but to stir up questions about the time or order in which they were created (see Lombard, book 2, distinction 2, and following) shows more perverseness than diligence. Moses relates that the heavens and the earth were finished, with all their host; what does it profit to inquire anxiously at what time other, more hidden heavenly hosts than the stars and planets also began to exist? Not to dwell on this, let us remember here that on the whole subject of religion one rule of modesty and sobriety is to be observed, and it is this: in obscure matters, not to speak or think, or even long to know, more than the Word of God has delivered. A second rule is that in reading the Scriptures we should constantly direct our inquiries and meditations to those things which tend to edification, and not indulge in curiosity or in studying things of no use. And since the Lord has been pleased to instruct us, not in frivolous questions, but in solid godliness, in the fear of his name, in true faith, and in the duties of holiness, let us rest satisfied with such knowledge. Therefore, if we would be rightly wise, we must renounce those vain babblings of idle men concerning the nature, ranks, and number of angels, which have no authority from the Word of God. I know that many seize on these topics more eagerly, and take greater pleasure in them, than in those that relate to daily practice. But if we do not refuse to be the disciples of Christ, let us not refuse to follow the method which he has prescribed. In this way, being content with him as our master, we will not only refrain from the superfluous speculations which he discourages, but will even feel averse to them. No one can deny that Dionysius (whoever he may have been) has many shrewd and subtle discussions in his Celestial Hierarchy; but on looking at them more closely, everyone must see that they are mere idle talk. The duty of a theologian, however, is not to tickle the ear, but to confirm the conscience, by teaching what is true, certain, and useful. When you read the work of Dionysius, you would think that the man had come down from heaven and was relating, not what he had learned, but what he had actually seen. Paul, however, though he was caught up to the third heaven, so far from delivering anything of the kind, plainly declares that it was not lawful for man to speak the secrets which he had seen. Bidding farewell, therefore, to that worthless wisdom, let us endeavor to learn from the simple doctrine of Scripture what the Lord is pleased that we should know about angels.

5¶

In Scripture, then, we uniformly read that angels are heavenly spirits whose obedience and ministry God employs to carry out all the purposes he has decreed; and hence their name, as being a kind of intermediate messengers who make his will known to men. The names by which several of them are distinguished refer to the same office. They are called hosts because they surround their Prince as his court — they adorn and display his majesty — and, like soldiers, they keep their eyes always turned to their leader's standard, and are so ready and prompt to carry out his orders that the moment he gives the nod they prepare for the work, or rather are already at work. In declaring the magnificence of the divine throne, the prophets give similar representations, and especially Daniel, when he says that when God stood up to judgment, "thousand thousands ministered unto him, and ten thousand times ten thousand stood before him" (Daniel 7:10). Since by these means the Lord wonderfully exerts and declares the power and might of his hand, they are called virtues. Again, since his government of the world is exercised and administered through them, they are called at one time Principalities, at another Powers, at another Dominions (Colossians 1:16; Ephesians 1:21). Lastly, since the glory of God in some measure dwells in them, they are also called Thrones; though as to this last designation I am unwilling to speak positively, since a different interpretation is equally fitting, if not more so. To say nothing, therefore, of the name Thrones, the former names are often used by the Holy Spirit to commend the dignity of angelic service. Nor is it right to pass by without honor those instruments by whom God especially displays the presence of his power. Indeed, they are more than once called gods, because the Deity is in some measure represented to us in their service, as in a mirror. I am rather inclined, however, to agree with the ancient writers that in those passages Genesis 18:2; 32:1, 28; Joshua 5:14; Judges 6:14; 13:10, 22.

6¶

But the point on which the Scriptures especially insist is the one that tends most to our comfort and to the confirmation of our faith, namely, that angels are the ministers and dispensers of the divine bounty toward us. Accordingly, we are told how they watch over our safety, how they undertake our defense, direct our path, and take care that no evil befalls us. There are whole passages which relate in the first instance to Christ, the Head of the Church, and after him to all believers. "He shall give his angels charge over thee, to keep thee in all thy ways. They shall bear thee up in their hands, lest thou dash thy foot against a stone." Again, "The angel of the Lord encampeth round about them that fear him, and delivereth them." Psalm 91:11; 34:8; Genesis 16:9; 24:7; 48:16; Exodus 14:19, 28, 29; Judges 2:1, 20; 6:11; 13:10; Matthew 4:11; Luke 22:43; Matthew 28:5; Luke 24:5; Acts 1:10; 2 Kings 19:35; Isaiah 37:36.

7¶

Whether or not each believer has a single angel assigned to him for his defense, I dare not positively affirm. When Daniel introduces the angel of the Persians and the angel of the Greeks, he undoubtedly indicates that certain angels are appointed as a kind of governors over kingdoms and provinces. Daniel 10:13, 20; 12:1; Matthew 18:20; Luke 15:7; 16:22; 2 Kings 16:17; Acts 12:15.

There is one passage which seems to indicate somewhat more clearly that each individual has a separate angel. When Peter, after his deliverance from prison, knocked at the door of the house where the brethren were gathered, they, unable to think it could be he himself, said that it was his angel. This idea seems to have been suggested to them by a common belief that every believer has a single angel assigned to him. Here, however, it may be answered that nothing prevents us from understanding it of any one of the angels to whom the Lord might have given the charge of Peter at that particular time, without implying that he was to be his perpetual guardian, according to the popular imagination (see Calvin on Mark 5:9) that two angels, a good one and a bad one, are assigned to each individual as a kind of genii. After all, it is not worthwhile to investigate anxiously a point which does not greatly concern us. If anyone does not think it enough to know that all the orders of the heavenly host are perpetually watching over his safety, I do not see what he could gain by knowing that he has one angel as a special guardian. Those, again, who limit the care which God takes of each of us to a single angel do great injury to themselves and to all the members of the Church, as if there were no value in those promises of auxiliary troops who, encircling and defending us on every side, embolden us to fight more manfully.

8¶

Those who presume to dogmatize about the ranks and numbers of angels would do well to consider on what foundation they rest. As to their rank, I admit that Michael is described by Daniel as a mighty Prince, and by Jude as an Archangel. Daniel 12:1; Jude 9; 1 Thessalonians 4:16; Daniel 10:13, 21; Luke 1:19, 26; Tobit 3:17; 5:5; Matthew 26:53; Daniel 7:10; 2 Kings 6:17; Psalm 34:7.

9¶

There is one point, however, which, though called into doubt by certain restless individuals, we ought to hold as certain — namely, that angels are ministering spirits (Hebrews 1:14), whose service God employs for the protection of his people, through whom he distributes his favors among men, and by whom he also carries out other works. The Sadducees of old maintained that by angels nothing more was meant than the impulses which God impresses on men, or the manifestations which he gives of his own power (Acts 23:8). But this dream is contradicted by so many passages of Scripture that it seems strange how such gross ignorance could have found any countenance among the Jews. To say nothing of the passages I have already quoted, there are passages which refer to thousands and legions of angels, and speak of them as rejoicing, as bearing up the faithful in their hands, carrying their souls to rest, beholding the face of their Father, and so forth: Luke 15:10; Psalm 91:11; Matthew 4:6; Luke 4:10, 16, 22; Matthew 18:10; Acts 7:55; Galatians 3:19; Matthew 22:80; 24:36; Ephesians 30:10; 1 Peter 1:12; Hebrews 1:6; Psalm 97:7.

10¶

It remains to warn against the superstition which usually begins to creep in when it is said that all blessings are ministered and dispensed to us by angels. For the human mind is apt to think at once that there is no honor which they ought not to receive, and so the peculiar offices of Christ and of God are bestowed on them. In this way the glory of Christ was greatly obscured for several past ages, extravagant praises being pronounced on angels without any authority from Scripture. Among the corruptions which we now oppose, there is scarcely any of greater antiquity. Even Paul appears to have had a severe contest with some who so exalted angels as to make them almost the superiors of Christ. Hence he so anxiously urges in his Epistle to the Colossians (Colossians 1:16, 20) that Christ is not only superior to all angels, but that all the endowments they possess are derived from him; and so he warns us against forsaking him by turning to those who are not sufficient for themselves, but must draw with us from a common fountain. Since the radiance of the divine glory is displayed in them, there is nothing to which we are more prone than to prostrate ourselves before them in stupid adoration, and then to ascribe to them the blessings which we owe to God alone. Even John confesses in the Apocalypse (Revelation 19:10; 22:8, 9) that this was his own case, but he immediately adds the answer that was given to him: "See thou do it not; I am thy fellow servant: worship God."

11¶

We will happily avoid this danger if we consider why it is that God, instead of acting directly without their agency, is accustomed to employ it in displaying his power, providing for the safety of his people, and imparting the gifts of his kindness. He certainly does this not from necessity, as if he were unable to do without them. Whenever he pleases, he passes them by and performs his own work by a single nod — so far are they from relieving him of any difficulty. Therefore, when he employs them, it is as a help to our weakness, so that nothing may be lacking to raise our hopes or strengthen our confidence. It ought indeed to be enough for us that the Lord declares himself to be our protector. But when we see ourselves beset by so many perils, so many injuries, so many kinds of enemies, such is our frailty and softness that we might at times be filled with alarm or driven to despair, if the Lord did not proclaim his gracious presence by some means suited to our feeble capacities. For this reason he not only promises to take care of us, but assures us that he has numberless attendants to whom he has committed the charge of our safety, so that whatever dangers may threaten, as long as we are encircled by their protection and guardianship, we are placed beyond all hazard of evil. I admit that once we have a simple assurance of the divine protection, it is wrong for us still to look around for help. But since the Lord, in his infinite goodness and indulgence, is pleased to provide for this weakness of ours, it would ill become us to overlook the favor. Of this we have an example in the servant of Elisha (2 Kings 6:17), who, seeing the mountain surrounded by the army of the Assyrians and no means of escape, was completely overcome with terror and thought it was all over with himself and his master. Then Elisha prayed to God to open the eyes of the servant, who at once saw the mountain filled with horses and chariots of fire — in other words, with a multitude of angels, to whom he and the prophet had been given in charge. Strengthened by the vision, he took courage and could boldly defy the enemy whose appearance had previously filled him with dismay.

12¶

Whatever, therefore, is said about the ministry of angels, let us use it for the purpose of removing all distrust and strengthening our confidence in God. Since the Lord has provided us with such protection, let us not be terrified at the multitude of our enemies, as if they could prevail in spite of his aid, but let us adopt the conviction of Elisha, that more are for us than against us. How perverse, therefore, is it to allow ourselves to be led away from God by angels who have been appointed for the very purpose of assuring us of his more immediate presence to help us! Yet we are led away in this way if angels do not lead us directly to him — making us look to him, call on him, and celebrate him as our only defender — if they are not regarded merely as hands moving to our assistance just as he directs — if they do not direct us to Christ as the only Mediator, on whom we must wholly depend and lean, looking toward him and resting in him. Our minds ought to give thorough heed to what Jacob saw in his vision (Genesis 28:12): angels descending to the earth to men, and again mounting up from men to heaven by means of a ladder, at the top of which the Lord of Hosts was seated. This indicates that it is solely by the intercession of Christ that the ministry of angels reaches us, as he himself declares: "Hereafter ye shall see heaven open, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of man" (John 1:51). Accordingly, the servant of Abraham, though he had been committed to the guardianship of an angel (Genesis 24:7), does not therefore call on that angel to be present with him, but, trusting in that commendation, pours out his prayers before the Lord and entreats him to show mercy to Abraham. As God does not make angels the ministers of his power and goodness in order to share his glory with them, so he does not promise his assistance through their instrumentality in order that we may divide our confidence between him and them. Away, then, with that Platonic philosophy of seeking access to God by means of angels, and of courting them with a view to making God more propitious (Plato, Epinomis and Cratylus) — a philosophy which presumptuous and superstitious men at first attempted to introduce into our religion, and which they persist in even to this day.

13¶

The whole tendency of what Scripture teaches about devils is to put us on our guard against their wiles and schemes, so that we may provide ourselves with weapons strong enough to drive away the most formidable foes. For when Satan is called the god and ruler of this world, the strong man armed, the prince of the power of the air, the roaring lion, 2 Corinthians 4:4; John 12:31; Matthew 12:29; Ephesians 2:2.

14¶

That we may feel all the more strongly urged to do so, Scripture declares that the enemies who war against us are not one or two, or few in number, but a great host. Mary Magdalene is said to have been delivered from seven devils by which she was possessed; and our Savior assures us that it commonly happens, when a devil has been cast out, that if access is given to it again, it takes seven other spirits more wicked than itself and resumes the vacant possession. Indeed, one man is said to have been possessed by a whole legion. Mark 16:9; Matthew 12:43; Luke 8:30.

15¶

One thing which ought to rouse us to perpetual combat with the devil is that he is everywhere called both our adversary and the adversary of God. For if the glory of God is dear to us, as it ought to be, we ought to struggle with all our might against him who aims at extinguishing that glory. If we are animated with proper zeal to maintain the kingdom of Christ, we must wage irreconcilable war with him who conspires its ruin. Again, if we have any concern for our own salvation, we ought to make neither peace nor truce with him who is continually laying schemes for its destruction. But such is the character given to Satan in the third chapter of Genesis, where he is seen seducing man from his allegiance to God, so that he may both deprive God of his due honor and plunge man headlong into destruction. Such too is the description given of him in the Gospels (Matthew 13:28), where he is called the enemy and is said to sow tares in order to corrupt the seed of eternal life. In a word, in all his actions we experience the truth of our Savior's description, that he was "a murderer from the beginning, and abode not in the truth" (John 8:44). Truth he assails with lies; light he obscures with darkness. He entangles the minds of men in error; he stirs up hatred, inflames strife and war, and all in order that he may overthrow the kingdom of God and drown men in eternal perdition with himself. Hence it is evident that his whole nature is depraved, mischievous, and malignant. There must be extreme depravity in a mind bent on assailing the glory of God and the salvation of man. John indicates this in his Epistle when he says that he "sinneth from the beginning" (1 John 3:8), implying that he is the author, leader, and contriver of all malice and wickedness.

16¶

But since the devil was created by God, we must remember that this malice which we attribute to his nature comes not from creation but from depravation. Everything damnable in him he brought upon himself by his revolt and fall. Scripture reminds us of this, lest by believing that he was created so at first we should ascribe to God what is most foreign to his nature. For this reason Christ declares (John 8:44) that Satan, when he lies, "speaketh of his own," and states the reason, "because he abode not in the truth." By saying that he abode not in the truth, he certainly implies that he once was in the truth; and by calling him the father of lies, he takes from him all power to charge God with the depravity of which he himself was the cause. But although the expressions are brief and not very explicit, they are amply sufficient to vindicate the majesty of God from every slander. And what more does it concern us to know about devils? Some murmur because Scripture does not give, in various passages, a distinct and orderly account of Satan's fall, its cause, manner, date, and nature. But since these things are of no consequence to us, it was better, if not to pass them over entirely in silence, at least to touch on them only lightly. The Holy Spirit would not deign to feed curiosity with idle, unprofitable histories. We see that it was the Lord's purpose to deliver nothing in his sacred oracles which we might not learn for edification. Therefore, instead of dwelling on superfluous matters, let it be enough for us to hold briefly, with regard to the nature of devils, that at their first creation they were the angels of God, but by revolting they both ruined themselves and became the instruments of perdition to others. Since it was useful to know this much, it is clearly taught by Peter and Jude: "God," they say, "spared not the angels that sinned, but cast them down to hell, and delivered them into chains of darkness to be reserved unto judgment" (2 Peter 2:4; Jude verse 6). And Paul, by speaking of the elect angels, plainly draws a tacit contrast between them and reprobate angels.

17¶

With regard to the strife and war which Satan is said to wage with God, it must be understood with this qualification: that Satan cannot possibly do anything against the will and consent of God. For we read in the history of Job that Satan appears in the presence of God to receive his commands, and does not dare to proceed with any undertaking until he is authorized. In the same way, when Ahab was to be deceived, he undertook to be a lying spirit in the mouth of all the prophets, and on being commissioned by the Lord, he proceeds to do so. For this reason too, the spirit which tormented Saul is said to be an evil spirit from the Lord, because he was, as it were, the scourge by which the misdeeds of the wicked king were punished. In another place it is said that the plagues of Egypt were inflicted by God through the instrumentality of wicked angels. In keeping with these particular examples, Paul declares generally that unbelievers are blinded by God, though he had previously described it as the doing of Satan. Job 1:6; 2:1; 1 Kings 22:20; 1 Samuel 16:14; 18:10; 2 Thessalonians 2:9, 11.

18¶

God, thus turning the unclean spirits this way and that at his pleasure, employs them in exercising believers: they war against them, assail them with wiles, urge them with solicitations, press close upon them, disturb, alarm, and occasionally wound them, but never conquer or overwhelm them; whereas they hold the wicked in bondage, exercise dominion over their minds and bodies, and employ them as slaves in every kind of iniquity. Because believers are disturbed by such enemies, they are addressed with exhortations such as these: "Neither give place to the devil"; "Your adversary the devil, as a roaring lion, walketh about seeking whom he may devour; whom resist steadfast in the faith" (Ephesians 4:27; 1 Peter 5:8). Paul acknowledges that he was not exempt from this kind of contest when he says that, for the purpose of subduing his pride, a messenger of Satan was sent to buffet him (2 Corinthians 12:7). This trial, therefore, is common to all the children of God. But since the promise of bruising Satan's head (Genesis 3:15) applies alike to Christ and to all his members, I deny that believers can ever be overwhelmed or vanquished by him. They are often, indeed, thrown into alarm, but never so thoroughly that they do not recover themselves. They fall under the violence of the blows, but they get up again; they are wounded, but not mortally. In short, they labor on through the whole course of their lives, so as ultimately to gain the victory, though they meet with occasional defeats. We know how David, through the just anger of God, was left for a time to Satan, and at his instigation numbered the people (2 Samuel 24:1); and it is not without cause that Paul holds out a hope of pardon in case any should have become ensnared by the wiles of the devil (2 Timothy 2:26). Accordingly, he shows elsewhere that the promise quoted above begins in this life, where the struggle is carried on, and that it is completed after the struggle is ended. His words are, "The God of peace shall bruise Satan under your feet shortly" (Romans 16:20). In our Head, indeed, this victory was always perfect, because the prince of the world "had nothing" in him (John 14:30); but in us, who are his members, it is now partially obtained, and will be perfected when we have put off our mortal flesh, through which we are subject to infirmity, and have been filled with the power of the Holy Spirit. In this way, when the kingdom of Christ is raised up and established, that of Satan falls, as our Lord himself expresses it: "I beheld Satan as lightning fall from heaven" (Luke 10:18). By these words he confirmed the report which the apostles gave of the effectiveness of their preaching. In like manner he says, "When a strong man armed keepeth his palace, his goods are in peace. But when a stronger than he shall come upon him, and overcome him, he taketh from him all his armor wherein he trusted, and divideth his spoils" (Luke 11:21, 22). And to this end Christ, by dying, overcame Satan, who had the power of death (Hebrews 2:14), and triumphed over all his hosts, so that they might not injure the Church, which otherwise would suffer from them every moment. For (such being our weakness, and such his raging fury) how could we withstand his manifold and unceasing assaults for any period, however short, if we did not trust in the victory of our leader? God, therefore, does not allow Satan to have dominion over the souls of believers, but gives over to his sway only the ungodly and unbelieving, whom he does not deign to number among his flock. For the devil is said to have undisputed possession of this world until he is dispossessed by Christ. In like manner he is said to blind all who do not believe the Gospel, and to do his own work in the children of disobedience. And justly; for all the wicked are vessels of wrath, and to whom, then, should they be subjected but to the minister of the divine vengeance? In short, they are said to be of their father the devil. 2 Corinthians 4:4; Ephesians 2:2; Romans 9:22; John 8:44; 1 John 3:8.

19¶

Having refuted above that worthless philosophy concerning the holy angels which teaches that they are nothing but good impulses or inspirations which God stirs up in the minds of men, we must likewise refute here those who foolishly allege that devils are nothing but evil affections or disturbances suggested by our carnal nature. A brief refutation is to be found in the passages of Scripture on this subject, passages neither few nor obscure. First, when they are called unclean spirits and apostate angels (Matthew 12:43; Jude, verse 6), who have degenerated from their original state, the very terms sufficiently declare that they are not impulses or affections of the mind, but truly, as they are called, minds or spirits endowed with sense and intellect. In like manner, when the children of God are contrasted by John, and also by our Savior, with the children of the devil, would not the contrast be absurd if the term devil meant nothing more than evil inspirations? And John adds still more emphatically that the devil sinneth from the beginning (1 John 3:8). In like manner, when Jude introduces the archangel Michael contending with the devil (Jude, verse 9), he certainly contrasts a wicked and rebellious angel with a good one. To this corresponds the account given in the Book of Job, that Satan appeared in the presence of God with the holy angels. But the clearest passages of all are those which mention the punishment which, by the judgment of God, they already begin to feel, and are to feel more especially at the resurrection: "What have we to do with thee, Jesus, thou Son of God? art thou come hither to torment us before the time?" (Matthew 8:29); and again, "Depart, ye cursed, into everlasting fire, prepared for the devil and his angels" (Matthew 25:41). Again, "If God spared not the angels that sinned, but cast them down to hell, and delivered them into chains of darkness to be reserved unto judgment," and so on (2 Peter 2:4). How absurd would be the expressions that devils are doomed to eternal punishment, that fire is prepared for them, that they are even now tormented and racked by the glory of Christ, if there were in truth no devils at all! But since all discussion of this subject is superfluous for those who believe the Word of God, while little is gained by quoting Scripture to those empty speculators whom nothing but novelty can please, I believe I have already done enough for my purpose, which was to put the godly on their guard against the delirious dreams with which restless men harass themselves and the simple. The subject, however, deserved to be touched on, lest any, by embracing that error, should imagine that they have no enemy, and so be more remiss or less cautious in resisting.

20¶

Meanwhile, placed as we are in this most beautiful theater, let us not refuse to take a godly delight in the clear and manifest works of God. For, as we have observed elsewhere, though it is not the chief evidence of faith, it is in point of order the first: to remember, whichever way we turn, that all which meets the eye is the work of God, and at the same time to meditate with godly care on the end which God had in view in creating it. Therefore, in order that we may grasp with true faith what it is necessary to know about God, it is important to attend to the history of the creation, as briefly recorded by Moses and afterward more fully illustrated by godly writers, especially by Basil and Ambrose. From this history we learn that God, by the power of his Word and his Spirit, created the heavens and the earth out of nothing; that thereafter he produced things inanimate and animate of every kind, arranging an innumerable variety of objects in admirable order, giving each kind its proper nature, office, place, and station; and at the same time, since all things were liable to corruption, providing for the perpetuation of each single species — cherishing some by secret means and, as it were, instilling new vigor into them from time to time, and bestowing on others a power of propagating their kind, so that it might not perish at their own death. Heaven and earth being thus most richly adorned and abundantly supplied with all things, like a large and splendid mansion magnificently built and exquisitely furnished, at length man was made — man, who by the beauty of his person and his many noble endowments is the most glorious specimen of the works of God. But since I have no intention of giving the history of creation in detail, it is enough to have touched on it again briefly in passing. I have already reminded my reader that the best course for him is to derive his knowledge of the subject from Moses and others who have carefully and faithfully handed down an account of the creation.

21¶

It is unnecessary to dwell at length on the end that should be aimed at in considering the works of God. The subject has been explained in great measure elsewhere, and, so far as our present work requires, may now be disposed of in a few words. Undoubtedly, if one were to attempt to speak in fitting terms of the inestimable wisdom, power, justice, and goodness of God in the formation of the world, no grace or splendor of language could equal the greatness of the subject. Still, there can be no doubt that the Lord would have us constantly occupied with such holy meditation, so that, while we contemplate the immense treasures of wisdom and goodness displayed in the creatures as in so many mirrors, we may not only run our eye over them with a hasty and, as it were, fleeting glance, but dwell long upon them, turn them over in our minds seriously and faithfully, and call them to mind again and again. But since the present work is of a teaching nature, we cannot fittingly enter on topics which require lengthy discourse. Therefore, to be brief, let the reader understand that he has a genuine grasp of the character of God as the Creator of the world: first, if he follows the general rule never thoughtlessly or forgetfully to overlook the glorious perfections which God displays in his creatures; and secondly, if he applies to himself what he sees, so as to fix it deeply in his heart. The former is illustrated when we consider how great the Architect must be who framed and ordered the multitude of the starry host so admirably that it is impossible to imagine a more glorious sight: so stationing some and fixing them to particular spots that they cannot move; giving a freer course to others, yet setting limits to their wanderings; so tempering the movement of the whole as to measure out day and night, months, years, and seasons; and at the same time so regulating the inequality of days as to prevent anything like confusion. The former course is illustrated, moreover, when we attend to his power in sustaining the vast mass and guiding the swift revolutions of the heavenly bodies, and so on. These few examples sufficiently explain what is meant by recognizing the divine perfections in the creation of the world. Were we to attempt to go over the whole subject, we should never come to an end, since there are as many miracles of divine power, as many striking evidences of wisdom and goodness, as there are classes of objects — indeed, as there are individual objects, great or small — throughout the universe.

22¶

The other course, which has a closer relation to faith, remains to be considered — namely, that while we observe how God has destined all things for our good and salvation, we at the same time feel his power and grace both in ourselves and in the great blessings which he has bestowed on us, and so stir ourselves up to confidence in him, to calling on him, to praise, and to love. Moreover, as I observed a little earlier, the Lord himself, by the very order of creation, has demonstrated that he created all things for the sake of man. Nor is it unimportant to note that he divided the formation of the world into six days, though it would have been in no way more difficult to complete the whole work, in all its parts, in one moment than by gradual progression. But he was pleased to display his providence and fatherly care toward us in this: that before he formed man, he provided whatever he foresaw would be useful and wholesome for him. How ungrateful, then, would it be to doubt whether we are cared for by this most excellent Parent, who we see cared for us even before we were born! How impious would it be to tremble in distrust, lest we should one day be abandoned in our need by that kindness which, before we existed, displayed itself in a complete supply of all good things! Moreover, Moses tells us that everything the world contains is liberally placed at our disposal. God certainly did not do this in order to mock us with an empty form of gift. Nothing, therefore, which concerns our safety will ever be lacking. To conclude in a word: as often as we call God the Creator of heaven and earth, let us remember that the disposal of all the things which he created is in his hand and power, but that we are his sons, whom he has undertaken to nourish and bring up in allegiance to him, so that we may expect the substance of all good from him alone, and have full hope that he will never allow us to lack the things necessary for salvation, so as to leave us dependent on some other source; that in everything we desire we may address our prayers to him, and in every benefit we receive acknowledge his hand and give him thanks; and that, thus drawn by his great goodness and kindness, we may strive with our whole heart to love and serve him.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin reads the creation as a mirror in which the true God is distinguished from every counterfeit—the world made from nothing in six unhurried days, Adam set last upon an earth already richly furnished by a provident Father. The same Scripture that shows us angels as ministering servants of our safety also unmasks Satan as a real and restless enemy who can do nothing without God's permission, that we may neither idolize the one nor despair before the other. This is bracing for 2026. We live among those who flatten reality to what the eye can measure and laugh off the spiritual war Scripture describes; the surest victory of the enemy, Calvin notes, is to persuade us he is not there. Yet the believer stands encircled by more than are against him. Let the Christian home rest in the providence of a Father who cared for us before we were born, wage steadfast war against the evil one, and give thanks for a world made for our good and his glory.
