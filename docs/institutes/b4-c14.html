Of the Sacraments

This chapter consists of two principal parts,—I. Of sacraments in general. The sum of the doctrine stated, sec. 1-6. Two classes of opponents to be guarded against—viz. those who undervalue the power of the sacraments, sec. 7-13; and those who attribute too much to the sacraments, sec. 14-17. II. Of the sacraments in particular, both of the Old and the New Testament. Their scope and meaning. Refutation of those who have either too high or too low ideas of the sacraments. 1. Of the sacraments in general. A sacrament defined. 2. Meaning of the word sacrament. 3. Definition explained. Why God seals his promises to us by sacraments. 4. The word which ought to accompany the element, that the sacrament may be complete. 5. Error of those who attempt to separate the word, or promise of God, from the element. 6. Why sacraments are called Signs of the Covenant. 7. They are such signs, though the wicked should receive them, but are signs of grace only to believers. 8. Objections to this view answered. 9. No secret virtue in the sacraments. Their whole efficacy depends on the inward operation of the Spirit. 10. Objections answered. Illustrated by a simile. 11. Of the increase of faith by the preaching of the word. 12. In what way, and how far, the sacraments are confirmations of our faith. 13. Some regard the sacraments as mere signs. This view refuted. 14. Some again attribute too much to the sacraments. Refutation. 15. Refutation confirmed by a passage from Augustine. 16. Previous views more fully explained. 17. The matter of the sacrament always present when the sacrament is duly administered. 18. Extensive meaning of the term sacrament. 19. The ordinary sacraments in the Church. How necessary they are. 20. The sacraments of the Old and of the New Testament. The end of both the same —viz. to lead us to Christ. 21. This apparent in the sacraments of the Old Testament. 22. Apparent also in the sacraments of the New Testament. 23. Impious doctrine of the Schoolmen as to the difference between the Old and the New Testaments. 24. Scholastic objection answered. 25. Another objection answered. 26. Sacraments of the New Testament sometimes excessively extolled by early Theologians. Their meaning explained.

1¶

Closely joined to the preaching of the gospel, we have another help to our faith in the sacraments. It greatly concerns us that some sure doctrine about them should be set forth, teaching us both the end for which they were instituted and their present use. First, we must consider what a sacrament is. It seems to me, then, a simple and fitting definition to say that it is an outward sign by which the Lord seals on our consciences his promises of good will toward us, in order to sustain the weakness of our faith; and we in our turn testify our piety toward him, both before himself and before angels as well as men. We may also define it more briefly by calling it a testimony of the divine favor toward us, confirmed by an outward sign, with a corresponding attestation of our faith toward him. You may choose either of these definitions. In meaning they do not differ from that of Augustine, who defines a sacrament as a visible sign of a sacred thing, or a visible form of an invisible grace; but his does not contain a better or surer explanation. Because its brevity makes it somewhat obscure, and so misleads the less educated, I wished to remove all doubt and to make the definition fuller by stating it at greater length.

2¶

The reason why the ancients used the term in this sense is not obscure. The old translator, whenever he wished to render the Greek term μυστήριον into Latin, especially when it was used with reference to divine things, used the word sacramentum. So in Ephesians: "Having made known unto us the mystery (sacramentum) of his will"; and again, "If you have heard of the dispensation of the grace of God, which is given me to you-ward, how that by revelation he made known unto me the mystery" (sacramentum) (Ephesians 1:9; 3:2). In Colossians: "Even the mystery which has been hid from ages and from generations, but is now made manifest to his saints, to whom God would make known what is the riches of the glory of this mystery" (sacramentum) (Colossians 1:26). Also in the First Epistle to Timothy: "Without controversy, great is the mystery (sacramentum) of godliness: God was manifest in the flesh" (1 Timothy 3:16). He was unwilling to use the word arcanum (secret), lest the word should seem beneath the greatness of the thing meant. When the thing, therefore, was both sacred and secret, he used the term sacramentum. In this sense it occurs frequently in the church writers. And it is well known that what the Latins call sacramenta, the Greeks call μυστήρια (mysteries). The sameness of meaning removes all dispute. Hence the term came to be applied to those signs which gave a majestic representation of spiritual and sublime things. Augustine also observes this: "It would be tedious to discourse on the variety of signs; those which relate to divine things are called sacraments" (Augustine, Letter 5, to Marcellinus).

3¶

From the definition we have given, we see that there is never a sacrament without a preceding promise. The sacrament is added as a kind of appendix, with the purpose of confirming and sealing the promise, and of giving it a better attestation, or rather, in a certain way, of confirming it. In this way God provides first for our ignorance and sluggishness, and second for our weakness; and yet, properly speaking, the sacrament does not so much confirm his word as establish us in faith in it. 122 D122 That is, the sacrament cannot make the promise of God objectively more certain, but it can make our faith in God's promise subjectively more certain. God's Word is always absolute, strong, unchangeable, and "settled in heaven"; but our faith, throughout this life, is always relative, weak, changeable, and frequently in need of confirmation and assurance. So we rightly distinguish between the objective certainty of God's Word and the subjective certainty of our faith.

4¶

This is commonly expressed by saying that a sacrament consists of the word and the outward sign. By the word we ought to understand, not a word which, muttered without meaning and without faith, consecrates the element by its mere sound, as by a magical incantation, but one which, when preached, makes us understand what the visible sign means. What was frequently done under the tyranny of the Pope, therefore, was not free from great profanation of the mystery. They thought it enough if the priest muttered the formula of consecration while the people, without understanding, looked stupidly on. Indeed, this was done for the express purpose of preventing any instruction from reaching the people by it, for everything was said in Latin to uneducated hearers. Superstition was afterward carried to such a height that the consecration was thought not to be duly performed except in a low murmur which few could hear. Very different is Augustine's doctrine of the sacramental word: "Let the word be added to the element, and it will become a sacrament. For from where can water have such power as to touch the body and cleanse the heart, unless by the working of the word, and this not because it is spoken, but because it is believed? For even in the word, the passing sound is one thing, the abiding power another. 'This is the word of faith which we preach,' says the Apostle" (Romans 10:8). Hence in the Acts of the Apostles we have the expression, "Purifying their hearts by faith" (Acts 15:9). And the apostle Peter says, "The like figure whereunto even baptism does now save us (not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience)" (1 Peter 3:21). "This is the word of faith which we preach: by which word, no doubt, baptism also is consecrated, so that it may be able to cleanse" (Augustine, Homilies on John, 13). You see how he requires preaching for the production of faith. Nor do we need to labor to prove this, since there is not the least room for doubt about what Christ did and commanded us to do, what the apostles followed, and what a purer Church observed. Indeed, it is known that from the very beginning of the world, whenever God offered any sign to the holy patriarchs, it was inseparably bound to doctrine, without which our senses would gaze bewildered at a meaningless object. Therefore, when we hear mention of the sacramental word, let us understand it as the promise which, proclaimed aloud by the minister, leads the people by the hand to that toward which the sign points and directs us.

5¶

Nor should we listen to those who oppose this view with a dilemma that is more subtle than solid. They argue in this way: either we know that the word of God which precedes the sacrament is the true will of God, or we do not know it. If we know it, we learn nothing new from the sacrament that follows. If we do not know it, we cannot learn it from the sacrament, whose whole efficacy depends on the word. Our brief reply is this. The seals attached to diplomas and other public documents are nothing considered in themselves, and would be attached to no purpose if nothing were written on the parchment; and yet this does not prevent them from sealing and confirming when they are appended to writings. It cannot be said that this comparison is a recent invention of our own, since Paul himself used it, calling circumcision a seal (Romans 4:11). There he expressly maintains that the circumcision of Abraham was not for justification, but was an attestation to the covenant, by faith in which he had already been justified. And how, I ask, can anyone be greatly offended when we teach that the promise is sealed by the sacrament, since it is plain from the promises themselves that one promise confirms another? The clearer any evidence is, the fitter it is to support our faith. But sacraments bring with them the clearest promises, and, when compared with the word, they have this peculiarity: they represent the promises to the life, as if painted in a picture. Nor should we be moved by an objection founded on the difference between sacraments and the seals of documents, namely, that since both consist of the bodily elements of this world, the former cannot be sufficient or adequate to seal the promises of God, which are spiritual and eternal, though the latter may be used to seal the edicts of princes concerning fleeting and fading things. For the believer, when the sacraments are presented to his eye, does not stop short at the bodily spectacle, but by the steps of analogy which I have indicated, rises with godly reflection to the sublime mysteries which lie hidden in the sacraments.

6¶

As the Lord calls his promises covenants (Genesis 6:18; 9:9; 17:2), and the sacraments signs of the covenants, something similar may be inferred from human covenants. What could the slaughter of a hog accomplish unless words came between, or rather went before? Swine are often killed without any inward or hidden mystery. What could be gained by giving the right hand, since hands are not seldom joined in giving battle? But when words have gone before, then by such symbols of covenant, sanction is given to laws, though they were previously conceived, worked out, and enacted in words. Sacraments, therefore, are exercises which confirm our faith in the word of God; and because we are creatures of flesh, they are shown to us under bodily objects, so that they may train us in a manner suited to our sluggish capacity, just as nurses lead children by the hand. And hence Augustine calls a sacrament a visible word (Augustine, Homilies on John, 89), because it represents the promises of God as in a picture, and sets them before our view in a vivid bodily form (Augustine, Against Faustus, book 19). We might mention other comparisons by which the sacraments are more plainly described, as when they are called the pillars of our faith. For just as a building stands and rests on its foundation, and yet is made more stable when supported by pillars, so faith rests on the word of God as its proper foundation, and yet, when sacraments are added, rests more firmly, as if supported on pillars. Or we may call them mirrors, in which we may contemplate the riches of the grace which God bestows on us. For in them, as has been said, he shows himself to us as far as our dullness enables us to recognize him, and testifies his love and kindness to us more expressly than by word.

7¶

It is unreasonable to contend that the sacraments are not manifestations of divine grace toward us because they are held out to the ungodly also, who, far from finding God more favorable to them, only incur greater condemnation. By the same reasoning the gospel will be no manifestation of the grace of God, because it is spurned by many who hear it; nor will Christ himself be a manifestation of grace, because of the many by whom he was seen and known, very few received him. Something similar may be seen in public enactments. A great part of the people mock and evade the authenticating seal, though they know it was used by their sovereign to confirm his will; others trample it underfoot, as a matter that in no way concerns them; while others even curse it. So, seeing that the condition of the two things is alike, the fitness of the comparison I made above ought to be more readily granted. It is certain, therefore, that the Lord offers us his mercy, and a pledge of his grace, both in his sacred word and in the sacraments; but it is not grasped except by those who receive the word and sacraments with firm faith, just as Christ, though offered and held out to all for salvation, is not, however, acknowledged and received by all. Augustine, wishing to indicate this, said that the efficacy of the word is produced in the sacrament, not because it is spoken, but because it is believed. Hence Paul, when addressing believers, includes communion with Christ in the sacraments, as when he says, "As many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ" (Galatians 3:27); and again, "For by one Spirit we are all baptized into one body" (1 Corinthians 12:13). But when he speaks of a perverse use of the sacraments, he attributes nothing more to them than to cold, empty figures. By this he indicates that however much the ungodly and hypocrites may, by their perversity, suppress, obscure, or hinder the effect of divine grace in the sacraments, this does not prevent the sacraments, where and whenever God is pleased, from giving true evidence of communion with Christ, or prevent them from displaying, and the Spirit of God from performing, the very thing which they promise. We conclude, therefore, that the sacraments are truly called evidences of divine grace, and, as it were, seals of the good will which he bears toward us. By sealing it to us, they sustain, nourish, confirm, and increase our faith. The objections usually urged against this view are frivolous and weak. They say that our faith, if it is good, cannot be made better, for there is no faith except that which rests unshakably, firmly, and undividedly on the mercy of God. It would have been better for the objectors to pray with the apostles, "Lord, increase our faith" (Luke 17:5), than confidently to maintain a perfection of faith which none of the sons of men ever attained, and none ever shall attain, in this life. Let them explain what kind of faith his was who said, "Lord, I believe; help my unbelief" (Mark 9:24). That faith, though only begun, was good, and might be made better by the removal of the unbelief. But there is no better argument to refute them than their own consciousness. For if they confess themselves sinners (and this, whether they like it or not, they cannot deny), then they must of necessity attribute this very quality to the imperfection of their faith.

8¶

But Philip, they say, replied to the eunuch who asked to be baptized, "If you believe with all your heart, you may" (Acts 8:37). What room is there for a confirmation by baptism when faith fills the whole heart? I in turn ask them: do they not feel that a good part of their heart is empty of faith? Do they not perceive new additions to it every day? There was one who boasted that he grew old while still learning. Three times miserable, then, are we Christians if we grow old without making progress, we whose faith ought to advance through every period of life until it grows up into a perfect man (Ephesians 4:13). In this passage, therefore, to believe with the whole heart is not to believe Christ perfectly, but only to embrace him sincerely with heart and soul; not to be filled with him, but with ardent affection to hunger and thirst and sigh after him. It is common in Scripture to say that a thing is done with the whole heart when it is done sincerely and from the heart. Of this kind are the following passages: "With my whole heart have I sought you" (Psalm 119:10); "I will confess unto you with my whole heart," and so on. In the same way, when the fraudulent and deceitful are rebuked, it is said, "With flattering lips, and with a double heart, do they speak" (Psalm 12:2). The objectors next add: "If faith is increased by means of the sacraments, the Holy Spirit is given in vain, since it is his office to begin, sustain, and complete our faith." I admit, indeed, that faith is the proper and entire work of the Holy Spirit; enlightened by him, we recognize God and the treasures of his grace, and without his illumination our mind is so blind that it can see nothing, so dull that it has no taste for spiritual things. But for the one divine blessing which they proclaim, we count three. For first, the Lord teaches and trains us by his word; next, he confirms us by his sacraments; lastly, he enlightens our mind by the light of his Holy Spirit, and opens an entrance into our hearts for his word and sacraments, which would otherwise only strike our ears and fall upon our sight, but would in no way affect us inwardly.

9¶

Therefore, with regard to the increase and confirmation of faith, I would remind the reader (though I think I have already stated it in unambiguous terms) that in assigning this office to the sacraments, I do not mean that there is a kind of secret efficacy perpetually inherent in them, by which they can of themselves promote or strengthen faith, but that our Lord has instituted them for the express purpose of helping to establish and increase our faith. The sacraments duly perform their office only when accompanied by the Spirit, the inward Teacher, whose power alone penetrates the heart, stirs the affections, and gains entrance for the sacraments into our souls. If he is absent, the sacraments can do no more for us than the sun shining on the eyeballs of the blind, or sounds spoken into the ears of the deaf. Therefore, in dividing between the Spirit and the sacraments, I ascribe the whole power to him and leave only a ministry to them. This ministry, without the working of the Spirit, is empty and trifling, but when he acts within and exerts his power, it is full of power. It is now clear in what way, according to this view, a godly mind is confirmed in faith by means of the sacraments, namely, in the same way in which the light of the sun is seen by the eye and the sound of the voice is heard by the ear. The eye would not be affected by the light at all unless it had a pupil on which the light might fall; nor would the ear be reached by any sound, however loud, if it were not naturally fitted for hearing. But if it is true, as has been explained, that in the eye it is the power of sight which enables it to see the light, and in the ear the power of hearing which enables it to perceive the voice, and that in our hearts it is the work of the Holy Spirit to begin, maintain, cherish, and establish faith, then two things follow: that the sacraments do not avail one iota without the power of the Holy Spirit; and that, nevertheless, in hearts already taught by that Teacher, there is nothing to prevent the sacraments from strengthening and increasing faith. There is only this difference: the faculty of seeing and hearing is naturally implanted in the eye and ear, whereas Christ acts in our minds above the measure of nature by special grace.

10¶

In this way we also dispose of certain objections by which some anxious minds are troubled. If we ascribe either an increase or a confirmation of faith to creatures, they say, injustice is done to the Spirit of God, who alone ought to be regarded as its author. But we do not rob him of the credit of confirming and increasing faith; on the contrary, we maintain that what confirms and increases faith is nothing else than his preparing of our minds by his inward illumination to receive the confirmation which is set forth by the sacraments. But if the subject is still obscure, it will be made plain by the following comparison. If you were to begin to persuade a person by word to do something, you would think of all the arguments by which he might be brought over to your view, and in a sense compelled to serve your purpose. But nothing is gained if the man himself does not have a clear and sharp judgment by which he can weigh the value of your arguments; if, moreover, he is not of a teachable disposition and ready to listen to instruction; if, finally, he does not have such an opinion of your good faith and prudence as in a sense to incline him in your favor and secure his assent. For there are many obstinate spirits who cannot be bent by any arguments; and where good faith is suspected or authority despised, little progress is made even with the teachable. On the other hand, when the opposite feelings exist, the result will be that the person whose interests you are consulting will agree to the very counsels which he would otherwise have mocked. The same work is performed in us by the Spirit. So that the word may not fall on our ear, or the sacraments be presented to our eye, in vain, he shows that it is God who speaks to us there, softens our hardened hearts, and shapes them to the obedience which is due to his word; in short, he carries those outward words and sacraments from the ear to the soul. Both word and sacraments, therefore, confirm our faith by setting before us the kind intentions of our heavenly Father, on the knowledge of which the whole assurance of our faith depends, and by which its strength is increased; and the Spirit also confirms our faith when, by engraving that assurance on our minds, he makes it effectual. Meanwhile, it is easy for the Father of lights, just as he illumines the bodily eye by the rays of the sun, to illumine our minds by the sacraments, as by a kind of intermediate brightness.

11¶

Our Lord showed that this property belongs to the outward word when, in the parable, he compared it to seed (Matthew 13:4; Luke 8:15). For as the seed, when it falls on a deserted and neglected part of the field, can do nothing but die, but when thrown into ground properly worked and cultivated will yield a hundredfold, so the word of God, when addressed to a stubborn spirit, will remain without fruit, as if thrown on barren waste ground, but when it meets with a soul which the hand of the heavenly Spirit has subdued, it will be most fruitful. But if the case of the seed and of the word is the same, and if from the seed grain can grow and increase and reach maturity, why may not faith also take its beginning, increase, and completion from the word? Both things are admirably explained by Paul in different passages. For when he wishes to remind the Corinthians how God had given effect to his labors, he boasts that he possessed the ministry of the Spirit (1 Corinthians 2:4), just as if his preaching were inseparably joined with the power of the Holy Spirit in inwardly enlightening and stirring the mind. But in another passage, when he wishes to remind them what the power of the word is in itself, when preached by man, he compares ministers to farmers who, after they have spent labor and diligence in cultivating the ground, have nothing more they can do. For what would plowing and sowing and watering avail unless what was sown should, by the kindness of Heaven, spring up? Therefore he concludes that he who plants and he who waters is nothing, but that the whole is to be ascribed to God, who alone gives the increase. The apostles, therefore, exert the power of the Spirit in their preaching, inasmuch as God uses them as instruments which he has ordained for the unfolding of his spiritual grace. Still, we must not lose sight of the distinction, but must remember what man is able to do of himself, and what belongs to God alone.

12¶

The sacraments are confirmations of our faith in such a sense that the Lord, when he sees fit to withdraw our assurance of the things he had promised in the sacraments, sometimes takes away the sacraments themselves. So when he deprives Adam of the gift of immortality and expels him from the garden, "lest he put forth his hand and take also of the tree of life, and live for ever" (Genesis 3:22), what is this we hear? Could that fruit have restored Adam to the immortality from which he had already fallen? By no means. It is just as if he had said: "Lest he indulge in vain confidence if allowed to keep the symbol of my promise, let that be withdrawn which might give him some hope of immortality." On this ground, when the apostle urges the Ephesians to remember that they "were without Christ, being aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of promise, having no hope, and without God in the world" (Ephesians 2:12), he says that they were not partakers of circumcision. He thus indicates by metonymy that all who had not received the badge of the promise were excluded from the promise. To the other objection, namely, that when so much power is attributed to creatures, the glory of God is given to them and so impaired, the obvious reply is that we attribute no power to the creatures. All we say is that God uses the means and instruments which he sees to be fitting, so that all things may serve his glory, since he is the Lord and disposer of all. Therefore, as he feeds our bodies by bread and other food, as he illumines the world by the sun and warms it by fire, and yet bread, sun, and fire are nothing except insofar as they are instruments through which he dispenses his blessings to us, so in the same way he spiritually nourishes our faith by means of the sacraments, whose only office is to make his promises visible to our eye, or rather, to be pledges of his promises. And with regard to the other creatures which the divine generosity and kindness has appointed for our use, and through which he bestows the gifts of his goodness on us, it is our duty to put no confidence in them, nor to admire and extol them as the causes of our mercies. So neither ought our confidence to be fixed on the sacraments, nor ought the glory of God to be transferred to them; but, passing beyond them all, our faith and confession should rise to Him who is the Author of the sacraments and of all things.

13¶

There is nothing in the argument which some build on the very term sacrament. This term, they say, while it has many meanings in approved authors, has only one which applies to signs, namely, when it is used for the formal oath which the soldier gives to his commander on entering the service. For as by that military oath recruits bind themselves to be faithful to their commander and make a profession of military service, so by our signs we acknowledge Christ as our commander and declare that we serve under his standard. They add comparisons to make the matter clearer. As the toga distinguished the Romans from the Greeks, who wore the pallium, and as the different orders of Romans were distinguished from each other by their particular insignia (for example, the senatorial from the equestrian by purple and crescent shoes, and the equestrian from the plebeian by a ring), so we wear our symbols to distinguish us from the profane. But it is sufficiently clear from what has been said above that the ancients, in giving the name of sacraments to signs, had paid no attention at all to the use of the term by Latin writers, but had, for the sake of convenience, given it this new meaning as a means of simply expressing sacred signs. But if we wished to argue more subtly, we might say that they seem to have given the term this meaning in a way analogous to that in which they use the term faith in the sense in which it is now used. For while faith is truthfulness in performing promises, they have used it for the certainty or firm persuasion which one has of the truth. In this way, while a sacrament is the act of the soldier when he vows obedience to his commander, they made it the act by which the commander admits soldiers to the ranks. For in the sacraments the Lord promises that he will be our God, and we promise that we will be his people. But we pass over such subtleties, since I think I have shown by arguments abundantly plain that all the ancient writers intended was to indicate that sacraments are the signs of sacred and spiritual things. The comparisons drawn from outward objects (chapter 15, section 1) we do indeed admit; but we do not approve that what is a secondary thing in the sacraments is made by them the first, and indeed the only, thing. The first thing is that they may contribute to our faith in God; the secondary, that they may attest our confession before men. These comparisons apply to the secondary reason. Let it therefore remain a fixed point that the mysteries would be cold (as has been seen) if they were not helps to our faith, and adjuncts joined to doctrine for the same end and purpose.

14¶

On the other hand, it must be observed that, as these objectors weaken the force and altogether overthrow the use of the sacraments, so there are others who ascribe to the sacraments a kind of secret power which is nowhere said to have been implanted in them by God. By this error the simpler and less wary are dangerously deceived, since they are taught to seek the gifts of God where they cannot possibly be found, and are gradually drawn away from God, so as to embrace mere vanity instead of his truth. For the schools of the Sophists have taught with general consent that the sacraments of the new law, in other words, those now in use in the Christian Church, justify and confer grace, provided only that we do not put in the way the obstacle of mortal sin. It is impossible to describe how deadly and pestilential this opinion is, all the more so because for many ages it has prevailed over a considerable part of the world, to the great loss of the Church. It is plainly of the devil. For, first, in promising a righteousness without faith, it drives souls headlong to destruction; second, in deriving a cause of righteousness from the sacraments, it entangles miserable minds, already of their own accord too much inclined to the earth, in a superstitious notion which makes them rest in the spectacle of a bodily object rather than in God himself. I wish we did not have such experience of both evils as to make it altogether unnecessary to give a lengthy proof of them. For what is a sacrament received without faith but the most certain destruction to the Church? For since nothing is to be expected beyond the promise, and the promise no less denounces wrath to the unbeliever than it offers grace to the believer, it is an error to suppose that anything more is conferred by the sacraments than is offered by the word of God and obtained by true faith. From this another thing follows, namely, that assurance of salvation does not depend on participation in the sacraments, as if justification consisted in it. Justification, which is stored up in Christ alone, we know to be communicated no less by the preaching of the Gospel than by the seal of the sacrament, and it may be completely enjoyed without this seal. So true is it, as Augustine declares, that there may be invisible sanctification without a visible sign, and, on the other hand, a visible sign without true sanctification (Augustine, Questions on the Old Testament, book 3). For, as he says elsewhere, "Men put on Christ, sometimes to the extent of partaking of the sacrament, and sometimes to the extent of holiness of life" (Augustine, On Baptism, Against the Donatists, chapter 24). The former may be common to the good and the bad; the latter belongs to the good alone.

15¶

Hence the distinction, if properly understood, which Augustine repeatedly makes between the sacrament and the matter of the sacrament. For he does not mean merely that the figure and the truth are contained in it, but that they do not so cling together that they cannot be separated, and that in this connection it is always necessary to distinguish the thing from the sign, so that we do not transfer to the one what belongs to the other. 123 D123 Sometimes this distinction is expressed in terms of the form of administration of the sacraments (the words of institution, the consecration of the element(s), and their application or distribution), on the one hand, and their spiritual significance and value, on the other. The grace of the sacraments does not lie in their fact or form, but in the Word received by faith.

16¶

If this is obscure because of its brevity, I will explain it at greater length. I say that Christ is the matter, or, if you prefer, the substance, of all the sacraments, since in him they have their whole solidity, and apart from him they promise nothing. Hence the error of Peter Lombard deserves all the less tolerance, since he expressly makes them causes of the righteousness and salvation of which they are parts (Sentences, book 4, distinction 1). Bidding farewell to all the other causes of righteousness which the wit of man devises, our duty is to hold to this one alone. Insofar, therefore, as we are helped by their instrumentality in cherishing, confirming, and increasing the true knowledge of Christ, so as both to possess him more fully and to enjoy him in all his richness, so far are they effectual for us. This is the case when what is offered there is received by us in true faith. You will ask, then: do the wicked, by their ingratitude, make the ordinance of God fruitless and void? I answer that what I have said is not to be understood as if the power and truth of the sacrament depended on the condition or pleasure of the one who receives it. What God instituted remains firm and keeps its nature, however men may vary. But since it is one thing to offer and another to receive, there is nothing to prevent a symbol consecrated by the word of the Lord from being truly what it is said to be, and from keeping its power, though it may at the same time confer no benefit on the wicked and ungodly. Augustine solves this question well in a few words: "If you receive carnally, it does not cease to be spiritual, but it is not spiritual to you" (Augustine, Homilies on John, 26). But as Augustine shows in the passages above that a sacrament is a thing of no value if separated from its truth, so also, when the two are joined, he reminds us that it is necessary to distinguish them, so that we may not cling too much to the outward sign. "As it is a servile weakness to follow the letter, and to take the signs for the thing signified, so to interpret the signs as of no use is an extravagant error" (Augustine, On Christian Doctrine, book 3, chapter 9). He mentions two faults which are to be avoided here. The one is when we receive the signs as if they had been given in vain, and, by maliciously destroying or weakening their hidden meanings, prevent them from yielding any fruit. The other is when, by not raising our minds beyond the visible sign, we attribute to it blessings which are conferred on us by Christ alone, and that by means of the Holy Spirit, who makes us partakers of Christ. Outward signs help if they invite us to Christ; but when they are twisted to any other purpose, their whole usefulness is overthrown.

17¶

Therefore let it be a fixed point that the office of the sacraments does not differ from that of the word of God; and this office is to hold out and offer Christ to us, and in him the treasures of heavenly grace. They confer nothing and avail nothing if they are not received in faith, just as wine or oil or any other liquid, however much of it you pour out, will run off and be lost unless there is an open vessel to receive it. When the vessel is not open, though it may be sprinkled all over, it will nevertheless remain entirely empty. We must beware of being led into a related error by the somewhat too extravagant terms which ancient Christian writers have used in extolling the dignity of the sacraments. We must not suppose that there is some hidden power inherent in the sacraments by which they, in themselves, confer the gifts of the Holy Spirit on us, in the same way that wine is drunk out of a cup. For the only office divinely assigned to them is to attest and ratify the goodwill of the Lord toward us; and they avail no further than they are accompanied by the Holy Spirit, to open our minds and hearts and make us capable of receiving this testimony, in which various distinguished graces are clearly displayed. For the sacraments, as we observed a little earlier (chapter 13, section 6; and chapter 14, sections 6, 7), are to us what messengers of good news are to men, or pledges in ratifying agreements. They do not of themselves bestow any grace, but they announce and display it, and, like pledges and badges, give a ratification of the gifts which the divine generosity has bestowed on us. The Holy Spirit, whom the sacraments do not bring indiscriminately to all, but whom the Lord confers specially on his own people, brings the gifts of God along with him, makes way for the sacraments, and causes them to bear fruit. But though we do not deny that God, by the immediate working of his Spirit, supports his own ordinance and prevents the administration of the sacraments he has instituted from being fruitless and vain, we still maintain that the inward grace of the Spirit, as distinct from the outward ministry, ought to be viewed and considered separately. God, therefore, truly performs whatever he promises and figures by signs; nor are the signs without effect, for they prove that he is their true and faithful author. The only question here is whether the Lord works by his own proper and intrinsic power (as it is called), or hands over his office to outward symbols. We maintain that whatever instruments he uses take nothing away from his primary working. In this doctrine of the sacraments, their dignity is highly extolled, their use plainly shown, their usefulness sufficiently proclaimed, and moderation duly maintained in all things, so that nothing is attributed to them which ought not to be attributed, and nothing denied them which they ought to have. Meanwhile, we get rid of that fiction by which the cause of justification and the power of the Holy Spirit are enclosed in elements as in vessels and vehicles, and the special power which was overlooked is distinctly explained. Here also we ought to observe that what the minister figures and attests by outward action, God performs inwardly, lest what God claims for himself alone should be ascribed to mortal man. Augustine is careful to observe this: "How do both God and Moses sanctify? Not Moses in place of God, but Moses by visible sacraments through his ministry, God by invisible grace through the Holy Spirit. In this lies the whole fruit of visible sacraments; for what do these visible sacraments avail without that sanctification of invisible grace?"

18¶

The term sacrament, in the sense in which we have so far considered it, includes in general all the signs which God ever commanded men to use, so that he might make them sure and confident of the truth of his promises. These he was pleased sometimes to place in natural objects, and sometimes to display in miracles. Of the first kind we have an example in his giving the tree of life to Adam and Eve as a pledge of immortality, so that they might feel confident of the promise as often as they ate of its fruit. Another example was when he gave the bow in the cloud to Noah and his descendants, as a memorial that he would not again destroy the earth by a flood. These were to Adam and Noah as sacraments: not that the tree could give Adam and Eve the immortality which it could not give to itself, or that the bow (which is only a reflection of the sun's rays on the clouds opposite) could have the effect of holding back the waters; but they had a mark engraved on them by the word of God, so as to be proofs and seals of his covenant. The tree was a tree before, and the bow a bow; but when they were inscribed with the word of God, a new form was given to them: they began to be what they were not before. Lest anyone suppose that these things were said in vain, the bow is even today a witness to us of the covenant which God made with Noah (Calvin on Genesis 9:6). As often as we look at it, we read in it this promise from God, that the earth will never be destroyed by a flood. Therefore, if some petty philosopher, to mock the simplicity of our faith, contends that the variety of colors arises naturally from the rays reflected by the opposite cloud, let us admit the fact; but at the same time let us laugh at his stupidity in not recognizing God as the Lord and governor of nature, who at his pleasure makes all the elements serve his glory. If he had impressed memorials of this kind on the sun, the stars, the earth, and stones, they would all have been to us as sacraments. For why are unshaped silver and coined silver not of the same value, since they are the same metal? Simply because the former has nothing but its own nature, whereas the latter, stamped with the public mark, becomes money and receives a new value. And shall the Lord not be able to stamp his creatures with his word, so that things which were formerly bare elements may become sacraments? Examples of the second kind were given when he showed Abraham light in the smoking furnace (Genesis 15:17); when he covered the fleece with dew while the ground was dry, and, on the other hand, when the dew covered the ground while the fleece was untouched, to assure Gideon of victory (Judges 6:37); and also when he made the shadow go back ten degrees on the dial, to assure Hezekiah of his recovery (2 Kings 20:9; Isaiah 38:7). These things, which were done to help and establish their faith, were also sacraments.

19¶

But my present purpose is to speak especially of those sacraments which the Lord has been pleased to institute as ordinary sacraments in his Church, to bring up his worshipers and servants in one faith and in the confession of one faith. For, to use the words of Augustine, "In no name of religion, true or false, can men be assembled unless they are united by some common use of visible signs or sacraments" (Augustine, Against Faustus, book 9, chapter 11). Our most merciful Father, foreseeing this need, appointed certain exercises of piety for his servants from the very first; and these Satan afterward corrupted and depraved in many ways by transferring them to ungodly and superstitious worship. Hence came those initiations of the Gentiles into their mysteries, and other degenerate rites. Yet although they were full of error and superstition, they were at the same time an indication that men could not be without such outward signs of religion. But since they were neither founded on the word of God nor had reference to that truth which ought to be held out by all signs, they are unworthy of being named when mention is made of the sacred symbols which were instituted by God and have not been perverted from their end, namely, to be helps to true piety. And these consist not of simple signs, like the rainbow and the tree of life, but of ceremonies; or (if you prefer) the signs used here are ceremonies. But since, as has been said above, they are testimonies of grace and salvation from the Lord, so with regard to us they are marks of profession by which we openly swear by the name of God, binding ourselves to be faithful to him. Hence Chrysostom somewhere shrewdly calls them pacts, by which God enters into covenant with us and we become bound to holiness and purity of life, because a mutual agreement is here made between God and us. For as God there promises to cover and blot out any guilt and penalty which we may have incurred by transgression, and reconciles us to himself in his only begotten Son, so we in our turn bind ourselves by this profession to the pursuit of piety and righteousness. And hence it may justly be said that such sacraments are ceremonies by which God is pleased to train his people: first, to stir up, cherish, and strengthen faith within; and second, to testify our religion to men.

20¶

Now these have been different at different times, according to the dispensation which the Lord has seen fit to use in making himself known to men. Circumcision was commanded to Abraham and his descendants, and to it were afterward added purifications and sacrifices and other rites of the Mosaic Law. These were the sacraments of the Jews until the coming of Christ. After these were abolished, the two sacraments of Baptism and the Lord's Supper, which the Christian Church now uses, were instituted. I speak of those which were instituted for the use of the whole Church. For the laying on of hands, by which the ministers of the Church are admitted to their office, though I have no objection to its being called a sacrament, I do not number among the ordinary sacraments. What place is to be given to the other things commonly reputed to be sacraments we shall see shortly. Still, the ancient sacraments had the same end in view as our own, namely, to direct and almost lead us by the hand to Christ; or rather, they were like images to represent him and set him forth to our knowledge. But as we have already shown that sacraments are a kind of seals of the promises of God, so let us hold it as a most certain truth that no divine promise has ever been offered to man except in Christ, and that therefore, when they remind us of any divine promise, they must of necessity display Christ. Hence that heavenly pattern of the tabernacle and of the worship of the Law which was shown to Moses on the mount. There is only this difference: the former foreshadowed a promised Christ while he was still awaited, whereas the latter bear witness to him as already come and made manifest.

21¶

When these things are explained one by one, they will be much clearer. Circumcision was a sign by which the Jews were reminded that whatever comes from the seed of man (in other words, the whole nature of man) is corrupt and needs to be cut off. Moreover, it was a proof and memorial to confirm them in the promise made to Abraham of a seed in whom all the nations of the earth should be blessed, and from whom they themselves were to look for a blessing. That saving seed, as Paul teaches us (Galatians 5:16), was Christ, in whom alone they trusted to recover what they had lost in Adam. Therefore circumcision was to them what Paul says it was to Abraham, namely, a sign of the righteousness of faith (Romans 9:11); that is, a seal by which they were more surely assured that their faith in waiting for the Lord would be accepted by God for righteousness. But we shall have a better opportunity elsewhere (chapter 16, sections 3, 4) to pursue the comparison between circumcision and baptism. Hebrews 9:1-14; 1 John 1:7; Revelation 1:5; Hebrews 4:14; 5:5; 9:11.

22¶

As for our sacraments, they present Christ to us all the more clearly, the more intimately he has been made known to man ever since he was truly displayed by the Father, as he had been promised. For Baptism testifies that we are washed and purified; the Supper of the Eucharist, that we are redeemed. Cleansing is figured by water, satisfaction by blood. Both are found in Christ, who, as John says, "came by water and blood," that is, to purify and to redeem. Of this the Spirit of God is also a witness. Indeed, there are three witnesses in one: water, Spirit, and blood. In the water and blood we have evidence of purification and redemption, but the Spirit is the primary witness, who gives us full assurance of this testimony. This sublime mystery was gloriously displayed on the cross of Christ, when water and blood flowed from his sacred side (John 19:34); and for this reason Augustine rightly called it the fountain of our sacraments (Augustine, Homilies on John, 26). Of these we shall soon treat at greater length. There is no doubt that, if you compare one time with another, the grace of the Spirit is now more abundantly displayed. For this forms part of the glory of the kingdom of Christ, as we gather from several passages, and especially from the seventh chapter of John. In this sense we are to understand the words of Paul, that the law was "a shadow of good things to come, but the body is of Christ" (Colossians 2:17). His purpose is not to declare the ineffectiveness of those manifestations of grace in which God was pleased to prove his truth to the patriarchs, just as he proves it to us today in Baptism and the Lord's Supper, but to contrast the two and show the great value of what is given to us, so that no one may think it strange that the ceremonies of the law have been abolished by the coming of Christ.

23¶

The Scholastic dogma (to touch on it in passing), by which the difference between the sacraments of the old and the new dispensation is made so great that the former did nothing but foreshadow the grace of God while the latter actually confer it, must be completely rejected. Since the apostle speaks in no higher terms of the one than of the other, when he says that the fathers ate the same spiritual food, and explains that this food was Christ (1 Corinthians 10:3), who will dare to regard as an empty sign that which gave the Jews a manifestation of true communion with Christ? And the circumstances of the case which the apostle is treating there argue strongly for our view. For to guard against our trusting in a cold knowledge of Christ, an empty title of Christianity, and outward observances, and so daring to despise the judgment of God, he sets forth striking examples of divine severity in the Jews, to make us aware that if we indulge in the same vices, the same punishments which they suffered hang over us. Now, to make the comparison fitting, it was necessary to show that there is no inequality between us and them in those blessings in which he forbade us to glory. Therefore he first makes them equal to us in the sacraments, and leaves us not one iota of privilege which could give us hope of escaping punishment. Nor can we rightly attribute more to our baptism than he elsewhere attributes to circumcision, when he calls it a seal of the righteousness of faith (Romans 4:11). Whatever, therefore, is now displayed to us in the sacraments, the Jews formerly received in theirs, namely, Christ with his spiritual riches. The same efficacy which ours have, they experienced in theirs, namely, that they were seals of the divine favor toward them with regard to the hope of eternal salvation. Had the objectors been sound interpreters of the Epistle to the Hebrews, they would not have been so deluded. But reading there that sins were not expiated by the ceremonies of the law, and indeed that the ancient shadows were of no importance for justification, they overlooked the contrast which is drawn there; and, fastening on the single point that the law in itself was of no avail to the worshiper, they thought that the ceremonies were mere figures, empty of truth. The purpose of the apostle is to show that there is nothing in the ceremonial law until we come to Christ, on whom alone the whole efficacy depends.

24¶

But they will build their case on what Paul says of the circumcision of the letter, Romans 2:25-29; 1 Corinthians 7:19; Galatians 6:15; 1 Corinthians 10:5; 1 Peter 3:21; Colossians 2:11.

In Hebrews 9 and 10 it may at first glance appear that the writer intends to draw a contrast between those sacrifices offered under the law which were never able to take away so much as a single sin, and the one sacrifice offered by Christ which is able to take away all sins. Such a contrast, however, raises certain questions. For example, what would have been the value of the atonement which the high priest was to make each year when, in the holy of holies, he offered blood for his own sins and for the sins of the people? Again, why did Moses sprinkle blood on the book, the people, the tabernacle, and all the vessels of the tabernacle, in order (as Hebrews 9:19-23 states) to purge and purify them, if the blood of calves and lambs and goats cannot take away a single sin? And how could David have written, "Blessed is the man whose sins are forgiven" (a blessedness applicable, according to Paul in Romans 4:6-8, not only to David but also to New Testament believers), if by the shedding of blood during the Old Testament economy there was no remission (forgiveness) of sins? The objection may be raised: but then what does the writer of Hebrews mean when he says (in 10:4) that "it is not possible that the blood of bulls and of goats should take away sins"? And how are we to understand the assertion (in 10:11) that "every priest standeth daily ministering and offering oftentimes the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins"? Two things appear clear: (1) that the writer of Hebrews does not mean that the Old Testament sacrifices commanded by God were valueless or worthless; (2) that our interpretation must be compared to, be in proportion to, and be related to the analogy of faith (the teaching of Scripture as a whole). Perhaps a workable solution to this problem can be found in two important distinctions: that between temporary and permanent value, and that between extrinsic efficacy. As we attempt to compare and contrast the sacrifices of the Old Testament with the sacrifice of Himself by Christ, we discover that the emphasis in Hebrews 9 and 10, with respect to the Old Testament sacrifices, is on their temporary value (because they were repeated again and again) and their extrinsic efficacy (because they were not intended to point to themselves, but to the atoning sacrifice of Christ which gave efficacy to them). And we discover that the emphasis in those chapters, with respect to the sacrifice of Christ, is on its permanent value (because it was completed once for all by the eternal Word made flesh) and its intrinsic efficacy (because it was and is a perfect and complete satisfaction). The temporary value and extrinsic efficacy of the sacrifices of the Old Testament is borne out by the terms used to express them in these two chapters of Hebrews. They are called signs, or significations (9:8), figures, or types (9:9, 24), patterns (9:23), and shadows (10:1). They could make the believing worshiper perfect "in the sense of final completeness," since He offered one sacrifice and then sat down, never needing to offer again. But this should not be understood to mean that the sacrifices of the Old Testament had no value and no efficacy with respect to the forgiveness of sins. If they were signs, they pointed to that which they signified; if they were figures or types, they anticipated their antitype; if they were patterns, they were patterns of the true reality; and if they were shadows, they outlined the substance. These, then, would appear to be the contrasts drawn in Hebrews 9 and 10. Impermanence and lack of self-sufficiency characterize the sacrifices of the Old Testament; permanence and self-sufficiency characterize the Sacrifice of the New. The Old Testament sacrifices of lambs were efficacious, but not of themselves and not without repetition; the New Testament sacrifice of the Lamb of God was efficacious of itself, gave value and efficacy to the Old Testament sacrifices, and is perfect and complete forever.

26¶

Those wretched sophists are perhaps deceived by the extravagant praises of our signs which occur in ancient writers, for instance this passage of Augustine: "The sacraments of the old law only promised a Savior, whereas ours give salvation" (Augustine, Preface on Psalm 73). Not perceiving that these and similar figures of speech are hyperbolical, they too have published their hyperbolical dogmas, but in a sense altogether foreign to that of the ancient writers. For Augustine means nothing more than he does in another place, where he says, "The sacraments of the Mosaic law foretold Christ; ours announce him" (Questions on Numbers, chapter 33). And again, "Those were promises of things to be fulfilled; these are indications of the fulfillment" (Against Faustus, book 19, chapter 14); as if he had said, "Those figured him when he was still awaited; ours, now that he has come, display him as present." Moreover, with regard to the manner of signifying, he says, as he also indicates elsewhere, "The Law and the Prophets had sacraments foretelling a thing to come; the sacraments of our time attest that what they foretold as to come has come" (Against the Letters of Petilian, book 2, chapter 37). His views concerning their reality and efficacy he explains in several passages, as when he says, "The sacraments of the Jews were different in the signs, alike in the things signified; different in the visible appearance, alike in spiritual power" (Homilies on John, 26). Again, "In different signs there was the same faith. It was so in different signs as in different words, because words change their sound according to the times, and yet words are nothing else than signs. The fathers drank of the same spiritual drink, but not of the same bodily drink. See, then, how, while faith remains, signs vary. There the rock was Christ; for us, that is Christ which is placed on the altar. They, as a great sacrament, drank of the water flowing from the rock; believers know what we drink. If you look at the visible appearance, there was a difference; if at the intelligible meaning, they drank of the same spiritual drink." Again, "In this mystery their food and drink are the same as ours; the same in meaning, not in form, for the same Christ was figured to them in the rock; to us he has been manifested in the flesh" (on Psalm 77). Yet we grant that in this respect also there is some difference. Both testify that the fatherly kindness of God and the graces of the Spirit are offered to us in Christ, but ours more clearly and splendidly. In both there is a displaying of Christ, but in ours it is fuller and more complete, in keeping with that distinction between the Old and New Testaments which we have discussed above. And this is the meaning of Augustine (whom we quote more frequently, as the best and most faithful witness of all antiquity) when he says that after Christ was revealed, sacraments were instituted that were fewer in number, but of more majestic significance and more excellent power (On Christian Doctrine, book 3; and Letter to Januarius). A sacrament, Calvin teaches, is an external sign by which God seals his promises upon our weak faith—never a vessel of secret power working apart from the Word, never a bare token emptied of grace, but a visible Word that holds Christ before our eyes and is made effectual only by the Spirit who opens the heart to receive it.
