Of the Sacraments

This chapter consists of two principal parts,—I. Of sacraments in general. The sum of the doctrine stated, sec. 1-6. Two classes of opponents to be guarded against—viz. those who undervalue the power of the sacraments, sec. 7-13; and those who attribute too much to the sacraments, sec. 14-17. II. Of the sacraments in particular, both of the Old and the New Testament. Their scope and meaning. Refutation of those who have either too high or too low ideas of the sacraments. 1. Of the sacraments in general. A sacrament defined. 2. Meaning of the word sacrament. 3. Definition explained. Why God seals his promises to us by sacraments. 4. The word which ought to accompany the element, that the sacrament may be complete. 5. Error of those who attempt to separate the word, or promise of God, from the element. 6. Why sacraments are called Signs of the Covenant. 7. They are such signs, though the wicked should receive them, but are signs of grace only to believers. 8. Objections to this view answered. 9. No secret virtue in the sacraments. Their whole efficacy depends on the inward operation of the Spirit. 10. Objections answered. Illustrated by a simile. 11. Of the increase of faith by the preaching of the word. 12. In what way, and how far, the sacraments are confirmations of our faith. 13. Some regard the sacraments as mere signs. This view refuted. 14. Some again attribute too much to the sacraments. Refutation. 15. Refutation confirmed by a passage from Augustine. 16. Previous views more fully explained. 17. The matter of the sacrament always present when the sacrament is duly administered. 18. Extensive meaning of the term sacrament. 19. The ordinary sacraments in the Church. How necessary they are. 20. The sacraments of the Old and of the New Testament. The end of both the same —viz. to lead us to Christ. 21. This apparent in the sacraments of the Old Testament. 22. Apparent also in the sacraments of the New Testament. 23. Impious doctrine of the Schoolmen as to the difference between the Old and the New Testaments. 24. Scholastic objection answered. 25. Another objection answered. 26. Sacraments of the New Testament sometimes excessively extolled by early Theologians. Their meaning explained.

1¶

Alongside the preaching of the gospel, we have another help to our faith in the sacraments. It greatly concerns us that some sure teaching about them be given, instructing us both in the purpose for which they were instituted and in their present use. First, we must consider what a sacrament is. It seems to me a simple and fitting definition to say that it is an outward sign by which the Lord seals upon our consciences his promises of goodwill toward us, in order to support the weakness of our faith; and by which we, in turn, testify our devotion to him, both before himself and before angels as well as men. We may also define it more briefly by calling it a testimony of God's favor toward us, confirmed by an outward sign, with a corresponding witness of our faith toward him. You may take your choice of these definitions, which in meaning do not differ from Augustine's, who defines a sacrament as a visible sign of a sacred thing, or a visible form of an invisible grace. Yet his definition gives no better or surer explanation. Since its brevity makes it somewhat obscure, and so misleads the less learned, I wished to remove all doubt and make the definition fuller by stating it at greater length.

2¶

The reason the ancients used the term in this sense is not hard to see. Whenever the old translator wished to render the Greek word mysterion into Latin, especially when it was used with reference to divine things, he used the word sacramentum. So in Ephesians: "Having made known unto us the mystery (sacramentum) of his will"; and again, "If ye have heard of the dispensation of the grace of God, which is given me to you-ward, how that by revelation he made known unto me the mystery" (sacramentum) (Eph. 1:9; 3:2). In Colossians: "Even the mystery which hath been hid from ages and from generations, but is now made manifest to his saints, to whom God would make known what is the riches of the glory of this mystery" (sacramentum) (Col. 1:26). Also in the First Epistle to Timothy: "Without controversy, great is the mystery (sacramentum) of godliness: God was manifest in the flesh" (1 Tim. 3:16). He was unwilling to use the word arcanum (secret), lest the word should seem beneath the greatness of the thing meant. So when the thing was sacred and secret, he used the term sacramentum. In this sense it occurs often in church writers. And it is well known that what the Latins call sacramenta, the Greeks call mysteria (mysteries). The sameness of meaning removes all dispute. This is why the term was applied to those signs which gave a majestic representation of things spiritual and sublime. Augustine notes this too: "It would be tedious to discourse of the variety of signs; those which relate to divine things are called sacraments" (Augustine, Letter 5, to Marcellinus).

Scripture cited
Eph 1:9 · Col 1:26 · 1 Tim 3:16 · Ep 5
3¶

From the definition we have given, we see that there is never a sacrament without a promise going before it. The sacrament is added as a kind of appendix, in order to confirm and seal the promise, and to give it a better attestation, or rather, in a way, to ratify it. In this way God provides, first, for our ignorance and dullness, and second, for our weakness. And yet, properly speaking, the sacrament does not so much confirm his Word as establish us in faith in it. That is, the sacrament cannot make the promise of God more certain in itself, but it can make our faith in God's promise more certain in us. God's Word is always absolute, strong, unchangeable, and "settled in heaven"; but our faith, throughout this life, is always relative, weak, changeable, and frequently in need of confirmation and assurance. So we rightly distinguish between the objective certainty of God's Word and the subjective certainty of our faith.

4¶

This is commonly expressed by saying that a sacrament consists of the Word and the outward sign. By the Word we should understand not one that is muttered without meaning and without faith, so that by its mere sound, as by a magical incantation, it has the power of consecrating the element; but one that, when preached, makes us understand what the visible sign means. So the practice that was so often followed under the tyranny of the pope was not free from gross profaning of the mystery, for they thought it enough if the priest muttered the formula of consecration while the people looked on stupidly, understanding nothing. Indeed, this was done for the express purpose of keeping any instruction from reaching the people, for it was all said in Latin to unlearned hearers. Afterward superstition was carried so far that the consecration was thought not to be properly performed unless in a low mumble that few could hear. Very different is Augustine's teaching about the sacramental word: "Let the word be added to the element, and it will become a sacrament. For how can there be so much power in water as to touch the body and cleanse the heart, unless by the agency of the word, and this not because it is spoken, but because it is believed? For even in the word the passing sound is one thing, the abiding power another. This is the word of faith which we preach, says the Apostle" (Rom. 10:8). So in the Acts of the Apostles we have the expression, "Purifying their hearts by faith" (Acts 15:9). And the Apostle Peter says, "The like figure whereunto even baptism doth also now save us (not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience)" (1 Pet. 3:21). "This is the word of faith which we preach, by which word, no doubt, baptism also, in order that it may be able to cleanse, is consecrated" (Augustine, Homily on John 13). You see how he requires preaching to produce faith. We need not labor to prove this, since there is not the least room for doubt about what Christ did and commanded us to do, what the apostles followed, and what a purer Church observed. Indeed, it is known that from the very beginning of the world, whenever God offered any sign to the holy patriarchs, it was inseparably joined to teaching, without which our senses would gaze bewildered at a meaningless object. Therefore, when we hear mention made of the sacramental word, let us understand the promise which, proclaimed aloud by the minister, leads the people by the hand to that to which the sign points and directs us.

Rom 10:8 · Acts 15:9 · 1 Pet 3:21
5¶

Nor should we listen to those who oppose this view with a dilemma that is more clever than sound. They argue this way: We either know that the Word of God which comes before the sacrament is the true will of God, or we do not. If we know it, we learn nothing new from the sacrament that follows. If we do not know it, we cannot learn it from the sacrament, whose whole power depends on the Word. Our brief reply is this: The seals affixed to charters and other public documents are nothing considered by themselves, and would be affixed to no purpose if nothing were written on the parchment; yet this does not keep them from sealing and confirming when they are joined to writings. It cannot be objected that this comparison is a recent invention of our own, since Paul himself used it, calling circumcision a seal (Rom. 4:11), where he expressly maintains that Abraham's circumcision was not for justification, but was a testimony to the covenant by faith in which he had already been justified. And how, I ask, can anyone be greatly offended when we teach that the promise is sealed by the sacrament, since it is plain from the promises themselves that one promise confirms another? The clearer any evidence is, the better suited it is to support our faith. But the sacraments bring with them the clearest promises, and, compared with the Word, they have this peculiar feature: that they represent the promises to the life, as if painted in a picture. Nor should we be moved by an objection drawn from the difference between sacraments and the seals of documents, namely, that since both consist of the earthly elements of this world, the former cannot be sufficient or adequate to seal the promises of God, which are spiritual and eternal, though the latter may serve to seal the decrees of princes about passing and perishable things. But the believer, when the sacraments are set before his eyes, does not stop short at the outward spectacle, but by the steps of analogy I have described, rises with reverent reflection to the sublime mysteries that lie hidden in the sacraments.

Rom 4:11
6¶

As the Lord calls his promises covenants (Gen. 6:18; 9:9; 17:2), and the sacraments signs of the covenants, so something similar may be drawn from human covenants. What could the slaughter of a hog accomplish, unless words were added, or rather went before it? Swine are often killed without any inner or hidden meaning. What could be gained by pledging the right hand, since hands are not seldom joined in going into battle? But when words have gone before, then by such symbols of covenant a binding force is given to laws, though these were already conceived, framed, and enacted by words. The sacraments, therefore, are exercises that confirm our faith in the Word of God; and because we are fleshly, they are set forth under fleshly objects, that they may train us in keeping with our sluggish capacity, just as nurses lead children by the hand. And so Augustine calls a sacrament a visible word (Augustine, Homily on John 89), because it represents the promises of God as in a picture, and sets them before our eyes in a graphic, bodily form (Augustine, Against Faustus, bk. 19). We might point to other comparisons that designate sacraments still more plainly, as when they are called the pillars of our faith. For just as a building stands and leans on its foundation, and yet is made more stable when supported by pillars, so faith leans on the Word of God as its proper foundation, and yet, when sacraments are added, leans more firmly, as if resting on pillars. Or we may call them mirrors, in which we may behold the riches of grace that God bestows on us. For then, as has been said, he reveals himself to us as far as our dullness can recognize him, and testifies his love and kindness to us more expressly than by word.

Gen 6:18
7¶

It is unreasonable to argue that sacraments are not manifestations of divine grace toward us merely because they are held out to the ungodly as well, who, far from finding God more favorable to them, only incur greater condemnation. By the same reasoning, the gospel would be no manifestation of the grace of God, because it is scorned by many who hear it; nor would Christ himself be a manifestation of grace, because of the many who saw and knew him, very few received him. Something similar may be seen in public laws. A great part of the people mock and evade the authenticating seal, though they know it was used by their sovereign to confirm his will; others trample it underfoot, as a matter in no way concerning them; while others even curse it. So, since the case of the two things is alike, the fitness of the comparison I made above ought to be more readily granted. It is certain, then, that the Lord offers us his mercy, and a pledge of his grace, both in his sacred Word and in the sacraments; but it is not grasped except by those who receive the Word and the sacraments with firm faith, just as Christ, though offered and held out for salvation to all, is nevertheless not acknowledged and received by all. Augustine, meaning to convey this, said that the power of the Word is produced in the sacrament not because it is spoken, but because it is believed. Hence Paul, addressing believers, includes communion with Christ in the sacraments, as when he says, "As many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ" (Gal. 3:27). Again, "For by one Spirit we are all baptized into one body" (1 Cor. 12:13). But when he speaks of a wrongful use of the sacraments, he attributes nothing more to them than to cold, empty figures, thereby indicating that however the ungodly and hypocrites may, by their perversity, suppress, obscure, or hinder the effect of divine grace in the sacraments, this does not keep the sacraments, wherever and whenever God so pleases, from giving a true evidence of communion with Christ, nor keep them from setting forth, and the Spirit of God from performing, the very thing they promise. We conclude, then, that the sacraments are rightly called evidences of divine grace, and, as it were, seals of the goodwill he holds toward us. By sealing it to us, they sustain, nourish, confirm, and increase our faith. The objections usually raised against this view are trivial and weak. They say that our faith, if it is good, cannot be made better, for there is no faith except that which leans unshakably, firmly, and without wavering on the mercy of God. It would have been better for these objectors to pray with the apostles, "Lord, increase our faith" (Luke 17:5), than confidently to insist on a perfection of faith which none of the sons of men ever attained, and none ever shall attain, in this life. Let them explain what kind of faith his was who said, "Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief" (Mark 9:24). That faith, though only begun, was good, and might, by the removal of the unbelief, be made better. But there is no better argument to refute them than their own conscience. For if they confess themselves sinners (and this, whether they will or not, they cannot deny), then they must of necessity charge this very quality to the imperfection of their faith.

Scripture cited
Gal 3:27 · 1 Cor 12:13 · Luke 17:5 · Mark 9:24
8¶

But Philip, they say, replied to the eunuch who asked to be baptized, "If thou believest with all thine heart thou mayest" (Acts 8:37). What room is there for confirming baptism when faith fills the whole heart? I, in my turn, ask them: Do they not feel that a good part of their heart is empty of faith? Do they not perceive new additions to it every day? There was a man who boasted that he grew old while still learning. Three times miserable, then, are we Christians if we grow old without making progress, we whose faith ought to advance through every period of life until it grows up into a perfect man (Eph. 4:13). In this passage, therefore, to believe with the whole heart is not to believe Christ perfectly, but only to embrace him sincerely with heart and soul; not to be filled with him, but with ardent affection to hunger and thirst and long after him. It is usual in Scripture to say that a thing is done with the whole heart when it is done sincerely and from the heart. Of this kind are the following passages: "With my whole heart have I sought thee" (Ps. 119:10); "I will praise thee with my whole heart," and so on. In the same way, when the deceitful and double-dealing are rebuked, it is said, "With flattering lips and with a double heart do they speak" (Ps. 12:2). The objectors next add: "If faith is increased by means of the sacraments, the Holy Spirit is given in vain, since it is his office to begin, sustain, and complete our faith." I admit, indeed, that faith is the proper and entire work of the Holy Spirit, enlightened by whom we come to know God and the treasures of his grace, and without whose illumination our mind is so blind that it can see nothing, so dull that it has no taste for spiritual things. But for the one divine blessing they proclaim, we count three. For, first, the Lord teaches and trains us by his Word; next, he confirms us by his sacraments; lastly, he illumines our mind by the light of his Holy Spirit, and opens an entrance into our hearts for his Word and sacraments, which would otherwise only strike our ears and meet our eyes, but in no way move us inwardly.

Scripture cited
Acts 8:37 · Eph 4:13 · Ps 119:10 · Ps 12:2
9¶

Therefore, regarding the increase and confirmation of faith, I would remind the reader (though I think I have already said it in plain terms) that in assigning this office to the sacraments, it is not as though I thought there is some secret power forever inherent in them, by which they can of themselves promote or strengthen faith; but because our Lord has instituted them for the express purpose of helping to establish and increase our faith. The sacraments properly perform their office only when accompanied by the Spirit, the inward Master, whose power alone penetrates the heart, stirs up the affections, and opens a way for the sacraments into our souls. If he is absent, the sacraments can avail us no more than the sun shining on the eyeballs of the blind, or sounds uttered in the ears of the deaf. So, in dividing between the Spirit and the sacraments, I ascribe the whole power to him, and leave only a ministry to them. This ministry, without the working of the Spirit, is empty and worthless, but when he acts within and exerts his power, it is full of power. It is now clear in what way, according to this view, a devout mind is confirmed in faith by means of the sacraments: namely, in the same way that the light of the sun is seen by the eye, and the sound of the voice heard by the ear. The eye would not be affected at all by the light unless it had a pupil on which the light might fall; nor would the ear be reached by any sound, however loud, were it not naturally fitted for hearing. But if it is true, as has been explained, that in the eye it is the power of sight that enables it to see the light, and in the ear the power of hearing that enables it to perceive the voice, and that in our hearts it is the work of the Holy Spirit to begin, maintain, cherish, and establish faith, then it follows both that the sacraments avail nothing without the power of the Holy Spirit, and that yet, in hearts already taught by that teacher, there is nothing to keep the sacraments from strengthening and increasing faith. There is only this difference: the faculty of seeing and hearing is naturally implanted in the eye and ear, whereas Christ acts in our minds above the measure of nature, by special grace.

10¶

In this way, too, we dispose of certain objections by which some anxious minds are troubled. If we ascribe either an increase or a confirmation of faith to creatures, they say, injustice is done to the Spirit of God, who alone ought to be regarded as its author. But we do not rob him of the credit of confirming and increasing faith; rather, we maintain that what confirms and increases faith is nothing else than the preparing of our minds by his inward illumination to receive that confirmation which is set forth by the sacraments. But if the subject is still obscure, it will be made plain by the following comparison. Suppose you set out to persuade someone by words to do something. You would think of all the arguments by which he might be brought over to your view, and in a manner compelled to serve your purpose. But nothing is gained if the person himself does not have a clear and keen judgment, by which he can weigh the value of your arguments; if, moreover, he is not of a teachable disposition, ready to listen to instruction; if, finally, he does not have such an opinion of your trustworthiness and good sense as in some way to incline him in your favor and secure his agreement. For there are many stubborn spirits who cannot be bent by any arguments; and where the speaker's good faith is suspected, or his authority despised, little progress is made even with the teachable. On the other hand, when the opposite feelings exist, the result will be that the person whose interests you are seeking will yield to the very counsels he would otherwise have mocked. The same work is done in us by the Spirit. So that the Word may not fall upon our ear, or the sacraments meet our eye, in vain, he shows that it is God who there speaks to us, softens our stubborn hearts, and shapes them to the obedience due to his Word; in short, he carries those outward words and sacraments from the ear to the soul. So both Word and sacraments confirm our faith by setting before us the kind intentions of our heavenly Father, in the knowledge of which the whole assurance of our faith depends, and by which its strength is increased. And the Spirit also confirms our faith when, by engraving that assurance on our minds, he makes it effectual. Meanwhile, it is easy for the Father of lights, just as he illumines the bodily eye by the rays of the sun, to illumine our minds by the sacraments, as by a kind of intermediate brightness.

11¶

Our Lord showed that this property belongs to the outward Word when, in the parable, he compared it to seed (Matt. 13:4; Luke 8:15). For just as the seed, when it falls on a deserted and neglected part of the field, can do nothing but die, yet when cast into ground properly worked and cultivated will yield a hundredfold, so the Word of God, when addressed to a stubborn spirit, will remain without fruit, as if thrown upon barren waste; but when it meets a soul that the hand of the heavenly Spirit has subdued, it will be most fruitful. But if the case of the seed and of the Word is the same, and from the seed corn can sprout, grow, and come to maturity, why may not faith also have its beginning, increase, and completion from the Word? Both things are admirably explained by Paul in different passages. For when he would remind the Corinthians how God had given effect to his labors, he boasts that he possessed the ministry of the Spirit (1 Cor. 2:4), just as if his preaching were inseparably bound up with the power of the Holy Spirit, in inwardly enlightening the mind and stirring it. But in another passage, when he would remind them what the power of the Word is in itself, when preached by man, he compares ministers to farmers, who, after they have spent labor and effort in cultivating the ground, have nothing more they can do. For what would plowing, sowing, and watering accomplish, unless what was sown should, by the kindness of heaven, spring up? So he concludes that he who plants and he who waters is nothing, but the whole is to be ascribed to God, who alone gives the increase. The apostles, therefore, exert the power of the Spirit in their preaching, inasmuch as God uses them as instruments he has appointed for unfolding his spiritual grace. Still, we must not lose sight of the distinction, but remember what man is able of himself to do, and what is proper to God alone.

Scripture cited
Mt 13:4 · Luke 8:15 · 1 Cor 2:4
12¶

The sacraments are confirmations of our faith in such a sense that the Lord, when he sees fit to withdraw our assurance of the things he had promised in the sacraments, sometimes takes away the sacraments themselves. When he deprives Adam of the gift of immortality and expels him from the garden, it is "lest he put forth his hand and take also of the tree of life, and live for ever" (Gen. 3:22). What is this we hear? Could that fruit have restored Adam to the immortality from which he had already fallen? By no means. It is just as if he had said: Lest he indulge in vain confidence, if he is allowed to keep the symbol of my promise, let that be withdrawn which might give him some hope of immortality. On this ground, when the apostle urges the Ephesians to remember that they "were without Christ, being aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of promise, having no hope, and without God in the world" (Eph. 2:12), he says they were not partakers of circumcision. By this he indicates, by a figure of speech, that all were shut out from the promise who had not received the badge of the promise. To the other objection, namely, that when so much power is attributed to creatures, the glory of God is given to them and thereby diminished, it is easy to reply that we attribute no power to the creatures. All we say is that God uses the means and instruments he sees to be fitting, so that all things may serve his glory, since he is the Lord and disposer of all. Therefore, just as by bread and other food he feeds our bodies, as by the sun he gives light and by fire gives warmth to the world, and yet bread, sun, and fire are nothing except insofar as they are instruments through which he dispenses his blessings to us, so in the same way he spiritually nourishes our faith by means of the sacraments, whose only office is to make his promises visible to our eye, or rather, to be pledges of his promises. And just as it is our duty, with regard to the other creatures which God's generosity and kindness have destined for our use, and by whose service he bestows the gifts of his goodness upon us, to put no confidence in them, nor to admire and exalt them as the causes of our blessings, so neither should our confidence be fixed on the sacraments, nor should the glory of God be transferred to them; but passing beyond them all, our faith and confession should rise to him who is the Author of the sacraments and of all things.

Gen 3:22 · Eph 2:12
13¶

There is nothing in the argument that some base on the very term sacrament. This term, they say, while it has many meanings in approved authors, has only one that applies to signs, namely, when it is used for the formal oath the soldier gives to his commander on entering service. For just as by that military oath recruits bind themselves to be faithful to their commander and make a profession of military service, so by our signs we acknowledge Christ to be our commander and declare that we serve under his standard. They add comparisons to make the matter clearer. As the toga distinguished the Romans from the Greeks, who wore the pallium; and as the different ranks of Romans were distinguished from one another by their particular insignia (for example, the senatorial rank from the equestrian by the purple and crescent-shaped shoes, and the equestrian from the common people by a ring), so we wear our symbols to distinguish us from the profane. But it is clear enough from what has been said above that the ancients, in giving the name of sacraments to signs, had not at all regarded the use of the term by Latin writers, but had, for convenience, given it this new meaning, simply as a way of expressing sacred signs. But if we were to argue more subtly, we might say that they seem to have given the term this meaning in a way like that in which they use the term faith in the sense in which it is now used. For while faith is truthfulness in keeping promises, they have used it for the certainty or firm conviction we have of the truth. In this way, while a sacrament is the act of the soldier when he vows obedience to his commander, they made it the act by which the commander admits soldiers to the ranks. For in the sacraments the Lord promises that he will be our God, and we promise that we will be his people. But we leave aside such subtleties, since I think I have shown by arguments abundantly plain that all the ancient writers meant was to indicate that sacraments are signs of sacred and spiritual things. The comparisons drawn from outward objects (chap. 15, sec. 1) we indeed admit; but we do not approve their making what is a secondary thing in the sacraments into the first, and indeed the only thing. The first thing is that they may help our faith in God; the secondary, that they may attest our confession before men. These comparisons apply to the secondary reason. Let it therefore remain a fixed point that mysteries would be cold (as we have seen) if they were not helps to our faith, and supports joined to doctrine for the same end and purpose.

14¶

On the other side, we must observe this. Just as the people I described diminish the force of the sacraments and overthrow their use altogether, so there are others who ascribe to the sacraments a kind of secret power that nowhere is said to have been planted in them by God. By this error the simpler and less wary are dangerously deceived. They are taught to seek the gifts of God where those gifts cannot possibly be found, and so they are imperceptibly drawn away from God, embracing empty vanity in place of his truth. For the schools of the Sophists have taught, with general agreement, that the sacraments of the new law—that is, those now in use in the Christian church—justify and confer grace, provided only that we do not throw up the obstacle of mortal sin. It is impossible to say how deadly and pestilential this opinion is, and all the more because for many ages it has prevailed over a large part of the world, to the great loss of the church. It is plainly of the devil. First, in promising a righteousness without faith, it drives souls headlong to destruction. Second, in drawing a cause of righteousness from the sacraments, it entangles miserable minds—already too inclined toward the earth on their own—in a superstitious notion that makes them rest in the spectacle of a bodily object rather than in God himself. I wish we did not have such experience of both these evils as to make it altogether unnecessary to give a lengthy proof of them. For what is a sacrament received without faith but the most certain destruction to the church? Since nothing is to be expected beyond the promise, and the promise no less proclaims wrath to the unbeliever than it offers grace to the believer, it is an error to suppose that anything more is conferred by the sacraments than is offered by the Word of God and obtained by true faith. From this another thing follows: that the assurance of salvation does not depend on partaking of the sacraments, as though justification consisted in it. This justification, which is treasured up in Christ alone, we know to be communicated no less by the preaching of the gospel than by the seal of the sacrament, and it may be fully enjoyed without this seal. So true is it, as Augustine declares, that there may be invisible sanctification without a visible sign, and, on the other hand, a visible sign without true sanctification. For, as he says elsewhere, men put on Christ sometimes only to the extent of sharing in the sacrament, and sometimes to the extent of holiness of life. The former may be common to the good and the bad; the latter belongs to the good alone.

15¶

From this comes the distinction, repeatedly made by Augustine and rightly understood, between the sacrament and the matter of the sacrament. For he does not mean merely that the figure and the truth are contained in it, but that they do not so cling together that they cannot be separated, and that in this connection it is always necessary to distinguish the thing from the sign, so that we do not transfer to the one what belongs to the other.

16¶

If this is obscure because of its brevity, I will explain it more fully. I say that Christ is the matter, or, if you prefer, the substance of all the sacraments, since in him they have their whole solidity, and apart from him they promise nothing. This is why there is the less excuse for the error of Peter Lombard, who plainly makes the sacraments causes of the righteousness and salvation of which they are only parts. Bidding farewell to all other causes of righteousness that the wit of man invents, our duty is to hold to this one alone. To the degree that we are helped by the sacraments to cherish, confirm, and increase the true knowledge of Christ, so that we both possess him more fully and enjoy him in all his richness, to that same degree they are effectual for us. This is the case when what is offered there is received by us in true faith. You will ask, then: do the wicked, by their ingratitude, make the ordinance of God fruitless and void? I answer that what I have said is not to be understood as though the power and truth of the sacrament depended on the condition or pleasure of the one who receives it. What God instituted remains firm and keeps its own nature, however much men may vary. But since it is one thing to offer and another to receive, nothing prevents a symbol consecrated by the word of the Lord from truly being what it is said to be, and preserving its power, even though at the same time it confers no benefit on the wicked and ungodly. Augustine solves this question well in a few words: "If you receive carnally, it does not cease to be spiritual; but it is not spiritual to you." Yet, just as Augustine shows in the passages above that a sacrament is worthless if separated from its truth, so also, when the two are joined together, he reminds us that we must still distinguish, so that we do not cling too much to the outward sign. "Just as it is a servile weakness to follow the letter and take the signs for the things signified, so to interpret the signs as of no use is an extravagant error." He names two faults to be avoided here. The first is when we receive the signs as if they had been given in vain, and—by maliciously destroying or weakening their hidden meaning—prevent them from yielding any fruit. The second is when, by not raising our minds beyond the visible sign, we attribute to it the blessings that are conferred upon us by Christ alone, and that by means of the Holy Spirit, who makes us partakers of Christ. The outward signs help us if they invite us to Christ; but when they are twisted to any other purpose, their whole usefulness is overthrown.

17¶

Therefore let this be a settled point: that the office of the sacraments differs in no way from the Word of God, and this office is to hold forth and offer Christ to us, and, in him, the treasures of heavenly grace. They confer nothing and accomplish nothing unless they are received in faith—just as wine and oil, or any other liquid, however great the quantity you pour out, will run away and be lost unless there is an open vessel to receive it. When the vessel is not open, though it may be sprinkled all over on the outside, it will still remain entirely empty. We must be careful not to be led into a similar error by the terms, somewhat too extravagant, that the ancient Christian writers used in praising the dignity of the sacraments. We must not suppose that there is some hidden power inherent in the sacraments by which they, of themselves, confer the gifts of the Holy Spirit upon us, the way wine is drunk out of a cup. For the only office divinely assigned to them is to attest and ratify the goodwill of the Lord toward us; and they avail no further than as they are accompanied by the Holy Spirit, who opens our minds and hearts and makes us able to receive this testimony, in which various distinguished graces are clearly displayed. For the sacraments, as we observed a moment ago, are to us what messengers of good news are to people, or what earnest payments are in ratifying agreements. They do not of themselves bestow any grace, but they announce and display it; and, like a deposit or a badge, they give a ratification of the gifts that the divine generosity has bestowed upon us. The Holy Spirit—whom the sacraments do not bring indiscriminately to all, but whom the Lord especially confers on his own people—brings the gifts of God along with him, makes way for the sacraments, and causes them to bear fruit. But although we do not deny that God, by the immediate working of his Spirit, gives his support to his own ordinance, keeping the administration of the sacraments he has instituted from being fruitless and empty, still we maintain that the inward grace of the Spirit, since it is distinct from the outward ministration, ought to be viewed and considered separately. God, therefore, truly performs whatever he promises and pictures by signs; and the signs are not without effect, for they prove that he is their true and faithful author. The only question here is whether the Lord works by his own proper and intrinsic power (as it is called), or hands over his office to outward symbols. We maintain that whatever instruments he employs take nothing away from his primary working. In this doctrine of the sacraments their dignity is highly extolled, their use plainly shown, their usefulness sufficiently proclaimed, and moderation duly preserved in all things—so that nothing is attributed to them that ought not to be, and nothing denied them that they ought to have. Meanwhile, we get rid of that fiction by which the cause of justification and the power of the Holy Spirit are shut up in the elements as vessels and vehicles, and the special power that was overlooked is now distinctly explained. Here, too, we ought to note that what the minister pictures and attests by outward action, God performs inwardly, so that what God claims for himself alone may not be ascribed to mortal man. Augustine is careful to observe this: "How do both God and Moses sanctify? Not Moses in place of God, but Moses by visible sacraments through his ministry, and God by invisible grace through the Holy Spirit. In this lies the whole fruit of visible sacraments; for what do these visible sacraments avail without that sanctification of invisible grace?"

18¶

The term "sacrament," in the sense we have so far considered, includes, in general, all the signs that God ever commanded people to use so that he might make them sure and confident of the truth of his promises. These he was pleased sometimes to place in natural objects, and sometimes to display in miracles. Of the first kind we have an example in his giving the tree of life to Adam and Eve as a pledge of immortality, that they might feel confident of the promise as often as they ate of its fruit. Another example was when he gave the bow in the cloud to Noah and his descendants, as a memorial that he would not again destroy the earth by a flood. These were to Adam and Noah what sacraments are: not that the tree could give Adam and Eve the immortality that it could not give to itself, nor that the bow—which is only a reflection of the sun's rays on the clouds opposite—could have the effect of holding back the waters. Rather, they had a mark engraved on them by the word of God, to be proofs and seals of his covenant. The tree had been a tree before, and the bow a bow; but when they were inscribed with the word of God, a new form was given to them: they began to be what they had not been before. So that no one may suppose these things were said in vain, the bow to this very day is a witness to us of the covenant that God made with Noah. As often as we look upon it, we read this promise from God: that the earth will never be destroyed by a flood. Therefore, if some petty philosopher, to mock the simplicity of our faith, should argue that the variety of colors arises naturally from the rays reflected by the cloud opposite, let us grant the fact; but at the same time let us mock his stupidity in not recognizing God as the Lord and governor of nature, who at his pleasure makes all the elements serve his glory. If he had stamped memorials of this kind on the sun, the stars, the earth, and the stones, they would all have been to us as sacraments. For why is unshaped silver and minted silver not of the same value, since they are the same metal? Simply because the first has nothing but its own nature, while the second, stamped with the public mark, becomes money and receives a new value. And shall the Lord not be able to stamp his creatures with his word, so that things which were once bare elements may become sacraments? Examples of the second kind were given when he showed light to Abraham in the smoking furnace; when he covered the fleece with dew while the ground was dry, and, on the other hand, when the dew covered the ground while the fleece was untouched, to assure Gideon of victory; and when he made the shadow go back ten degrees on the dial, to assure Hezekiah of his recovery. These things, which were done to assist and establish their faith, were also sacraments.

19¶

But my present purpose is to speak especially of those sacraments which the Lord has been pleased to institute as ordinary sacraments in his church, to raise up his worshipers and servants in one faith and in the confession of one faith. For, to use Augustine's words, "In no religion, true or false, can men be gathered together unless they are united by some common use of visible signs or sacraments." Our most merciful Father, foreseeing this need, from the very first appointed certain exercises of godliness for his servants. These exercises Satan afterward corrupted and debased in many ways by transferring them to ungodly and superstitious worship. Hence those rites by which the Gentiles were initiated into their mysteries, and other degenerate ceremonies. Yet, although they were full of error and superstition, they were at the same time an indication that men could not be without such outward signs of religion. But since they were neither founded on the word of God nor pointed to that truth which all signs ought to hold forth, they do not deserve to be named when we speak of the sacred symbols that were instituted by God and have not been turned from their proper end—namely, to be helps to true godliness. And these ordinary sacraments do not consist of simple signs, like the rainbow and the tree of life, but of ceremonies—or, if you prefer, the signs here employed are ceremonies. But since, as has been said above, they are testimonies of grace and salvation from the Lord, so, in regard to us, they are marks of profession by which we openly swear by the name of God, binding ourselves to be faithful to him. This is why Chrysostom somewhere shrewdly calls them agreements, by which God enters into covenant with us, and we become bound to holiness and purity of life, because a mutual pledge is here exchanged between God and us. For just as God there promises to cover and blot out any guilt and penalty we may have incurred by our transgression, and reconciles us to himself in his only-begotten Son, so we, in our turn, oblige ourselves by this profession to the pursuit of godliness and righteousness. And so it may justly be said that such sacraments are ceremonies by which God is pleased to train his people: first, to stir up, cherish, and strengthen faith within; and second, to testify our religion before men.

20¶

Now these sacraments have been different at different times, according to the way of governing that the Lord has seen fit to use in revealing himself to men. Circumcision was commanded to Abraham and his descendants, and to it were afterward added purifications and sacrifices and other rites of the law of Moses. These were the sacraments of the Jews right up until the coming of Christ. After these were abolished, the two sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper, which the Christian church now uses, were instituted. I speak of those instituted for the use of the whole church. As for the laying on of hands, by which the ministers of the church are set apart for their office—though I have no objection to its being called a sacrament—I do not number it among the ordinary sacraments. The place to be assigned to the other things commonly reckoned as sacraments we will see shortly. Still, the ancient sacraments had the same end in view as our own—namely, to direct us, and almost to lead us by the hand, to Christ; or rather, they were like images to represent him and hold him forth to our knowledge. But since we have already shown that the sacraments are a kind of seal of the promises of God, let us hold it as a most certain truth that no divine promise has ever been offered to man except in Christ, and that therefore, when the sacraments remind us of any divine promise, they must of necessity set Christ before us. From this came that heavenly pattern of the tabernacle and the legal worship, which was shown to Moses on the mountain. There is only this difference: while the ancient sacraments foreshadowed a Christ who was still awaited, ours bear testimony to him as already come and revealed.

21¶

When these things are explained one by one and separately, they will be much clearer. Circumcision was a sign by which the Jews were reminded that whatever comes from the seed of man—in other words, the whole nature of man—is corrupt and needs to be cut off. Moreover, it was a proof and a memorial to confirm them in the promise made to Abraham, of a seed in whom all the nations of the earth should be blessed, and from whom they themselves were to look for a blessing. That saving seed, as Paul teaches us, was Christ, in whom alone they trusted to recover what they had lost in Adam. Therefore circumcision was to them what Paul says it was to Abraham—namely, a sign of the righteousness of faith: a seal by which they were more certainly assured that their faith, in waiting for the Lord, would be accepted by God for righteousness. But we will have a better opportunity elsewhere to follow out the comparison between circumcision and baptism.

22¶

As for our own sacraments, they present Christ the more clearly to us, the more fully he has been revealed to man ever since he was set forth by the Father, exactly as he had been promised. For baptism testifies that we are washed and purified; the Supper of the Eucharist testifies that we are redeemed. Cleansing is pictured by water, satisfaction by blood. Both are found in Christ, who, as John says, "came by water and blood"—that is, to purify and to redeem. Of this the Spirit of God is also a witness. Indeed, there are three witnesses in one: water, the Spirit, and blood. In the water and the blood we have evidence of purification and redemption, but the Spirit is the chief witness, who gives us full assurance of this testimony. This sublime mystery was gloriously displayed on the cross of Christ, when water and blood flowed from his sacred side—which, for this reason, Augustine rightly called the fountain of our sacraments. Of these we will treat at greater length shortly. There is no doubt that, if you compare one age with another, the grace of the Spirit is now more abundantly displayed. For this belongs to the glory of the kingdom of Christ, as we gather from several passages, and especially from the seventh chapter of John. In this sense we are to understand the words of Paul, that the law was "a shadow of good things to come, but the body is of Christ." His purpose is not to declare that those manifestations of grace were ineffective—those by which God was pleased to prove his truth to the patriarchs, just as he proves it to us today in baptism and the Lord's Supper—but to contrast the two, and to show the great value of what is given to us, so that no one may think it strange that by the coming of Christ the ceremonies of the law have been abolished.

23¶

The Scholastic teaching—to glance at it in passing—by which the difference between the sacraments of the old and the new dispensation is made so great that the former did nothing but foreshadow the grace of God, while the latter actually confer it, must be utterly rejected. Since the apostle speaks in no higher terms of the one than of the other—when he says that the fathers ate of the same spiritual food, and explains that that food was Christ—who will dare to regard as an empty sign that which gave the Jews a true display of communion with Christ? And the very case the apostle is there treating argues strongly for our view. For, to warn us against trusting in a cold knowledge of Christ, an empty title of Christianity, and outward observances, and so daring to despise the judgment of God, he sets out striking examples of God's severity toward the Jews, to make us aware that if we indulge in the same vices, the same punishments they suffered hang over us. Now, to make the comparison fitting, it was necessary to show that there is no inequality between us and them in those blessings in which he forbade us to glory. Therefore he first makes them equal to us in the sacraments, and leaves us not one bit of privilege that could give us any hope of going unpunished. Nor can we justly attribute more to our baptism than he elsewhere attributes to circumcision, when he calls it a seal of the righteousness of faith. Whatever, then, is now set forth to us in the sacraments, the Jews formerly received in theirs—namely, Christ, with his spiritual riches. The same power that ours possess they experienced in theirs—namely, that they were seals of the divine favor toward them with respect to the hope of eternal salvation. Had these objectors been sound interpreters of the Epistle to the Hebrews, they would not have been so deceived; but reading there that sins were not atoned for by legal ceremonies—indeed, that the ancient shadows were of no importance for justification—they overlooked the contrast that is there being drawn, and, fastening on the single point that the law in itself was of no avail to the worshiper, concluded that the old sacraments were mere figures, devoid of truth. The apostle's purpose is to show that there is nothing in the ceremonial law until we arrive at Christ, on whom alone its whole power depends.

26¶

Those wretched sophists are perhaps deceived by the extravagant praises of our signs that appear in the ancient writers—for example, this passage of Augustine: "The sacraments of the old law only promised a Savior, whereas ours give salvation." Not perceiving that these and similar turns of phrase are overstatements, they too have put forward their own overblown doctrines, but in a sense altogether foreign to that of the ancient writers. For Augustine means nothing more than he does in another place, where he says, "The sacraments of the law of Moses foretold Christ; ours announce him." And again, "Those were promises of things to be fulfilled; these are signs that the fulfillment has come." It is as if he had said: those foreshadowed him while he was still awaited; ours, now that he has arrived, set him before us as present. Moreover, concerning the manner of signifying, he says, as he also indicates elsewhere, "The Law and the Prophets had sacraments foretelling a thing future; the sacraments of our time attest that what they foretold as coming has come." His thoughts about the reality and power of the sacraments he explains in several passages, as when he says, "The sacraments of the Jews were different in their signs but alike in the things signified; different in outward appearance but alike in spiritual power." Again: "In different signs there was the same faith. So it was in different signs as in different words, because the words change their sound according to the times, and yet words are nothing but signs. The fathers drank of the same spiritual drink, but not of the same bodily drink. See, then, how, while the faith remains the same, the signs vary. There the rock was Christ; to us that is Christ which is placed on the altar. They, as a great sacrament, drank of the water flowing from the rock; believers know what we drink. If you look at the outward appearance, there was a difference; if at the meaning it conveys, they drank of the same spiritual drink." And again: "In this mystery their food and drink are the same as ours—the same in meaning, not in form; for the same Christ was figured to them in the rock, and to us he has been revealed in the flesh." Yet we grant that in this respect too there is some difference. Both testify that the fatherly kindness of God and the graces of the Spirit are offered to us in Christ—but ours more clearly and splendidly. In both there is a setting forth of Christ, but in ours it is more full and complete, in keeping with that distinction between the Old and New Testaments of which we spoke above. And this is the meaning of Augustine—whom we quote more often, as being the best and most faithful witness of all antiquity—where he says that after Christ was revealed, sacraments were instituted that were fewer in number, but of more majestic significance and more excellent power. A sacrament, Calvin teaches, is an external sign by which God seals his promises upon our weak faith—never a vessel of secret power working apart from the Word, never a bare token emptied of grace, but a visible Word that holds Christ before our eyes and is made effectual only by the Spirit who opens the heart to receive it. The two errors he names still divide the church in 2026: those who shrink the sacraments to mere symbols of belonging, and those who trust the rite itself as though water and bread conferred grace by their performance. Both forget that Christ alone is the substance, and faith the open vessel. For believers raised in a sign-saturated age—quick to confuse the badge with the blessing—this is a sober anchor: come to the table and the font not to gaze at the element but to lay hold of the promise sealed there. The sign points away from itself, and our whole confidence must rise past it to the One who keeps every word he gives.
