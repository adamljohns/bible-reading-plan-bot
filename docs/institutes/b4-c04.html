Of the State of the Primitive Church, and the Mode of Government in Use Before the Papacy

The divisions of this chapter are,—I. The mode of government in the primitive Church, sec 1-10. II. The formal ordination of Bishops and Ministers in the primitive Church, sec. 10-15. 1. The method of government in the primitive Church. Not in every respect conformable to the rule of the word of God. Three distinct orders of Ministers. 2. First, the Bishop, for the sake of preserving order, presided over the Presbyters or Pastors. The office of Bishop. Presbyter and Bishop the same. The institution of this order ancient. 3. The office of Bishop and Presbyters. Strictly preserved in the primitive Church. 4. Of Archbishops and Patriarchs. Very seldom used. For what end instituted. Hierarchy an improper name, and not used in Scripture. 5. Deacons, the second order of Ministers in the primitive Church. Their proper office. The Bishop their inspector. Subdeacons, their assistants. Archdeacons, their presidents. The reading of the Gospel, an adventitious office conferred in honour on the Deacons. 6. Mode in which the goods of the Church were anciently dispensed. 1. The support of the poor. 2. Due provision for the ministers of the Church. 7. The administration at first free and voluntary. The revenues of the Church afterwards classed under four heads. 8. A third part of the revenues devoted to the fabric of churches. To this, however, when necessary, the claim of the poor was preferred. Sayings, testimonies, and examples to this effect, from Cyril, Acatius, Jerome, Exuperius, Ambrose. 9. The Clerici, among whom were the Doorkeepers and Acolytes, were the names given to exercises used as a kind of training for tyros. 10. Second part of the chapter, treating of the calling of Ministers. Some error introduced in course of time in respect to celibacy from excessive strictness. In regard to the ordination of Ministers, full regard not always paid to the consent of the people. Why the people less anxious to maintain their right. Ordinations took place at stated times. 11. In the ordination of Bishops the liberty of the people maintained. 12. Certain limits afterwards introduced to restrain the inconsiderate licence of the multitude. 13. This mode of election long prevailed. Testimony of Gregory. Nothing repugnant to this in the decretals of Gratian. 14. The form of ordination in the ancient Church. 15. This form gradually changed.

1¶

Up to this point we have discussed the order of church government as it has been handed down to us in the pure word of God, and the ministerial offices as Christ instituted them (chapter 1, sections 5, 6; chapter 3). Now, so that the whole subject may be explained more clearly and familiarly, and also fixed better in our minds, it will be useful to look at the form of the early Church, since this will give us a kind of visible picture of the divine institution. For although the bishops of those times published many canons in which they seemed to express more than is expressed in the sacred volume, yet they were so careful to frame their whole order on the word of God, the only standard, that it is easy to see that they hardly departed from it in any respect. Even if something may be lacking in these enactments, still, since they sincerely desired to preserve the divine institution and did not stray far from it, it will be of great benefit here to explain briefly what their practice was. As we have stated that three classes of ministers are set before us in Scripture, so the early Church divided all its ministers into three orders. For from the order of presbyters, some were chosen as pastors and teachers, while to the rest was committed the censure of morals and discipline. To the deacons belonged the care of the poor and the dispensing of alms. Readers and Acolytes were not the names of particular offices; rather, those whom they called clergy they trained from their youth to serve the Church by certain exercises, so that they might better understand what they were destined for, and later come better prepared to their duty, as I will shortly show at greater length. Accordingly Jerome, in setting out five orders in the Church, lists Bishops, Presbyters, Deacons, Believers, and Catechumens; to the other Clergy and to the Monks he gives no proper place. "Pourtant Sainct Hierome apres avoir divisé l'Eglise en cinq ordres, nomme les Eveques, secondement, les Pretres, tiercement, les Diacres, puis les fideles en commun, finalement, ceux qui n'etoient pas baptisés encore, mais qui s'etoient presentés pour etre instruits en la foy Chretienne; et puis recevoient le baptéme. Ainsi il n'attribue point de certain lieu au reste du Clergé ni aux Moines." That is: However, Saint Jerome, after dividing the Church into five orders, names the Bishops; second, the Priests; third, the Deacons; then the faithful in common; and last, those who were not yet baptized but had presented themselves to be instructed in the Christian faith, and afterward received baptism. Thus he assigns no fixed place to the rest of the Clergy or to the Monks.

2¶

All those, therefore, to whom the office of teaching was committed they called presbyters, and in each city these presbyters chose one of their number, to whom they gave the special title of bishop, lest, as usually happens, dissension should arise from equality. The bishop, however, was not so superior in honor and dignity as to have dominion over his colleagues. Rather, just as it belongs to the president of an assembly to bring matters before it, to gather the opinions, to take the lead in consulting, advising, and exhorting, to guide the whole proceeding by his authority, and to carry out what is decreed by common consent, so a bishop held the same office in a meeting of presbyters. And the ancients themselves admit that this practice was introduced by human arrangement, according to the needs of the times. So Jerome, on the Epistle to Titus, chapter 1, says, "A bishop is the same as a presbyter. And before dissensions were introduced into religion by the instigation of the devil, and it was said among the people, I am of Paul, and I of Cephas, churches were governed by a common council of presbyters. Afterwards, that the seeds of dissension might be plucked up, the whole charge was devolved upon mendatory rescripts, preventions, and the like. But they all conduct one. Therefore, as presbyters know that by the custom of the Church they are subject to him who presides, so let bishops know that they are greater than presbyters more by custom than in consequence of our Lord's appointment, and ought to rule the Church for the common good." In another place he shows how ancient the custom was (Jerome, Epistle to Evangelus). For he says that at Alexandria, from Mark the Evangelist down to Heraclas and Dionysius, the presbyters always placed one man, chosen from among themselves, in a higher rank, and gave him the name of bishop. Each city, therefore, had a college of presbyters, made up of pastors and teachers. For they all performed for the people that office of teaching, exhorting, and correcting which Paul lays on bishops (Titus 1:9); and so that they might leave a seed behind them, they made it their business to train the younger men who had devoted themselves to the sacred warfare. To each city was assigned a certain district, which took its presbyters from it and was regarded as, so to speak, incorporated into that church. Each presbyter, as I have said, merely to preserve order and peace, was under one bishop, who, though he excelled the others in dignity, was subject to the assembly of the brethren. But if the district under his bishopric was too large for him to discharge all the duties of a bishop in it, presbyters were distributed over it in certain places to act as his substitutes in lesser matters. These were called Chorepiscopi (rural bishops), because they represented the bishops throughout the province.

3¶

But as regards the office of which we are now treating, the bishop as well as the presbyters had to devote themselves to the administration of the word and sacraments. For only at Alexandria (because Arius had troubled the Church there) was it enacted that no presbyter should deliver an address to the people, as Socrates says, Tripartite History, book 9. Jerome does not hide his dissatisfaction with this enactment (Jerome, Epistle to Evagrius). It would certainly have been thought monstrous for a man to give himself out as a bishop and yet not show himself a true bishop by his conduct. Such, then, was the strictness of those times that all ministers were obliged to fulfill the office as the Lord requires of them. Nor am I referring to the practice of one age only, since not even in the time of Gregory, when the Church had almost fallen (and had certainly declined greatly from its ancient purity), would any bishop have been tolerated who refrained from preaching. In one part of his twenty-fourth Epistle he says, "The priest dies when no sound is heard from him: for he calls forth the wrath of the unseen Judge against him if he walks without the sound of preaching." Elsewhere he says, "When Paul testifies that he is pure from the blood of all men (Acts 20:26), by his words, we, who are called priests, are charged, are arraigned, are shown to be guilty, since to those sins which we have of our own we add the deaths of other men, for we commit murder as often as lukewarm and silent we see them daily going to destruction" (Gregory, Homily on Ezekiel 11:26). He calls himself and others silent when they are less diligent in their work than they ought to be. Since he does not spare even those who did their duty only in part, what do you think he would do with those who neglected it entirely? For a long time, therefore, it was held in the Church to be the first duty of a bishop to feed the people with the word of God, or to build up the Church, in public and in private, with sound doctrine.

4¶

As for the fact that each province had an archbishop among its bishops (see chapter 7, section 15), and further, that at the Council of Nicaea patriarchs were appointed to be above archbishops in order and dignity, this was designed for the preservation of discipline, although, in treating the subject here, it should not be left unsaid that the practice was very rare. The chief reason these orders were instituted was that if anything arose in any church that could not well be settled by a few, it might be referred to a provincial synod. If the size or difficulty of the case called for wider discussion, the patriarchs were brought in along with synods. French, "La cognoissance venoit aux patriarches, qui assemblerent le concile do tous les eveques respondant a leur primauté"; that is, the hearing of the case fell to the patriarchs, who assembled a council of all the bishops under their primacy.

5¶

Nor was the situation of the deacons then any different from what it had been under the apostles (chapter 3, section 6). For they received the daily offerings of the faithful and the annual revenues of the Church, so that they might apply them to their true uses, that is, partly to maintaining ministers and partly to supporting the poor; but this was done under the oversight of the bishop, to whom they gave an account of their stewardship every year. For although the canons uniformly make the bishop the dispenser of all the goods of the Church, this is not to be understood as if he took that charge on himself alone. It means that it belonged to him to tell the deacon who were to be admitted to the public support of the Church, and to point out to what persons and in what portions the rest was to be distributed, and that he had the right to see whether the deacon faithfully performed his office. So in the canons which they ascribe to the apostles it is said, "We command that the bishop have the affairs of the Church under his control. For if the souls of men, which are more precious, have been intrusted to him, much more is he entitled to have the charge of money matters, so that under his control all may be dispensed to the poor by the presbyters and deacons, that the ministration may be made reverently and with due care." And at the Council of Antioch it was decreed (chapter 35) that bishops who meddled with the property of the Church without the knowledge of the presbyters and deacons should be restrained. But there is no need to discuss this point further, since it is evident from many of the letters of Gregory that even at that time, when the ordinances of the Church were otherwise much corrupted, it was still the practice for the deacons, under the bishops, to be the stewards of the poor. It is probable that at first subdeacons were attached to the deacons to assist them in the care of the poor; but the distinction was gradually lost. Archdeacons began to be appointed when the size of the revenues called for a new and more exact method of administration, though Jerome mentions that the office already existed in his day. Jerome, Epistle to Nepotian. It is also mentioned by Chrysostom, Epistle to Innocent.

6¶

From this we may also judge what the use of the goods of the Church was, and how they were distributed. You will find everywhere, both in the decrees of synods and in the ancient writers, that whatever the Church possessed, whether in lands or in money, was the patrimony of the poor. Accordingly, this saying is sounded again and again in the ears of bishops and deacons: Remember that you are not handling your own property, but what is set apart for the needs of the poor; if you dishonestly hide it or squander it, you will be guilty of blood. Hence they are urged to distribute these goods to those to whom they are due, with the greatest fear and reverence, as in the sight of God, without respect of persons. Hence also those solemn appeals in Chrysostom, Ambrose, Augustine, and other bishops like them, in which they assert their integrity before the people. But since it is just in itself, and was sanctioned by a divine law, that those who devote their labor to the Church should be supported at the public expense of the Church, and since some presbyters in that age had consecrated their patrimony to God and become poor of their own will, the distribution was made in such a way that support was given to the ministers and the poor were not neglected. Meanwhile it was provided that the ministers themselves, who ought to be an example of frugality to others, should not have so much as might be abused for luxury or self-indulgence, but only what was needed to supply their wants. "For those clergy who can be supported by their own patrimony," says Jerome, "commit sacrilege if they accept what belongs to the poor, and by such abuse eat and drink judgment to themselves."

7¶

At first the administration was free and voluntary, when bishops and deacons were faithful of their own accord, and integrity of conscience and purity of life took the place of laws. Later, when bad examples arose from the greed and corrupt desires of some, canons were framed to correct these evils, and they divided the revenues of the Church into four parts, assigning one to the clergy, another to the poor, another to the repair of churches and other buildings, and a fourth to the poor, whether In the Amsterdam edition the words are only "quartam vero advenis pauperibus." The Geneva edition of 1559, the last published under Calvin's own eye, has "quartam vero tam advenis quam indigenis pauperibus." With this Tholuck agrees.

8¶

Moreover, the amount spent on adorning churches was at first very small, and even later, when the Church had become somewhat wealthier, they kept to moderation in that matter. Still, whatever money was then collected was held in reserve for the poor whenever any greater need arose. So Cyril, when a famine prevailed in the province of Jerusalem and the want could not be met in any other way, took the vessels and robes and sold them for the support of the poor. In the same way Acacius, Bishop of Amida, when a great multitude of the Persians was nearly destroyed by famine, assembled the clergy, delivered this noble address, "Our God has no need either of chalices or salvers, for he neither eats nor drinks" (Tripartite History, book 5 and book 11, chapter 16), and melted down the plate so that he might provide food and obtain the means of ransoming the wretched. Jerome also, while inveighing against the excessive splendor of churches, relates that Exuperius, Bishop of Toulouse in his day, though he carried the body of the Lord in a wicker basket and his blood in a glass, nevertheless let no poor man go hungry (Jerome, to Nepotian). What I have just said of Acacius, Ambrose relates of himself. For when the Arians attacked him for having broken up the sacred vessels to ransom captives, he gave this most admirable defense: "He who sent the apostles without gold has also gathered churches without gold. The Church has gold not to keep but to distribute, and to give support in necessity. What need is there of keeping what is of no benefit? Are we ignorant how much gold and silver the Assyrians carried off from the temple of the Lord? Is it not better for a priest to melt them for the support of the poor, if other means are lacking, than for a sacrilegious enemy to carry them away? Would not the Lord say, Why have you allowed so many poor to die of hunger, when you certainly had gold with which to minister to their support? Why have so many captives been carried away and not redeemed? Why have so many been slain by the enemy? It would have been better to preserve living vessels than vessels of metal. To these charges you will not be able to answer: for what could you say? I feared lest the temple of God should lack ornament. He would answer, Sacraments require not gold, and things which are not bought with gold please not by gold. The ornament of the Sacraments is the ransom of captives" (Ambrose, On Offices, book 2, chapter 28). In a word, we see the exact truth of what he says elsewhere, namely, that whatever the Church then possessed was the revenue of the needy. Again: A bishop has nothing but what belongs to the poor (Ambrose, book 5, Epistles 31, 33).

9¶

We have now reviewed the ministerial offices of the ancient Church. For the others, which church writers mention, were exercises and preparations rather than distinct offices. These holy men, so that they might leave a nursery of the Church behind them, took in young men who, with the consent and authority of their parents, devoted themselves to the spiritual warfare under their guardianship and training; and they so formed them from their tender years that they might not enter on the discharge of the office as ignorant novices. All who received this training were called by the general name of Clerks. I could wish that some more suitable name had been given them, for this title had its origin in error, or at least in an improper feeling, since Peter calls the whole Church κληρος (clerus), that is, the inheritance of the Lord (1 Peter 5:3). In itself, however, it was a most sacred and wholesome institution that those who wished to devote themselves and their labor to the Church should be brought up under the charge of the bishop, so that no one should minister in the Church unless he had first been well trained, unless he had absorbed sound doctrine early in life, unless by stricter discipline he had formed habits of gravity and of more severe morals, had been withdrawn from ordinary business, and had grown accustomed to spiritual cares and studies. For as recruits in the military art are trained by mock battles for real and serious warfare, so there was an elementary training by which they were exercised in clerical duty before they were actually appointed to office. First, then, they entrusted them with the opening and shutting of the church, and called them Ostiarii. Next, they gave the name of Acolytes to those who assisted the bishop in household services and constantly attended him, first as a mark of respect, and second so that no suspicion might arise. The French adds, "Afin qu'il n'all' nulle part sans compagnie et sans temoin"; in order that he might not go anywhere without company and without a witness.

French, "On leur ordonnoit de faire la lecture des Pseaumes au pulpitre"; they ordered them to read the Psalms at the desk.

10¶

As to what we set down as the first and second heads in the calling of ministers, namely, the persons to be elected and the religious care to be exercised in it, the ancient Church followed the command of Paul and the examples of the apostles. For they were accustomed to meet for the election of pastors with the greatest reverence and with earnest prayer to God. Moreover, they had a form of examination by which they tested the life and doctrine of those to be elected against the standard of Paul (1 Timothy 3:2). Only here they sometimes erred through excessive strictness, by requiring more of a bishop than Paul requires, and especially, in the course of time, by requiring celibacy; but in other respects their practice matched Paul's description. As to our third head, however, namely, who had the right to appoint ministers, they did not always keep the same rule. In ancient times no one was admitted to the number of the clergy without the consent of the whole people; and hence Cyprian makes an elaborate apology for having appointed Aurelius a reader without consulting the Church, because, although it was done contrary to custom, it was not done without reason. He begins thus: "In ordaining clergy, dearest brethren, we are wont previously to consult you, and weigh the manners and merits of each by the common advice" (Cyprian, book 2, Epistle 5). But as in these minor exercises The French adds, "Comme de Lecteurs et Acolytes"; as Readers and Acolytes.

11¶

In the election of bishops, the people long kept their right to prevent anyone from being thrust in who was not acceptable to all. Accordingly, the Council of Antioch forbade anyone to be installed over those who were unwilling. Leo I also carefully confirms this. Hence these passages: "Let him be elected whom the clergy and people or the majority demand." Again: "Let him who is to preside over all be elected by all" (Leo, Epistle 90, chapter 2). He, therefore, who is appointed while unknown and unexamined must of necessity be forcibly thrust in. Again: "Let him be elected who is chosen by the clergy, and called by the people, and let him be consecrated by the provincials with the judgment of the metropolitan." So careful were the holy fathers that this liberty of the people should on no account be diminished, that when a general council assembled at Constantinople was ordaining Nectarius, they declined to do it without the approval of the whole clergy and people, as their letter to the Roman synod testified. Accordingly, when any bishop nominated his successor, the act was not ratified without consulting the whole people. Of this you have not only an example but the very form in Augustine, in the nomination of Eradius (Augustine, Epistle 110). And Theodoret, after relating that Peter was the successor nominated by Athanasius, immediately adds that the priestly order ratified it, and that the magistracy, the chief men, and the whole people approved it by their acclamation. The whole narrative in Theodoret is most worth noting. Theodoret, book 4, chapter 20.

12¶

It was indeed decreed by the Council of Laodicea (Canon 18), and I admit on the best grounds, that the election should not be left to crowds. For it hardly ever happens that so many heads settle any matter well with one consent. It generally holds true that "Incertum scindi studia in contraria vulgus"; "Opposing wishes rend the fickle crowd." For, first, the clergy alone made the choice, and presented the man they had chosen to the magistrate, or senate, and chief men. These, after deliberation, put their signature to the election if it seemed proper; if not, they chose another whom they approved more highly. The matter was then laid before the multitude, who, although they were not bound by those earlier proceedings, were less able to act in a tumultuous way. Or, if the matter began with the multitude, it was only so that it might be known whom they most wanted to have; and after the wishes of the people had been heard, the clergy at length made the election. Thus the clergy were neither free to appoint whomever they pleased, nor yet obliged to give way to the foolish desires of the people. Leo sets down this order when he says, "The wishes of the citizens, the testimonies of the people, the choice of the honorable, the election of the clergy, are to be waited for" (Leo, Epistle 87). Again: "Let the testimony of the honorable, the subscription of the clergy, the consent of the magistracy and people, be obtained; otherwise (he says) it must on no account be done." Nor is anything more intended by the decree of the Council of Laodicea than that the clergy and rulers were not to let themselves be carried away by the rash multitude, but rather by their prudence and gravity to restrain its foolish desires whenever there was occasion.

13¶

This mode of election was still in force in the time of Gregory, and probably continued to a much later period. Many of his letters that are still extant clearly prove this, for whenever a new bishop is to be elected, his custom is to write to the clergy, the magistrates, and the people, and sometimes also to the governor, according to the nature of the government. But if, because of the unsettled state of the Church, he gives the oversight of the election to a neighboring bishop, he always requires a formal decision confirmed by the signatures of all. Indeed, when one Constantius was elected Bishop of Milan, and many of the Milanese had fled to Genoa because of the incursions of the Barbarians, he thought the election would not be lawful unless they too were called together and gave their assent (Gregory, book 2, Epistle 69). Indeed, not five hundred years have passed since Pope Nicholas fixed the election of the Roman Pontiff in this way: first, that the cardinals should take the lead; next, that they should join the rest of the clergy to themselves; and last, that the election should be ratified by the consent of the people. And at the end he recites the decree of Leo which I quoted a little earlier, and orders it to be enforced in the future. But should the malice of the wicked so prevail that the clergy are forced to leave the city in order to make a pure election, he nevertheless orders that some of the people be present at the same time. The vote of the Emperor, as far as we can tell, was required in only two churches, those of Rome and Constantinople, these being the two seats of empire. For when Ambrose was sent by Valentinian to Milan with authority to oversee the election of a new bishop, it was an extraordinary measure, made necessary by the violent factions that raged among the citizens. But at Rome the authority of the Emperor in the election of the bishop was so great that Gregory says he was appointed to the government of the Church by his order (Gregory, book 1, Epistle 5), though he had been called by the people in the regular way. The custom, however, was that when the magistrates, clergy, and people had nominated anyone, he was at once presented to the Emperor, who either ratified the election by approving it or annulled it by disapproving. There is nothing contrary to this practice in the decretals collected by Gratian, where all that is said is that it was on no account to be tolerated that canonical election should be abolished and a king appoint a bishop at his pleasure, and that one promoted in this way by violent authority was not to be consecrated by the metropolitans. For it is one thing to deprive the Church of her right and transfer it entirely to the whim of a single individual; it is another thing to grant a king or emperor the honor of confirming a lawful election by his authority.

14¶

It now remains to treat of the form by which the ministers of the ancient Church were initiated into their office after election. The Latins called this Ordination or consecration, and the Greeks χειροτονία, sometimes also χειροθεσία, though χειροτονία properly denotes the mode of election in which votes are declared by a show of hands. There is extant a decree of the Council of Nicaea to the effect that the metropolitan, with all the bishops of the province, was to meet to ordain the one who had been chosen. But if some were prevented by distance, or sickness, or any other necessary cause, at least three should meet, and those who were absent should signify their consent by letter. And this canon, after it had fallen into disuse, was later renewed by several councils. All, or at least all who had no excuse, were required to be present, so that a stricter examination might be made of the life and doctrine of the man to be ordained; for the thing was not done without examination. And it appears from the words of Cyprian that in former times they were not accustomed to be called after the election, but to be present at the election, so that they might act as moderators and no disorder be committed by the crowd. For after saying that the people had the power either of choosing worthy priests or of refusing unworthy ones, he immediately adds, "For which reason, we must carefully observe and hold by the divine and apostolic tradition (which is observed by us also, and almost by all the provinces), that for the due performance of ordinations all the nearest bishops of the province should meet with the people over whom the person is proposed to be ordained, and the bishop should be elected in presence of the people. But since they were sometimes too slow to assemble, and there was a risk that some might abuse the delay for purposes of intrigue, it was thought sufficient if they came after the designation had been made, and, after due investigation, consecrated the man who had been approved.

15¶

While this was done everywhere without exception, a different custom gradually gained ground, namely, that those who had been elected should go to the metropolitan to obtain ordination. This came about more from ambition and the corruption of the ancient custom than from any good reason. And not long afterward, when the authority of the Roman See had grown, another custom still worse was introduced, of applying to it for the consecration of the bishops of almost all Italy. This we may observe from the letters of Gregory (book 2, Epistles 69, 76). The ancient right was kept by only a few cities that had not given way so easily, Milan for example. Perhaps only the metropolitan sees kept their privilege. For in order to consecrate an archbishop, it was the practice for all the bishops of the province to meet in the metropolitan city. The form used was the laying on of hands (chapter 19, sections 28, 31). I do not read that any other ceremonies were used, except that in the public meeting the bishops wore some dress to distinguish them from the other presbyters. Presbyters and deacons were also ordained by the laying on of hands; but each bishop, with the college of presbyters, ordained his own presbyters. But although they all performed the same act, yet because the bishop presided and the ordination was carried out, as it were, under his auspices, it was said to be his. Hence ancient writers often say that a presbyter differs from a bishop in no respect except that he does not have the power of ordaining.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin holds up the primitive Church not as a relic to be idolized but as a witness — its ordered offices, its careful ordination, its bishops who presided to preserve peace rather than to lord it over the flock — that good government flows from the Word and serves the gospel, not the other way round. A word for 2026: the early Church was no anarchy of gifted enthusiasts, and neither was it a throne for ambitious men; it was a body knit together under Christ, where authority bent its knee to Scripture. Let the church in our day learn from that ancient sobriety — wary alike of the loose congregation that scorns all oversight and the celebrity machinery that crowns its leaders kings. In the home, model the same submission you ask of your children: order under the Word, service before status. The form matters because the Head is glorious, and a household and a congregation rightly governed are a quiet rebuke to a fractious age and a steady shelter for those he is gathering.
