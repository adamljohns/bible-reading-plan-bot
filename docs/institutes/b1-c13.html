The Unity of the Divine Essence in Three Persons Taught, in Scripture, From the Foundation of the World

This chapter consists of two parts. The former delivers the orthodox doctrine concerning the Holy Trinity. This occupies from sec. 1-21, and may be divided into four heads; the first, treating of the meaning of Person, including both the term and the thing meant by it, sec. 2-6; the second, proving the deity of the Son, sec. 7-13; the third, the deity of the Holy Spirit, sec. 14 and 15; and the fourth, explaining what is to be held concerning the Holy Trinity. The second part of the chapter refutes certain heresies which have arisen, particularly in our age, in opposition to this orthodox doctrine. This occupies from sec. 21 to the end.

1¶

The teaching of Scripture concerning the immensity and the spirituality of God's essence should serve not only to scatter the wild dreams of the common people, but also to refute the subtleties of a profane philosophy. One of the ancients thought he was speaking shrewdly when he said that everything we see and everything we do not see is God (Seneca, Preface to book 1 of Natural Questions). In this way he imagined that deity was poured out into every separate part of the world. But although God, to keep us within the bounds of sobriety, speaks sparingly of his essence, still, by the two attributes I have mentioned, he at once puts down all gross imaginations and checks the boldness of the human mind. His immensity surely ought to deter us from measuring him by our senses, while his spiritual nature forbids us to indulge in carnal or earthly speculation about him. With the same purpose he often represents heaven as his dwelling place. It is true, indeed, that since he is incomprehensible, he fills the earth also; but knowing that our minds are heavy and crawl along the earth, he raises us above the world, so that he may shake off our sluggishness and inactivity. And here we have a refutation of the error of the Manichees, who, by adopting two first principles, made the devil almost the equal of God. This, certainly, was both to destroy his unity and to limit his immensity. Their attempt to twist certain passages of Scripture proved their shameful ignorance, just as the very nature of the error proved their monstrous infatuation. The Anthropomorphites also, who dreamed of a bodily God because mouth, ears, eyes, hands, and feet are often ascribed to him in Scripture, are easily refuted. For who is so lacking in understanding as not to see that God, in speaking this way, lisps with us as nurses commonly do with little children? Such ways of speaking, therefore, do not so much express what kind of being God is, as accommodate the knowledge of him to our weakness. In doing so, he must of course stoop far below his proper height.

2¶

But there is another special mark by which he designates himself, in order to give us a more intimate knowledge of his nature. While he proclaims his unity, he distinctly sets it before us as existing in three persons. These we must hold, unless the bare and empty name of deity is merely to flutter about in our brain without any genuine knowledge. Moreover, so that no one may dream of a threefold God, or think that the simple essence is divided among the three Persons, we must here look for a brief and easy definition that will effectively guard us from error. But since some inveigh strongly against the term Person as a merely human invention, let us first consider how far they have any ground for doing so.

When the Apostle calls the Son of God "the express image of his person" (Heb. 1:3), he undoubtedly does assign to the Father some subsistence in which he differs from the Son. For to hold with some interpreters that the term is equivalent to essence (as if Christ represented the substance of the Father like the impression of a seal on wax) would be not only harsh but absurd. For since the essence of God is simple and undivided, and contained whole in himself, in full perfection, without partition or diminution, it is improper, even ridiculous, to call it his express image (χαρακτερ). But because the Father, though distinguished by his own peculiar properties, has expressed himself wholly in the Son, he is said with perfect reason to have made his person (hypostasis) manifest in him. And this fits well with what is added immediately after, namely, that he is "the brightness of his glory." The fair inference from the Apostle's words is that there is a proper subsistence (hypostasis) of the Father, which shines out brightly in the Son. From this, again, it is easy to infer that there is a subsistence (hypostasis) of the Son which distinguishes him from the Father. The same holds in the case of the Holy Spirit; for we will shortly prove both that he is God and that he has a subsistence distinct from the Father. This, moreover, is not a distinction of essence, which it would be impious to multiply. If, then, the Apostle's testimony is believed, it follows that there are three persons (hypostases) in God. Since the Latins used the word Persona to express the same thing as the Greek ὑπόστατις, it shows excessive fastidiousness and even perverseness to quarrel over the term. The most literal translation would be subsistence. Many have used substance in the same sense. Nor, indeed, was the use of the term Person confined to the Latin Church. For the Greek Church likewise, perhaps in order to testify to their agreement, taught that there are three πρόσωπα (aspects) in God. All of these, however, whether Greeks or Latins, though they differ about the word, are in full agreement on the substance.

3¶

Now, then, heretics may snarl, and the overly fastidious may carp at the word Person as inadmissible because it is of human origin. But since they cannot dislodge us from our position that three are named, each of whom is perfect God, and yet that there is no plurality of gods, it is most unfair to attack terms that do nothing more than explain what the Scriptures declare and establish. "It would be better," they say, "to confine not only our meanings but also our words within the bounds of Scripture, and not to scatter about foreign terms that will become the seedbeds of future quarrels and dissensions. In this way men grow weary of disputes about words; the truth is lost in wrangling, and love melts away amid hateful strife." If they call it a foreign term because it cannot be pointed out in Scripture in so many syllables, they certainly impose an unjust law, a law that would condemn every interpretation of Scripture not composed of other words of Scripture. But if by foreign they mean what has been idly devised and is then superstitiously defended, what tends more to strife than to edification, what is used either out of place or with no benefit, what offends pious ears by its harshness and leads them away from the simplicity of God's Word, then I embrace their sobriety with all my heart. For I think we are bound to speak of God as reverently as we are bound to think of him. As our own thoughts about him are foolish, so our own language about him is absurd. Still, some middle way must be kept. The unerring standard both of thinking and of speaking must be drawn from the Scriptures: by it all the thoughts of our minds and the words of our mouths should be tested. But as for those parts of Scripture that are dark and intricate to our capacities, what forbids us to explain them in clearer terms, provided those terms are kept in reverent and faithful subordination to the truth of Scripture, and are used sparingly and modestly, and not without occasion? Of this we have many examples. When it has been proved that the Church was driven by the strongest necessity to use the words Trinity and Person, will not the man who still inveighs against novelty of terms be rightly suspected of taking offense at the light of truth, and of having no other ground for his attack than that the truth is made plain and transparent?

4¶

Such novelty (if it should be called novelty) becomes most necessary when the truth must be defended against slanderers who evade it by quibbling. Of this we today have more than enough experience, since we are constantly called upon to attack the enemies of pure and sound doctrine. These slippery snakes escape by their swift and twisting turns unless they are vigorously pursued and, when caught, firmly held. So the early Christians, when they were harassed by the disputes that heresies produced, were forced to declare their convictions in the most scrupulously exact terms, so that no indirect loopholes might remain for ungodly men, to whom ambiguity of expression was a kind of hiding place. Arius confessed that Christ was God and the Son of God, because the passages of Scripture to this effect were too clear to be resisted; and then, as if he had done well, he pretended to agree with others. But all the while he did not stop teaching that Christ was created and had a beginning like other creatures. To drag this man of wiles out of his hiding places, the ancient Church went a step further and declared that Christ is the eternal Son of the Father and consubstantial with the Father. The impiety was fully exposed when the Arians began to declare their hatred and utter loathing of the term ὁμοούσιος. If their first confession, namely, that Christ was God, had been sincere and from the heart, they would not have denied that he was consubstantial with the Father. Who would dare to accuse those ancient writers of being men of strife and contention because they debated so warmly and disturbed the peace of the Church over a single word? That little word distinguished Christians of pure faith from the blasphemous Arians. Next Sabellius arose, who treated the names of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit as almost nothing; he maintained that they were not used to mark any distinction, but were different attributes of God, like many others of a similar kind. When the matter was debated, he acknowledged his belief that the Father was God, the Son God, the Spirit God; but then he had the evasion ready that he had said nothing more than if he had called God powerful, and just, and wise. And so he sang another tune, namely, that the Father was the Son, and the Holy Spirit the Father, without order or distinction. The worthy teachers of that day, who had the cause of godliness at heart, proclaimed, in order to defeat this man's dishonesty, that three subsistences must truly be acknowledged in the one God. To protect themselves against twisting craftiness by the simple, open truth, they affirmed that a Trinity of Persons subsists in the one God, or (which is the same thing) in the unity of God.

5¶

Where names have not been invented rashly, we must take care not to make ourselves guilty of arrogance and rashness by rejecting them. I could wish, indeed, that such names were buried, provided all would agree in believing that the Father, Son, and Spirit are one God, and yet that the Son is not the Father, nor the Spirit the Son, but that each has his own peculiar subsistence. I am not so minutely precise as to fight furiously over mere words. For I observe that the writers of the ancient Church, while they always spoke with great reverence on these matters, neither agreed with one another nor were always consistent with themselves. How strange the formulas used by the Councils and defended by Hilary! How extravagant the view Augustine sometimes takes! How unlike the Greeks are to the Latins! But let one example of disagreement suffice. The Latins, in translating ὁμοούσιος, used consubstantialis (consubstantial), indicating that there was one substance of the Father and the Son, and so using the word Substance for Essence. Hence Jerome, in his Letter to Damasus, says it is profane to affirm that there are three substances in God. But in Hilary you will find it said more than a hundred times that there are three substances in God. Then how greatly Jerome is troubled by the word Hypostasis! He suspects some hidden poison when it is said that there are three Hypostases in God. And he does not hide his belief that the expression, though used in a pious sense, is improper; if, indeed, he was sincere in saying this, and did not rather deliberately try, by a groundless slander, to bring odium on the Eastern bishops whom he hated. He certainly shows little fairness in asserting that in all the pagan schools οὐσία is equivalent to Hypostasis, an assertion completely refuted by ordinary and common usage.

More courtesy and moderation is shown by Augustine (On the Trinity, book 5, chapters 8 and 9), who, although he says that Hypostasis in this sense is new to Latin ears, is still so far from objecting to the ordinary use of the term by the Greeks that he even tolerates the Latins who had imitated the Greek way of speaking. The gist of what Socrates says about the term, in the sixth book of the Tripartite History, is that it had been improperly applied to this purpose by the unskilled. Hilary (On the Trinity, book 2) charges it against the heretics as a great crime that their misconduct had made it necessary to expose to the peril of human speech things that ought to have been reverently kept within the mind; and he does not hide his opinion that those who do so do what is unlawful, speak what is unspeakable, and pry into what is forbidden. Shortly after, he apologizes at great length for presuming to introduce new terms. For after setting down the natural names of Father, Son, and Spirit, he adds that all further inquiry goes beyond the meaning of words, the perception of the senses, and the grasp of the intellect. And in another place (On the Councils) he congratulates the bishops of France for not having framed any other confession, but for having received without alteration the ancient and most simple confession received by all the churches from the days of the Apostles. Not unlike this is Augustine's apology, that the term had been wrung from him by necessity, because of the poverty of human language in so high a matter: not that the reality could be expressed by it, but that he might not pass over in silence, without attempting to show, how the Father, Son, and Spirit are three.

The modesty of these holy men should warn us not to dip our pen in gall at once and sternly denounce those who may be unwilling to swear to the terms we have devised, provided they do not in this betray pride, or petulance, or unbecoming heat, but are willing to weigh the necessity that compels us to speak this way, and may so become gradually accustomed to a useful form of expression. Let men also take careful heed that, in opposing the Arians on the one side and the Sabellians on the other, and in eagerly trying to deprive both of any handle for cavil, they do not bring themselves under some suspicion of being disciples of either Arius or Sabellius. Arius says that Christ is God, and then mutters that he was made and had a beginning. He says that he is one with the Father, but secretly whispers in the ears of his party, made one, like other believers, though with a special privilege. Say that he is consubstantial, and you immediately tear the mask from this chameleon, though you add nothing to Scripture. Sabellius says that the Father, Son, and Spirit indicate some distinction in God. Say that they are three, and he will shout that you are making three Gods. Say that there is a Trinity of Persons in one divine essence, and you will only express in one word what the Scriptures say, and stop his empty prattle. If any are so superstitiously precise that they will not tolerate these terms, then, let them do their worst, they will still not be able to deny that when one is spoken of, a unity of substance must be understood, and when three in one essence are spoken of, the persons in this Trinity are meant. When this is confessed without equivocation, we do not dwell on words. But I learned long ago, and indeed on more than one occasion, that those who contend stubbornly about words are tainted with some hidden poison; and therefore that it is wiser to provoke them deliberately than to court their favor by speaking obscurely.

6¶

But to say nothing more about words, let us now turn to the thing itself. By person, then, I mean a subsistence in the divine essence, a subsistence which, while related to the other two, is distinguished from them by incommunicable properties. By subsistence we want something other than essence to be understood. For if the Word were simply God and had no property peculiar to himself, John could not rightly have said that he had always been with God. When he adds immediately after that the Word was God, he calls us back to the one essence. But because he could not be with God without dwelling in the Father, there arises that subsistence which, though joined to the essence by an indissoluble bond and incapable of being separated from it, still has a special mark by which it is distinguished from it. Now, I say that each of the three subsistences, while related to the others, is distinguished by its own properties. Here relation is expressly stated, because when God is mentioned simply and without qualification, the name belongs no less to the Son and the Spirit than to the Father. But whenever the Father is compared with the Son, the peculiar property of each distinguishes the one from the other. Again, whatever is proper to each I affirm to be incommunicable, because nothing that is attributed to the Father as a mark of distinction can apply to or be transferred to the Son. I have no objection to adopting the definition of Tertullian, provided it is properly understood, "that there is in God a certain arrangement or economy, which makes no change in the unity of essence." (Tertullian, Against Praxeas.)

7¶

Before going further, it will be necessary to prove the deity of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. After that, we shall see how they differ from each other. When the Word of God is set before us in the Scriptures, it would certainly be most absurd to imagine that it is only a fleeting and passing voice, sent out into the air and going forth outside God himself, as was the case with the communications made to the patriarchs and with all the prophecies. The reference is rather to the wisdom that always dwells with God, and by which all oracles and prophecies were inspired. For, as Peter testifies (1 Pet. 1:11), the ancient prophets spoke by the Spirit of Christ just as the apostles did, and all who after them were ministers of the heavenly doctrine. But since Christ had not yet been manifested, we must understand that the Word was begotten of the Father before all ages. And if that Spirit, whose instruments the prophets were, belonged to the Word, the conclusion is irresistible that the Word was truly God. And this is shown clearly enough by Moses in his account of the creation, where he places the Word as the intermediary. For why does he relate so distinctly that God, in creating each of his works, said, Let there be this, let there be that, unless it was so that the unsearchable glory of God might shine forth in his image? I know that idle talkers would easily evade this by saying that Word is used for order or command; but the apostles are better interpreters, when they tell us that the worlds were created by the Son, and that he upholds all things by his mighty word (Heb. 1:2). For here we see that word is used for the nod or command of the Son, who is himself the eternal and essential Word of the Father. And no man of sound mind can have any doubt about Solomon's meaning when he presents Wisdom as begotten by God, and presiding over the creation of the world and all the other divine works (Prov. 8:22). For it would be trifling and foolish to imagine some temporary command at the time when God was pleased to carry out his fixed and eternal counsel, and something still more mysterious. To this our Savior's words refer: "My Father worketh hitherto, and I work" (John 5:17). In affirming in this way that from the foundation of the world he has constantly worked with the Father, he gives a clearer explanation of what Moses only touched on. The meaning, therefore, is that God spoke in such a way as to leave the Word his own proper part in the work, and so made the working common to both. But the clearest explanation is given by John, when he states that the Word, which was from the beginning, God and with God, was, together with God the Father, the maker of all things. For he both attributes a substantial and permanent essence to the Word, assigning to it a certain peculiarity, and shows distinctly how God spoke the world into being. Therefore, as all revelations from heaven are rightly called by the title of the Word of God, so the highest place must be given to that substantial Word, the source of all inspiration, which, being subject to no variation, remains forever one and the same with God, and is God.

8¶

Here an outcry is raised by certain men who, while they do not dare to deny his deity openly, secretly rob him of his eternity. For they contend that the Word only began to be when God opened his sacred mouth in the creation of the world. In this way, with excessive rashness, they imagine some change in the essence of God. For although the names of God that refer to his outward works began to be ascribed to him once the work existed (as when he is called the Creator of heaven and earth), godliness does not recognize or admit any name that would indicate that a change had taken place in God himself. For if anything new had come to be in him, the saying of James would cease to be true, that "every good gift, and every perfect gift, is from above, and cometh down from the Father of lights, with whom is no variableness, neither shadow of turning" (James 1:17). Nothing, therefore, is more intolerable than to imagine a beginning for that Word which was always God, and which afterward was the Creator of the world. But they think they argue cleverly when they maintain that Moses, in saying that God then spoke for the first time, must be taken to imply that until then no Word existed in him. This is the merest trifling. It surely does not follow that because a thing begins to be manifested at a certain time, it never existed before. I draw a very different conclusion. Since at the very moment when God said, "Let there be light," the power of the Word was immediately put forth, the Word must have existed long before. If anyone asks how long, he will find that it was without beginning. No definite period of time is fixed when he himself says, "Now, O Father, glorify thou me with thine own self with the glory which I had with thee before the world was" (John 17:5). Nor is this left out by John; for before he comes down to the creation of the world, he says that "in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God." We therefore conclude once again that the Word was eternally begotten by God and has dwelt with him from everlasting. In this way his true essence, his eternity, and his deity are established.

9¶

I am not now treating the office of the Mediator, having put it off until the subject of redemption is taken up. Yet because it ought to be clear and beyond dispute to all that Christ is that Word made flesh, this seems the most fitting place to bring forward the passages that assert the deity of Christ. When it is said in the forty-fifth Psalm, "Thy throne, O God, is for ever and ever," the Jews quibble that the name Elohim is applied to angels and sovereign powers. But no passage is to be found in Scripture where an eternal throne is set up for a creature. For he is not simply called God, but also the eternal Ruler. Besides, the title is never conferred on any man without some qualification, as when it is said that Moses would be a God to Pharaoh (Exod. 7:1). Some read it as if it were in the genitive case, but this is too flat. I admit that anything of singular excellence is often called divine, but it is clear from the context that such a meaning here would be harsh and forced, and wholly out of place. But if their perverseness still refuses to yield, there is surely no obscurity in Isaiah, where Christ is presented both as God and as possessed of supreme power, one of the attributes peculiar to God: "His name shall be called the Mighty God, the Everlasting Father, the Prince of Peace" (Isa. 9:6). Here, too, the Jews object, and turn the passage around this way: This is the name by which the mighty God, the Everlasting Father, will call him; so that all they leave to the Son is "Prince of Peace." But why should so many titles be heaped up here on God the Father, when the prophet's purpose is to present the Messiah with certain distinguished properties that may lead us to put our faith in him? There can be no doubt, therefore, that he who a little before was called Emmanuel is here called the Mighty God. Moreover, nothing could be clearer than the words of Jeremiah: "This is the name whereby he shall be called, THE LORD OUR RIGHTEOUSNESS" (Jer. 23:6). For since the Jews themselves teach that the other names of God are mere titles, whereas this one, which they call the ineffable name, is a proper noun and expresses his essence, we conclude that the only begotten Son is the eternal God, who declares elsewhere, "My glory will I not give to another" (Isa. 42:8). An attempt is made to evade this on the ground that this name is given by Moses to the altar he built, and by Ezekiel to the New Jerusalem. But who does not see that the altar was set up as a memorial to show that God was the one who exalted Moses, and that the name of God was applied to Jerusalem only to testify to the divine presence? For this is how the prophet speaks: "The name of the city from that day shall be, The Lord is there" (Ezek. 48:35). In the same way, "Moses built an altar, and called the name of it JEHOVAH-nissi" (Jehovah my exaltation). But the point seems to be disputed even more sharply in regard to another passage in Jeremiah, where the same title is applied to Jerusalem in these words: "In those days shall Judah be saved, and Jerusalem shall dwell safely; and this is the name wherewith she shall be called, The Lord our Righteousness." But this passage is so far from being against the truth we defend that it rather supports it. The prophet, having earlier declared that Christ is the true Jehovah from whom righteousness flows, now declares that the Church would be made so aware of this that she could glory in taking his very name. In the former passage, therefore, the fountain and cause of righteousness is set down; in the latter, the effect is described.

10¶

But if this does not satisfy the Jews, I do not know what cavils will enable them to evade the many passages in which Jehovah is said to have appeared in the form of an Angel (Judges 6:7; 13:16-23, etc.). This Angel claims for himself the name of the Eternal God. If it is alleged that he does this by virtue of the office he bears, the difficulty is by no means solved. No servant would rob God of his honor by allowing sacrifice to be offered to himself. But the Angel, by refusing to eat bread, commands that the sacrifice due to Jehovah be offered to him. So the fact itself proves that he was truly Jehovah. Accordingly, Manoah and his wife conclude from the sign that they had seen not only an angel, but God. Hence Manoah's exclamation, "We shall die; for we have seen the Lord." When the woman replies, "If Jehovah had wished to slay us, he would not have received the sacrifice at our hand," she acknowledges that he who was earlier called an angel was certainly God. We may add that the angel's own reply removes all doubt: "Why do ye ask my name, which is wonderful?" Hence the impiety of Servetus was all the more detestable when he maintained that God was never manifested to Abraham and the patriarchs, but that an angel was worshiped in his place. The orthodox teachers of the Church have rightly and wisely explained that the Word of God was the supreme angel, who then began, as it were in anticipation, to perform the office of Mediator. For though he was not yet clothed with flesh, he came down as in an intermediate form, so that he might have more familiar access to the faithful. This closer intercourse won for him the name of the Angel; yet he still kept the character that rightly belonged to him, that of the God of ineffable glory. The same thing is indicated by Hosea, who, after mentioning Jacob's wrestling with the angel, says, "Even the Lord God of hosts; the Lord is his memorial" (Hosea 12:5). Servetus again suggests that God played the part of an angel; as if the prophet did not confirm what had been said by Moses, "Wherefore is it that thou dost ask after my name?" (Gen. 32:29, 30). And the confession of the holy patriarch declares clearly enough that he was not a created angel, but one in whom the fullness of the Godhead dwelt, when he says, "I have seen God face to face." Hence also Paul's statement that Christ led the people in the wilderness (1 Cor. 10:4; see also Calvin on Acts 7:30, and below, chapter 14, section 9). Although the time of humiliation had not yet come, the eternal Word displayed a type of the office he was to fulfill. Again, if the first chapter of Zechariah (verse 9, etc.) and the second (verse 3, etc.) are considered fairly, it will be seen that the angel who sends the other angel is immediately afterward declared to be the Lord of hosts, and that supreme power is ascribed to him. I pass over countless passages on which our faith rests securely, though they may not carry much weight with the Jews. For when it is said in Isaiah, "Lo, this is our God; we have waited for him, and he will save us; this is the Lord: we have waited for him, we will be glad and rejoice in his salvation" (Isa. 25:9), even the blind can see that the God referred to is he who rises up again for the deliverance of his people. And the emphatic description, repeated twice, rules out the idea that it refers to anyone other than Christ. Still clearer and stronger is the passage in Malachi, where a promise is made that the messenger then expected would come to his own temple (Mal. 3:1). The temple was certainly dedicated to Almighty God alone, and yet the prophet claims it for Christ. Hence it follows that he is the God who was always worshiped by the Jews.

11¶

The New Testament abounds in countless passages, and our aim must therefore be to select a few rather than to pile up the whole. But though the apostles spoke of him as Mediator after his appearance in the flesh, every passage I bring forward will be enough to prove his eternal Godhead. And the first thing that deserves special notice is that predictions concerning the eternal God are applied to Christ, as either already fulfilled in him or to be fulfilled at some future time. Isaiah prophesies that "the Lord of Hosts" shall be "for a stone of stumbling, and for a rock of offence" (Isa. 8:14). Paul asserts that this prophecy was fulfilled in Christ (Rom. 9:33), and therefore declares that Christ is that Lord of Hosts. In the same way he says in another passage, "We shall all stand before the Judgment-seat of Christ. For it is written, As I live, saith the Lord, every knee shall bow to me, and every tongue shall confess to God." Since in Isaiah God foretells this of himself (Isa. 45:23), and Christ displays the reality fulfilled in himself, it follows that he is the very God whose glory cannot be given to another. It is clear also that the passage from the Psalms (Ps. 68:19) which he quotes in the Epistle to the Ephesians applies only to God: "When he ascended up on high, he led captivity captive" (Eph. 4:8). Understanding that such an ascension was foreshadowed when the Lord put forth his power and won a glorious victory over the heathen nations, he indicates that what was thus foreshadowed was more fully manifested in Christ. So John testifies that it was the glory of the Son that was revealed to Isaiah in a vision (John 12:41; Isa. 6:4), though Isaiah himself expressly says that what he saw was the Majesty of God. Again, there can be no doubt that the qualities applied to the Son in the Epistle to the Hebrews are the brightest attributes of God: "Thou, Lord, in the beginning hast laid the foundation of the earth," etc., and, "Let all the angels of God worship him" (Heb. 1:10, 6). And yet he does not twist these passages in applying them to Christ, since Christ alone performed the things these passages celebrate. It was he who arose and had pity on Zion; it was he who claimed for himself dominion over all nations and islands. And why should John have hesitated to ascribe the Majesty of God to Christ, after saying in his preface that the Word was God? (John 1:14). Why should Paul have been afraid to place Christ on the judgment seat of God (2 Cor. 5:10), after he had so openly proclaimed his deity when he said that he was God over all, blessed forever? And to show how consistent he is on this point, he says elsewhere that "God was manifest in the flesh" (1 Tim. 3:16). If he is God blessed forever, then he is the one to whom alone, as Paul affirms in another place, all glory and honor are due. Paul does not hide this, but openly exclaims that "being in the form of God (he) thought it not robbery to be equal with God, but made himself of no reputation" (Phil. 2:6). And so that the wicked may not clamor and say that he was a kind of counterfeit God, John goes further and affirms, "This is the true God, and eternal life." Though it ought to be enough for us that he is called God, especially by a witness who testifies distinctly that we have no more gods than one, Paul says, "Though there be that are called gods, whether in heaven or in earth (as there be gods many, and lords many), but to us there is but one God" (1 Cor. 8:5, 6). When we hear from the same lips that God was manifest in the flesh, and that God purchased the Church with his own blood, why do we dream of some second God, to whom he makes not the slightest allusion? And there is no room to doubt that all the godly held the same view. Thomas, by addressing him as his Lord and God, certainly professes that he was the only God he had ever worshiped (John 20:28).

12¶

The deity of Christ becomes still more evident if it is judged by the works ascribed to him in Scripture. When he said of himself, "My Father worketh hitherto, and I work," the Jews, though very dull with regard to his other sayings, perceived that he was laying claim to divine power. And therefore, as John relates (John 5:17), they sought all the more to kill him, because he not only broke the Sabbath, but also said that God was his Father, making himself equal with God. How great, then, will our stupidity be if we do not perceive from the same passage that his deity is plainly taught? To govern the world by his power and providence, and to regulate all things by a power inherent in himself (which an apostle ascribes to him, Heb. 1:3), surely belongs to none but the Creator. Nor does he merely share the government of the world with the Father, but also each of the other offices, which cannot be communicated to creatures. The Lord proclaims by his prophets, "I, even I, am he that blotteth out thy transgressions for mine own sake" (Is. 43:25). When, in keeping with this declaration, the Jews thought that wrong was done to God when Christ forgave sins, he not only asserted in plain terms that this power belonged to him, but also proved it by a miracle (Mt. 9:6). We see, then, that he possessed in himself not the ministry of forgiving sins, but the inherent power which the Lord declares he will not give to another. What! Does it not belong to God alone to penetrate and examine the secret thoughts of the heart? But Christ also had this power, and therefore we conclude that Christ is God.

13¶

How clearly and transparently this appears in his miracles! I admit that similar and equal miracles were performed by the prophets and apostles; but there is this most essential difference, that they dispensed the gifts of God as his ministers, whereas he put forth his own inherent might. Sometimes, indeed, he used prayer, so that he might give glory to the Father, but we see that for the most part his own proper power is displayed. And how could he not be the true author of miracles who, on his own authority, commissions others to perform them? For the Evangelist relates that he gave the apostles power to cast out devils, cure the lepers, raise the dead, and so on. And they, by the way in which they carried out this ministry, showed plainly that their whole power was derived from Christ. "In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth," says Peter (Acts 3:6), "rise up and walk." It is not surprising, then, that Christ appealed to his miracles in order to overcome the unbelief of the Jews, since these were performed by his own power, and therefore bore the fullest testimony to his deity.

Again, if apart from God there is no salvation, no righteousness, no life, then Christ, having all these in himself, is certainly God. Let no one object that life or salvation is poured into him by God. For it is said not that he received salvation, but that he himself is salvation. And if there is none good but God, how could a mere man be pure? How could he be, I do not say good and just, but goodness and justice itself? Then what shall we say to the testimony of the Evangelist, that from the very beginning of the creation "in him was life, and this life was the light of men"? Relying on such proofs, we can boldly put our hope and faith in him, though we know that it is blasphemous impiety to put our confidence in any creature. The French adds, "Et ne faisons point cela témérairement, mais selon sa parole." And let us not do this rashly, but in accordance with his Word.

Calvin translates it as a question, "Do ye believe in God?"

Again, the prayer of faith is addressed to him, prayer which belongs especially to the divine majesty, if anything does. For the prophet Joel says, "And it shall come to pass, that whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord (Jehovah) shall be delivered" (Joel 2:32). And another says, "The name of the Lord (Jehovah) is a strong tower; the righteous runneth into it and is safe" (Prov. 18:10). But the name of Christ is called upon for salvation, and therefore it follows that he is Jehovah. Moreover, we have an example of such calling upon him in Stephen, when he said, "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit"; and after that in the whole Church, when Ananias says in the same book, "Lord, I have heard by many of this man, how much evil he has done to thy saints at Jerusalem; and here he has authority from the chief priests to bind all that call on thy name" (Acts 9:13, 14). And to make it more clearly understood that in Christ dwelt all the fullness of the Godhead bodily, the Apostle declares that the only doctrine he professed among the Corinthians, the only doctrine he taught, was the knowledge of Christ (1 Cor. 2:2). Consider what kind of thing it is, and how great, that the name of the Son alone is preached to us, though God commands us to glory only in the knowledge of himself (Jer. 9:24). Who will dare to maintain that he whom to know is our only ground of glorying is a mere creature? To this we may add that the greetings at the head of Paul's Epistles pray for the same blessings from the Son as from the Father. By this we are taught not only that the blessings our heavenly Father bestows come to us through his intercession, but that the Son himself, by a partnership in power, is their author. This practical knowledge is doubtless surer and more solid than any idle speculation. For the godly soul has its best view of God, and may almost be said to handle him, when it feels that it is quickened, enlightened, saved, justified, and sanctified by him.

14¶

In asserting the deity of the Spirit, the proof must be drawn from the same sources. And it is by no means an obscure testimony that Moses gives in the history of the creation, when he says that the Spirit of God was spread out over the abyss, or shapeless matter; for it shows not only that the beauty the world displays is maintained by the life-giving power of the Spirit, but that even before this beauty existed the Spirit was at work cherishing the confused mass. The French adds, "à ce qu'elle ne fust point aneantie incontinent;" so as to prevent its being instantly annihilated.

But, as I observed, the best proof for us is our own familiar experience. For nothing could be more foreign to a creature than the office that the Scriptures ascribe to him, and that the godly actually feel him performing: his being spread through all space, sustaining, strengthening, and quickening all things, both in heaven and on earth. The mere fact that he is not confined by any limits raises him above the rank of creatures, while his pouring vigor into all things, breathing into them being, life, and motion, is plainly divine. Again, if regeneration to incorruptible life is higher and far more excellent than any present quickening, what must we think of him by whose power it is produced? Now, many passages of Scripture show that he is the author of regeneration, not by a borrowed but by an inherent power; and not only so, but that he is also the author of our future immortality. In short, all the attributes peculiar to the Godhead are ascribed to him in the same way as to the Son. He searches the deep things of God, and has no counselor among the creatures; he bestows wisdom and the faculty of speech, though God declares to Moses (Exod. 4:11) that this is his own peculiar province. In the same way, through him we become partakers of the divine nature, so that we in some measure feel his quickening power within us. Our justification is his work; from him come power, sanctification, truth, grace, and every good thought, since it is from the Spirit alone that all good gifts proceed. Special attention is due to Paul's expression that, though there are diversities of gifts, "all these worketh that one and the self-same Spirit" (1 Cor. 12:11), he being not only the beginning or origin, but also the author. The French adds, "Sainct Paul n'eust jamais ainsi parlé, s'il n'eust cognu la vraie Divinté du Sainct Esprit." St. Paul would never have spoken this way if he had not known the deity of the Holy Spirit.

15¶

Nor does Scripture, in speaking of him, withhold the name of God. Paul concludes that we are the temple of God from the fact that "the Spirit of God dwelleth in us" (1 Cor. 3:16; 6:19; and 2 Cor. 6:16). Now it ought not to be lightly passed over that all the promises God makes of choosing us for himself as a temple receive their only fulfillment through his Spirit dwelling in us. Surely, as Augustine admirably puts it (To Maximinus, Letter 66), "if we were ordered to make a temple of wood and stone for the Spirit, since such worship is due to God alone, it would be a clear proof of the Spirit's deity; how much clearer a proof it is that we are not to make a temple for him, but to be that temple ourselves." And the Apostle says at one time that we are the temple of God, and at another time, in the same sense, that we are the temple of the Holy Spirit. Peter, when he rebuked Ananias for having lied to the Holy Spirit, said that he had not lied to men, but to God. And where Isaiah had presented the Lord of Hosts as speaking, Paul says that it was the Holy Spirit who spoke (Acts 28:25, 26). Indeed, words that the prophets regularly say were spoken by the Lord of Hosts are ascribed by Christ and his apostles to the Holy Spirit. Hence it follows that the Spirit is the true Jehovah who dictated the prophecies. Again, where God complains that he was provoked to anger by the stubbornness of the people, Isaiah says in place of this that his Holy Spirit was grieved (Isa. 63:10). Lastly, since blasphemy against the Spirit is not forgiven, either in this life or in the life to come, whereas he who has blasphemed against the Son may obtain pardon, that majesty must certainly be divine which it is an unpardonable crime to offend or diminish. I deliberately pass over several passages that the ancient fathers brought forward. They thought it a plausible proof to quote from David, "By the word of the Lord were the heavens made, and all the host of them by the breath (Spirit) of his mouth" (Ps. 33:6), in order to prove that the world was no less the work of the Holy Spirit than of the Son. But since it is common in the Psalms to repeat the same thing twice, and in Isaiah the spirit (breath) of the mouth is equivalent to word, that proof was weak; and so my aim has been to touch briefly on those proofs on which godly minds may rest securely.

16¶

But as God has manifested himself more clearly by the coming of Christ, so he has made himself more familiarly known in three persons. Of many proofs, let this one suffice. Paul joins these three together, God, Faith, and Baptism, and reasons from the one to the other, namely: because there is one faith, he infers that there is one God; and because there is one baptism, he infers that there is one faith. Therefore, if by baptism we are initiated into the faith and worship of one God, we must of necessity believe that he into whose name we are baptized is the true God. And there can be no doubt that our Savior meant to testify by a solemn declaration that the perfect light of faith is now displayed, when he said, "Go and teach all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" (Mt. 28:19), since this is the same thing as to be baptized into the name of the one God, who has been fully manifested in the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. Hence it plainly appears that the three persons, in whom alone God is known, subsist in the divine essence. And since faith certainly ought not to look here and there, or run up and down after various objects, but to look to, refer to, and cling to God alone, it is obvious that if there were various kinds of faith, there would also have to be various gods. Then, since the baptism of faith is a sacrament, its unity assures us of the unity of God. Hence it is also proved that it is lawful to be baptized only into one God, because we make a profession of faith in him in whose name we are baptized. What, then, is our Savior's meaning in commanding baptism to be administered in the name of the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, if it is not that we are to believe with one faith in the name of the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit? The French entirely omits the three previous sentences, beginning, "Then, since," etc.

17¶

On the other hand, the Scriptures show that there is some distinction between the Father and the Word, and between the Word and the Spirit; but the greatness of the mystery reminds us of the deep reverence and sobriety that ought to be used in discussing it. It seems to me that nothing could be more admirable than the words of Gregory Nazianzen: "̔Ου φθάνω το ἕι νοη̑σαι, καὶ τοι̑ς τρισὶ περιλάμπομαι οὐ φθάνω τὰ τρία διελει̑ν καὶ εὶς τὸ ἑν ἀναφέρομαι" (Gregory Nazianzen, Sermon on Holy Baptism). "I cannot think of the unity without being shone upon by the Trinity: I cannot distinguish between the Trinity without being carried up to the unity." Bernard, On Consideration, book 5: "Cum dico unum, non me trinitatis turbat numerus, qui essentiam non multiplicat, non variat, nec partitur. Rursum, quum, dico tria, non me arguit intuitus unitatis, quia illa quæcunque tria, seu illos tres, nec in confusionem cogit, nec in singularitatem redigit." See also Bernard, Sermon 71 on the Song of Songs.

18¶

I am not sure whether it is wise to borrow analogies from human affairs to express the nature of this distinction. The ancient fathers sometimes do so, but at the same time they admit that what they put forward as analogous is very widely different. And that is why I greatly dread anything like presumption here, lest some rash saying should furnish an occasion of slander to the malicious, or of delusion to the unlearned. It would be unfitting, however, to say nothing of a distinction that we observe the Scriptures have pointed out. This distinction is that to the Father is attributed the beginning of action, the fountain and source of all things; to the Son, wisdom, counsel, and the ordering of action; while the power and efficacy of action is assigned to the Spirit. Moreover, though the eternity of the Father is also the eternity of the Son and the Spirit, since God could never be without his own wisdom and power; and though in eternity there can be no room for first or last, still the distinction of order is not meaningless or superfluous: the Father is considered first, next the Son from him, and then the Spirit from both. For the mind of every man naturally inclines to consider, first, God; second, the wisdom coming forth from him; and last, the power by which he carries out the purposes of his counsel. For this reason the Son is said to be of the Father only, and the Spirit of both the Father and the Son. This is done in many passages, but in none more clearly than in the eighth chapter of Romans, where the same Spirit is called without distinction the Spirit of Christ and the Spirit of him who raised up Christ from the dead. And not improperly. For Peter also testifies (1 Pet. 1:21) that it was the Spirit of Christ that inspired the prophets, though the Scriptures so often say that it was the Spirit of God the Father.

19¶

Moreover, this distinction is so far from interfering with the most perfect unity of God that by it the Son may be proved to be one God with the Father, since he constitutes one Spirit with him, and the Spirit may be proved to be no different from the Father and the Son, since he is the Spirit of the Father and of the Son. In each hypostasis the whole nature is understood, the only difference being that each has his own peculiar subsistence. The whole Father is in the Son, and the whole Son in the Father, as the Son himself also declares (John 14:10), "I am in the Father, and the Father in me"; nor do the writers of the Church admit that the one is separated from the other by any difference of essence. "By those names which denote distinctions," says Augustine, "is meant the relation which they bear to each other, not the very substance by which they are one." In this way the opinions of the Fathers, which might sometimes seem to be at odds with one another, are to be reconciled. At one time they teach that the Father is the beginning of the Son; at another they assert that the Son has both deity and essence from himself, and is therefore one beginning with the Father. The cause of this apparent discrepancy is well and clearly explained by Augustine when he says, Augustine, Homily on the Season 38, On the Trinity. See also To Pascentius, Letter 174; Cyril, On the Trinity, book 7; the same, book 3 of the Dialogues; Augustine on Psalm 59; and Tractates on John 89; the same, on Psalm 68.

20¶

Let those, then, who love sobriety and are content with the measure of faith, receive in brief what is useful to know. It is this. When we profess to believe in one God, by the name God is understood the one simple essence, comprehending three persons or hypostases; and accordingly, whenever the name of God is used without qualification, the Son and the Spirit are meant no less than the Father. But when the Son is joined with the Father, relation comes into view, and so we distinguish between the Persons. But since the personal subsistences carry an order with them, the principle and origin being in the Father, whenever the Father and the Son, or the Father and the Spirit, are mentioned together, the name of God is given in a special way to the Father. In this way the unity of essence is retained, and regard is paid to the order, which nevertheless takes nothing away from the deity of the Son and the Spirit. And surely, since we have already seen how the apostles declare the Son of God to be the one whom Moses and the prophets declared to be Jehovah, we must always come back to a unity of essence. We therefore hold it a detestable blasphemy to call the Son a different God from the Father, because the simple name God does not admit of relation, nor can God, considered in himself, be said to be this or that. Next, that the name Jehovah, taken without qualification, may be applied to Christ is clear from the words of Paul, "For this thing I besought the Lord thrice." After giving the answer, "My grace is sufficient for thee," he adds, "that the power of Christ may rest upon me" (2 Cor. 12:8, 9). For it is certain that the name of Lord (Κυρίος) is put there for Jehovah, and therefore to restrict it to the person of the Mediator would be childish and frivolous, since the words are used absolutely, and not for the purpose of comparing the Father and the Son. And we know that, following the accepted usage of the Greeks, the apostles regularly substitute the word Κυρίος for Jehovah. Not to go far for an example, Paul besought the Lord in the same sense in which Peter quotes the passage of Joel, "Whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved" (Acts 2:21; Joel 2:28). Where this name is applied specifically to the Son, there is a different ground for it, as will be seen in its own place; for the present it is enough to remember that Paul, after praying to God absolutely, immediately adds the name of Christ. So too the Spirit is called God absolutely by Christ himself. For nothing prevents us from holding that he is the entire spiritual essence of God, in which Father, Son, and Spirit are comprehended. This is plain from Scripture. For as God is there called a Spirit, so the Holy Spirit also, insofar as he is a hypostasis of the whole essence, is said to be both of God and from God.

21¶

Satan, in order to tear up our faith by the roots, has always stirred up fierce disputes, partly about the divine essence of the Son and the Spirit, and partly about the distinction of persons. In almost every age he has roused impious spirits to harass the orthodox teachers on this point, and in our own day he is trying to kindle a new flame out of the old embers. It will therefore be proper here to dispose of some of these perverse dreams. Until now our chief aim has been to stretch out our hand to guide those who are willing to learn, not to make war on the stubborn and contentious; but now the truth that was calmly demonstrated must be defended against the slanders of the ungodly. Still, our principal concern will be to provide sure footing for those whose ears are open to the word of God. Here, if anywhere, in considering the hidden mysteries of Scripture, we should reflect soberly and with great moderation, carefully guarding against letting either our mind or our tongue go a single step beyond the limits of God's word. For how can the human mind, which has not yet been able to discover what the body of the sun consists of, though it is set before the eye every day, bring down the boundless essence of God to its own little measure? Indeed, how can it, under its own guidance, reach a knowledge of the substance of God when it cannot understand its own? Let us therefore gladly leave to God the knowledge of himself. In the words of Hilary (On the Trinity, book 1), "He alone is a fit witness to himself who is known only by himself." If, then, we would leave this knowledge to God, we must conceive of him as he has made himself known, and in our inquiries turn to no other source than his word. On this subject we have five homilies of Chrysostom against the Anomoeans (On the Incomprehensible Nature of God), in which he tried, but in vain, to check the presumption of the sophists and to curb their talkativeness. They showed no more modesty here than they commonly do in everything else. The very unhappy results of their rashness should warn us to bring more teachableness than sharpness to the discussion of this question, never to try to search for God anywhere but in his sacred word, and never to speak or think of him further than we have that word for our guide. But if the distinction of Father, Son, and Spirit subsisting in the one Godhead (certainly a subject of great difficulty) gives more trouble and vexation to some minds than is fitting, let us remember that the human mind enters a labyrinth whenever it indulges its curiosity, and so let us submit to be guided by the divine oracles, however far the mystery may be beyond our reach.

22¶

It would be tedious, and laborious to no purpose, to draw up a catalog of the errors by which the purity of the faith has been assailed on this branch of doctrine. Most heretics, with their gross ravings, have made a general attack on the glory of God, thinking it enough if they could disturb and shake the unwary. From a few individuals numerous sects have sprung up, some of them tearing apart the divine essence, and others confusing the distinction of Persons. But if we hold what has already been demonstrated from Scripture, that the essence of the one God, belonging to the Father, Son, and Spirit, is simple and indivisible, and again, that the Father differs in some special property from the Son, and the Son from the Spirit, the door will be shut against Arius and Sabellius, as well as the other ancient authors of error. But since in our day certain frantic men have arisen, such as Servetus and others, who have thrown everything into confusion with new devices, it may be worthwhile to discuss their fallacies briefly.

The name of Trinity was so disliked, indeed detested, by Servetus that he charged all whom he called Trinitarians with being atheists. I say nothing of the insulting terms in which he saw fit to make his charges. The sum of his speculations was that a threefold deity is introduced wherever three Persons are said to exist in his essence, and that this Triad was imaginary, since it was inconsistent with the unity of God. At the same time, he would have it that the Persons are certain external ideas which do not truly subsist in the divine essence, but only picture God to us under this or that form: that at first, indeed, there was no distinction in God, because originally the Word was the same as the Spirit, but that ever since Christ came forth God of God, another Spirit, also a God, had proceeded from him. But although he sometimes cloaks his absurdities in allegory, as when he says that the eternal Word of God was the Spirit of Christ with God, and the reflection of the idea, and likewise that the Spirit was a shadow of deity, in the end he reduces the deity of both to nothing, maintaining that, according to the manner of distribution, there is a part of God in the Son as well as in the Spirit, just as the same Spirit is substantially a portion of God in us, and also in wood and stone. His absurd babbling about the person of the Mediator will be seen in its own place. See Calvin, Defense of the Orthodox Faith of the Holy Trinity against the Monstrous Errors of M. Servetus.

The monstrous fiction that a Person is nothing else than a visible appearance of the glory of God needs no long refutation. For when John declares that before the world was created the Logos was God (John 1:1), he shows that he was something very different from an idea. But if even then, and from the remotest eternity, that Logos who was God was with the Father, and had his own distinct and peculiar glory with the Father (John 17:5), he certainly could not have been an external or figurative splendor, but must necessarily have been a hypostasis that dwelt inherently in God himself. And although no mention is made of the Spirit before the account of the creation, he is not introduced there as a shadow, but as the essential power of God, where Moses relates that the shapeless mass was upheld by him (Gen. 1:2). It is obvious that the eternal Spirit always existed in God, since he cherished and sustained the confused materials of heaven and earth before they had order or beauty. Surely he could not then have been an image or representation of God, as Servetus dreams. But elsewhere he is forced to disclose his impiety more openly, when he says that God, by his eternal reason decreeing a Son for himself, in this way took on a visible appearance. For if this is true, no other deity is left to Christ than what is implied in his having been ordained a Son by God's eternal decree. Moreover, those phantoms that Servetus substitutes for the hypostases he transforms in such a way as to introduce new changes in God. But the most execrable heresy of all is his mixing up both the Son and the Spirit indiscriminately with all the creatures. For he plainly asserts that there are parts and divisions in the essence of God, and that every such portion is God. This he does especially when he says that the spirits of the faithful are coeternal and consubstantial with God, although elsewhere he assigns a substantial deity not only to the soul of man but to all created things.

23¶

This same pool has bred another monster not unlike the first. For certain restless spirits, unwilling to share the disgrace and reproach of Servetus's impiety, have confessed that there were indeed three Persons, but added as the reason that the Father, who alone is truly and properly God, poured his deity into the Son and the Spirit when he formed them. Nor do they refrain from expressing themselves in such shocking terms as these: that the Father is essentially distinguished from the Son and the Spirit by this, that he is the only essentiator. Their first pretext for this is that Christ is regularly called the Son of God. From this they infer that there is no proper God but the Father. But they forget that although the name of God is common to the Son as well, it is sometimes ascribed to the Father by way of preeminence, as the source and principle of deity; and this is done in order to mark the simple unity of essence. They object that if the Son is truly God, he must be regarded as the Son of a person, which is absurd. I answer that both are true: namely, that he is the Son of God, because he is the Word, begotten of the Father before all ages (for we are not now speaking of the Person of the Mediator); and yet that, for the sake of explanation, regard must be had to the Person, so that the name God may be understood not in its absolute sense, but as equivalent to Father. For if we hold that there is no other God than the Father, this rank is plainly denied to the Son.

Wherever the Godhead is mentioned, we are by no means to admit that there is an opposition between the Father and the Son, as if the name of God could properly be applied to the former alone. For certainly the God who appeared to Isaiah was the one true God, and yet John declares that he was Christ (Isa. 6; John 12:41). He who declared by the mouth of Isaiah that he would be "for a stone of stumbling" to the Jews was the one God; and yet Paul declares that he was Christ (Isa. 8:14; Rom. 9:33). He who proclaims through Isaiah, "Unto me every knee shall bow," is the one God; yet Paul again explains that he is Christ (Isa. 45:23; Rom. 14:11). To this we may add the passages quoted by an apostle: "Thou, Lord, hast laid the foundations of the earth"; "Let all the angels of God worship him" (Heb. 1:10; 10:6; Ps. 102:26; 97:7). All these apply to the one God; and yet the Apostle contends that they are the proper attributes of Christ. There is nothing to the cavil that what properly belongs to God is transferred to Christ because he is the brightness of his glory. Since the name of Jehovah is everywhere applied to Christ, it follows that, with respect to deity, he is of himself. For if he is Jehovah, it is impossible to deny that he is the same God who elsewhere proclaims through Isaiah, "I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God" (Is. 44:6). We would also do well to weigh the words of Jeremiah, "The gods that have not made the heavens and the earth, even they shall perish from the earth and from under these heavens" (Jer. 10:11); from which it follows conversely that he whose deity Isaiah repeatedly proves from the creation of the world is none other than the Son of God. 24¶

The assumption that whenever God is mentioned absolutely the Father alone is meant can be proved false by many passages. Even in the passages they quote in support of their views they show a lamentable inconsistency, because the name of Son occurs there by way of contrast, which shows that the other name, God, is used relatively and is in that way confined to the person of the Father. Their objection can be disposed of in a single word. If the Father were not the only true God, he would, they say, be his own Father. But there is nothing absurd in the name of God being applied specially, with respect to order and degree, to him who not only begat his own wisdom of himself, but is the God of the Mediator, as I will show more fully in its own place. For ever since Christ was manifested in the flesh he is called the Son of God, not only because he was the Eternal Word, begotten of the Father before all worlds, but because he took on the person and office of the Mediator in order to unite us to God. Since they are so bold in excluding the Son from the honor of God, I would like to know whether, when he declares that there is "none good but one, that is, God," he deprives himself of goodness. I am not speaking of his human nature, lest they object that whatever goodness was in it was derived as a free gift. I ask whether the Eternal Word of God is good, yes or no. If they say no, their impiety is obvious; if yes, they refute themselves. Christ's seeming at first glance to refuse the name of good (Mt. 19:17) rather confirms our view. Since goodness is the special property of God alone, and yet was at that time applied to him in the ordinary manner of greeting, his rejection of false honor indicates that the goodness in which he excels is divine. Again, I ask whether, when Paul affirms that God alone is "immortal," "wise, and true" (1 Tim. 1:17), he reduces Christ to the rank of beings that are mortal, foolish, and false. Is he not immortal who from the beginning had life so as to bestow immortality on angels? Is he not wise who is the eternal wisdom of God? Is he not true who is truth itself?

I ask, moreover, whether they think Christ should be worshiped. If he rightly claims that every knee shall bow to him, it follows that he is the God who in the law forbade worship to be offered to any but himself. If they insist on applying to the Father alone the words of Isaiah, "I am, and besides me there is none else" (Is. 44:6), I turn the passage against them, since we see that every property of God is attributed to Christ. The French is expressed somewhat differently, "veu que l'Apostre en l'allegant de Christ, lui attribue tout ce qu' est de Dieu;" seeing that the Apostle, by applying it to Christ, attributes to him everything that belongs to God.

Nor does another cavil help them, that Christ was God in his Father. For though we admit that, with respect to order and degree, the beginning of deity is in the Father, we hold it a detestable fiction to maintain that essence is proper to the Father alone, as if he were the deifier of the Son. On this view either the essence is manifold, or Christ is God only in name and imagination. If they grant that the Son is God, but only in subordination to the Father, then the essence which in the Father is unformed and unbegotten will in him be formed and begotten. I know that many who would like to be thought wise ridicule us for drawing the distinction of persons from the words of Moses when he presents God as saying, "Let us make man in our own image" (Gen. 1:26). Godly readers, however, see how coldly and absurdly Moses would have introduced this conversation if there were not several persons in the Godhead. It is certain that those whom the Father addresses must have been uncreated. But nothing is uncreated except the one God. Now then, unless they concede that the power of creating was common to the Father, Son, and Spirit, and the power of commanding common as well, it will follow that God did not speak this way inwardly with himself, but addressed other builders outside himself. In short, there is a single passage that will dispose of both these objections at once. Christ's declaration that "God is a Spirit" (John 4:24) cannot be confined to the Father alone, as if the Word were not of a spiritual nature. But if the name Spirit applies to the Son just as to the Father, I conclude that the Son is included under the unqualified name of God. He adds immediately after that the only worshipers approved by the Father are those who worship him in spirit and in truth; and from this I also conclude that because Christ performs the office of teacher under a head, he applies the name God to the Father, not in order to destroy his own deity, but in order to raise us up to it, as it were, step by step.

25¶

The delusion lies in dreaming of individuals, each of whom possesses a part of the essence. The Scriptures teach that there is essentially but one God, and therefore that the essence of both the Son and the Spirit is unbegotten; but since the Father is first in order, and of himself begat his own Wisdom, he, as we observed a little earlier, is rightly regarded as the principle and fountain of the whole Godhead. So God, taken without qualification, is unbegotten, and the Father, with respect to his person, is unbegotten. For it is absurd to imagine that our doctrine gives any ground for alleging that we set up a quaternity of gods. They falsely and slanderously ascribe to us the invention of their own brain, as if we in effect held that three persons flow out from one essence. 26¶

To the objection that if Christ is properly God, he is improperly called the Son of God, the answer has already been given: when one person is compared with another, the name God is not used without qualification, but is restricted to the Father, regarded as the beginning of the Godhead, not by essentiating, as the fanatics absurdly put it, but with respect to order. In this sense we are to understand the words Christ addressed to the Father, "This is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom thou hast sent" (John 17:3). For speaking in the person of the Mediator, he holds a middle place between God and man, yet in such a way that his majesty is not diminished by it. For though he humbled (emptied) himself, he did not lose the glory he had with the Father, though it was hidden from the world. So in the Epistle to the Hebrews (Heb. 1:10; 2:9), though the apostle confesses that Christ was made a little lower than the angels, he does not hesitate at the same time to assert that he is the eternal God who founded the earth. We must hold, therefore, that whenever Christ, in the character of Mediator, addresses the Father, he includes his own deity also under the term God. So when he says to the apostles, "It is expedient for you that I go away," "My Father is greater than I," he does not attribute to himself a merely secondary deity, as if with respect to eternal essence he were inferior to the Father; but having obtained heavenly glory, he gathers the faithful together to share it with him. He places the Father in the higher degree, because the full perfection of brightness that shines in heaven differs from the measure of glory he himself displayed when clothed in flesh. For the same reason Paul says that Christ will deliver up "the kingdom to God, even the Father," "that God may be all in all" (1 Cor. 15:24, 28). Nothing could be more absurd than to deny the permanence of Christ's deity. But if he will never cease to be the Son of God, but will always remain the same as he was from the beginning, it follows that under the name of Father the one divine essence common to both is comprehended. And certainly Christ came down to us for the very purpose of raising us to the Father, and by that, at the same time, raising us to himself, since he is one with the Father. It is therefore false and impious to confine the name of God to the Father in such a way as to deny it to the Son. Accordingly, John, in declaring that he is the true God, has no thought of placing him beneath the Father in a subordinate rank of deity. I wonder what these fabricators of new gods mean when they confess that Christ is truly God, and yet exclude him from the Godhead of the Father, as if there could be any true God but the one God, or as if transfused deity were anything but a modern fiction.

27¶

In the many passages they collect from Irenaeus, in which he maintains that the Father of Christ is the only eternal God of Israel, they display shameful ignorance or very great dishonesty. For they ought to have noticed that this holy man was contending against certain frantic persons who denied that the Father of Christ was the God who had spoken in former times by Moses and the prophets, and held that he was some phantom or other produced from the pollution of the world. His whole aim, therefore, is to make it plain that in the Scriptures no other God is proclaimed but the Father of Christ, and that it is wicked to imagine any other. Accordingly, there is nothing strange in his concluding so often that the God of Israel was no other than he who is celebrated by Christ and the apostles. Now, when a different heresy is to be resisted, we also say truly that the God who appeared to the fathers in former times was no other than Christ. Moreover, if it is objected that he was the Father, we have the answer ready that while we contend for the deity of the Son, we by no means exclude the Father. When the reader pays attention to the purpose of Irenaeus, the dispute is at an end. Indeed, we have only to look at book 3, chapter 6, where the godly writer insists on this one point, "that he who in Scripture is called God absolutely and without qualification is truly the only God; and that Christ is called God absolutely." Let us remember (as appears from the whole work, and especially from book 2, chapter 46) that the point under discussion was that the name of Father is not applied in a riddling and parabolic way to one who was not truly God. We may add that in book 3, chapter 9, he contends that the Son, united with the Father, was the God proclaimed by the prophets and apostles. He afterward explains (book 3, chapter 12) how Christ, who is Lord of all, and King and Judge, received power from him who is God of all, namely, with respect to the humiliation by which he humbled himself, even to the death of the cross. At the same time, shortly after, he affirms (book 3, chapter 16) that the Son is the maker of heaven and earth, who gave the law by the hand of Moses and appeared to the fathers. If any babbler should now insist that, according to Irenaeus, the Father alone is the God of Israel, I will refer him to a passage in which Irenaeus plainly says (book 3, chapters 18, 23) that Christ is always one and the same, and also applies to Christ the words of Habakkuk's prophecy, "God cometh from the south." To the same effect he says (book 4, chapter 9), "Therefore Christ himself, with the Father, is the God of the living." And in the twelfth chapter of the same book he explains that Abraham believed God, because Christ is the maker of heaven and earth, and very God.

28¶

With no more truth do they claim Tertullian as their patron. 29¶

Certainly, whoever will compare the writings of the ancient fathers with one another will find nothing in Irenaeus different from what is taught by those who came after him. Justin is one of the most ancient, and he agrees with us entirely. Let them object that by him and others the Father of Christ is called the one God. The same thing is taught by Hilary, who uses the still harsher expression that Eternity is in the Father. Is this so that he may withhold the divine essence from the Son? His whole work is a defense of the doctrine we maintain; and yet these men are not ashamed to bring forward some sort of mutilated excerpts in order to persuade us that Hilary is a patron of their heresy. As to what they allege from Ignatius, if they would have it carry the least weight, let them prove that the apostles made laws concerning Lent and other corruptions. Nothing could be more nauseating than the absurdities that have been published under the name of Ignatius; and therefore the conduct of those who furnish themselves with such masks for deception deserves all the less toleration.

Moreover, the agreement of the ancient fathers appears clearly from this, that at the Council of Nicaea Arius made no attempt to cloak his heresy with the authority of any approved author; and no Greek or Latin writer apologizes for dissenting from his predecessors. It hardly needs to be said how carefully Augustine, to whom all these wretches are most violently opposed, examined all the ancient writings, and how reverently he embraced the doctrine they taught (Augustine, On the Trinity, etc.). He is most scrupulous in stating the grounds on which he is compelled to differ from them, even in the smallest point. On this subject, too, if he finds anything ambiguous or obscure in other writers, he does not hide it. Athanasius expresses himself thus, learnedly and piously: "On this subject, though you cannot explain yourself, you are not therefore to distrust the Holy Scriptures. It is better, while hesitating through ignorance, to be silent and believe, than not to believe because you hesitate."
