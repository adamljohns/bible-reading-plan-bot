1¶

After God has once received us into his family, it is not so that he may regard us as servants but as sons, acting the part of a kind and careful parent, and providing for our maintenance through the whole course of our lives. And, not content with this, he has been pleased to assure us of his continued generosity by a pledge. To this end he has given another sacrament to his Church by the hand of his only-begotten Son, namely, a spiritual feast, at which Christ testifies that he himself is the living bread (John 6:51), on which our souls feed for a true and blessed immortality. Now the knowledge of this great mystery is most necessary, and in proportion to its importance it demands an accurate exposition. Satan, in order to rob the Church of this priceless treasure, long ago brought in first mists and then darkness to obscure its light, and stirred up strife and contention to turn the minds of the simple away from a relish for this sacred food; and in our own age he has tried the same trick. I will therefore first give a simple summary suited to the capacity of the unlearned, and then go on to explain the difficulties by which Satan has tried to ensnare the world. First, then, the signs are bread and wine, which represent the invisible food that we receive from the body and blood of Christ. For as God, regenerating us in baptism, engrafts us into the fellowship of his Church and makes us his own by adoption, so we have said that he performs the office of a provident parent in continually supplying the food by which he may sustain and preserve us in the life to which he has begotten us by his word. Moreover, Christ is the only food of our soul, and therefore our heavenly Father invites us to him, so that, refreshed by communion with him, we may again and again gather new vigor until we reach the heavenly immortality. But since this mystery of the secret union of Christ with believers is by nature incomprehensible, he displays its figure and image in visible signs suited to our capacity. Indeed, by giving us, as it were, pledges and tokens, he makes it as certain to us as if it were seen with the eye; for the familiarity of the comparison gives it access to minds however dull, and shows that souls are fed by Christ just as the bodily life is sustained by bread and wine. We now understand, therefore, the end which this mystical blessing has in view, namely, to assure us that the body of Christ was once sacrificed for us, so that we may now eat it, and in eating feel within ourselves the efficacy of that one sacrifice; and that his blood was once shed for us so as to be our perpetual drink. This is the force of the promise that is added, "Take, eat; this is my body, which is broken for you" (Matthew 26:26, etc.). We are commanded to take and eat the body which was once offered for our salvation, so that, seeing ourselves made partakers of it, we may safely conclude that the power of that death will be effective in us. Hence he calls the cup the covenant in his blood. For the covenant which he once ratified by his blood he in a manner renews, or rather continues, so far as the confirmation of our faith is concerned, as often as he holds out his sacred blood to us as drink.

2¶

Godly souls can draw great confidence and delight from this sacrament, since it is a testimony that they form one body with Christ, so that everything that is his they may call their own. Hence it follows that we can confidently assure ourselves that eternal life, of which he himself is the heir, is ours, and that the kingdom of heaven, into which he has entered, can no more be taken from us than from him. On the other hand, it follows that we cannot be condemned for our sins, from the guilt of which he absolves us, since he has been pleased to have them imputed to himself as if they were his own. This is the wondrous exchange made by his boundless goodness. Having become with us the Son of Man, he has made us with himself sons of God. By his own descent to the earth he has prepared our ascent to heaven. Having received our mortality, he has bestowed on us his immortality. Having taken on our weakness, he has made us strong in his strength. Having submitted to our poverty, he has transferred to us his riches. Having taken upon himself the burden of unrighteousness with which we were oppressed, he has clothed us with his righteousness.

3¶

To all these things we have a complete attestation in this sacrament, which enables us to conclude with certainty that they are as truly displayed to us as if Christ were placed in bodily presence before our eyes, or handled by our hands. For these are words which can never lie or deceive: Take, eat, drink. This is my body, which is broken for you; this is my blood, which is shed for the remission of sins. In bidding us take, he signifies that it is ours. In bidding us eat, he signifies that it becomes one substance with us. In affirming of his body that it was broken, and of his blood that it was shed for us, he shows that both were not so much his own as ours, because he took and laid down both, not for his own advantage, but for our salvation. And we ought carefully to observe that the chief, and almost the whole, force of the sacrament lies in these words: It is broken for you; it is shed for you. It would not matter much to us that the body and blood of the Lord are now distributed, had they not once been given up for our redemption and salvation. They are therefore represented under bread and wine, so that we may learn that they are not only ours, but are meant to nourish our spiritual life. That is, as we observed before, by the bodily things which are set forth in the sacrament we are led by a kind of analogy to spiritual things. Thus when bread is given as a symbol of the body of Christ, we must at once think of this comparison: as bread nourishes, sustains, and protects our bodily life, so the body of Christ is the only food that invigorates and keeps alive the soul. When we see wine set forth as a symbol of blood, we must think that whatever use wine serves for the body, the same is spiritually bestowed by the blood of Christ; and that use is to foster, refresh, strengthen, and gladden. For if we rightly consider what profit we have gained by the breaking of his sacred body and the shedding of his blood, we shall clearly see that these properties of bread and wine, according to this analogy, represent it most fittingly when they are communicated to us.

4¶

Therefore the principal part of a sacrament is not simply to hold out the body of Christ to us without any higher consideration, but rather to seal and confirm that promise by which he testifies that his flesh is meat indeed and his blood drink indeed, nourishing us to eternal life, and by which he affirms that he is the bread of life, of which whoever eats shall live forever. I say, it is to seal and confirm that promise, and in order to do so it sends us to the cross of Christ, where that promise was performed and fulfilled in all its parts. For we do not eat Christ rightly and savingly except as crucified, when with a lively apprehension we perceive the efficacy of his death. When he called himself the bread of life, he did not take that title from the sacrament, as some perversely interpret it. Rather, such as he was given to us by the Father, such he showed himself to be when, becoming partaker of our human mortality, he made us partakers of his divine immortality; when, offering himself in sacrifice, he took our curse upon himself so that he might cover us with his blessing; when by his death he devoured and swallowed up death; and when in his resurrection he raised our corruptible flesh, which he had put on, to glory and incorruption.

5¶

It only remains that the whole become ours by application. This is done by means of the gospel, and more clearly by the sacred Supper, where Christ offers himself to us with all his blessings, and we receive him in faith. The sacrament, therefore, does not make Christ become the bread of life for the first time. Rather, while it calls to remembrance that Christ was made the bread of life so that we may constantly eat him, it gives us a taste and relish for that bread and makes us feel its efficacy. For it assures us, first, that whatever Christ did or suffered was done to give us life; and secondly, that this quickening is eternal, so that by it we are ceaselessly nourished, sustained, and preserved in life. For as Christ would not have been the bread of life to us if he had not been born, and had not died and risen again, so he could not now be the bread of life if the efficacy and fruit of his birth, death, and resurrection were not eternal. All this Christ has elegantly expressed in these words, "The bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world" (John 6:51). Doubtless he signifies that his body will be as bread for the spiritual life of the soul, because it was to be delivered to death for our salvation, and that he extends it to us as food when he makes us partakers of it by faith. He therefore gave himself once that he might become bread, when he gave himself to be crucified for the redemption of the world; and he gives himself daily, when in the word of the gospel he offers himself to be partaken of by us as the one who was crucified, when he seals that offer by the sacred mystery of the Supper, and when he accomplishes inwardly what he outwardly signifies. Moreover, two faults are to be avoided here. We must neither, by setting too little value on the signs, sever them from the meanings to which they are in some degree joined, nor, by extolling them beyond measure, seem in some way to obscure the mysteries themselves. That Christ is the bread of life by which believers are nourished to eternal life, no man is so utterly without religion as not to acknowledge. But not all are agreed as to the mode of partaking of him. For there are some who define the eating of the flesh of Christ and the drinking of his blood to be, in a word, nothing more than believing in Christ himself. But Christ seems to me to have intended to teach something more definite and more sublime in that noble discourse in which he commends the eating of his flesh, namely, that we are quickened by the true partaking of him, which he designated by the terms eating and drinking, so that no one might suppose that the life we obtain from him is obtained by mere knowledge. For as it is not the sight but the eating of bread that gives nourishment to the body, so the soul must partake of Christ truly and thoroughly, so that by his power it may grow up into spiritual life. Meanwhile, we admit that this is nothing else than the eating of faith, and that no other eating can be imagined. But there is this difference between their way of speaking and mine. According to them, to eat is merely to believe; while I maintain that the flesh of Christ is eaten by believing, because it is made ours by faith, and that this eating is the effect and fruit of faith. Or, to put it more plainly, according to them eating is faith, whereas to me it seems rather to be a consequence of faith. The difference is small in words, but not small in substance. For although the apostle teaches that Christ dwells in our hearts by faith (Ephesians 3:17), no one will interpret that dwelling to be faith itself. All see that it explains the wonderful effect of faith, because it is owing to faith that believers have Christ dwelling in them. In this way the Lord was pleased, by calling himself the bread of life, to teach not only that our salvation is stored up in faith in his death and resurrection, but also that, by virtue of true communication with him, his life passes into us and becomes ours, just as bread taken for food gives vigor to the body.

6¶

When Augustine, whom they claim as their patron, wrote that we eat by believing, all he meant was to indicate that this eating is of faith and not of the mouth. This I do not deny; but at the same time I add that by faith we embrace Christ, not as appearing at a distance, but as uniting himself to us, he being our head and we his members. I do not absolutely disapprove of that way of speaking; I only deny that it is a full interpretation, if they mean by it to define what it is to eat the flesh of Christ. I see that Augustine repeatedly used this form of expression, as when he said (On Christian Doctrine, book 3) that "Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man" is a figurative expression commanding us to have communion with our Lord's passion, and sweetly and profitably to treasure in our memory that his flesh was crucified and wounded for us. So also when he says, "These three thousand men who were converted at the preaching of Peter (Acts 2:41), by believing, drank the blood which they had cruelly shed." See Augustine, Homilies on John 31 and 40, etc.; Chrysostom, Homilies to the People of Antioch, 60, 61; and Homily on Mark 89.

7¶

I am not satisfied with the view of those who, while acknowledging that we have some kind of communion with Christ, make us partakers only of the Spirit, and omit all mention of flesh and blood. As if it had been said to no purpose at all that his flesh is meat indeed and his blood is drink indeed; that we have no life unless we eat that flesh and drink that blood; and so on. Therefore, since it is evident that full communion with Christ goes beyond their description, which is too narrow, I will attempt briefly to show how far it extends, before going on to speak of the opposite fault of excess. For I shall have a longer dispute with those extravagant doctors who, according to their gross notions, invent an absurd mode of eating and drinking, and who strip Christ of his flesh and transform him into a phantom. That is, if it is even lawful to put this great mystery into words, a mystery which I feel, and therefore freely confess, that I am unable to comprehend with my mind; so far am I from wishing anyone to measure its sublimity by my feeble capacity. Indeed, I rather urge my readers not to confine their understanding within those too narrow limits, but to try to rise much higher than I can lead them. For whenever this subject is considered, after I have done my utmost, I feel that I have spoken far beneath its dignity. And though the mind is more powerful in thought than the tongue in expression, it too is overcome and overwhelmed by the greatness of the subject. All that remains, then, is to break forth in wonder at the mystery, which plainly the mind is unable to comprehend or the tongue to express. I will, however, give a summary of my view as best I can, not doubting its truth, and therefore trusting that it will not be disapproved by godly hearts.

8¶

First of all, we are taught by the Scriptures that Christ was from the beginning the living Word of the Father, the fountain and origin of life, from which all things were always to receive life. Hence John at one time calls him the Word of life, and at another says that in him was life, signifying that even then he pervaded all creatures and instilled into them the power of breathing and living. He afterward adds that the life was at length manifested, when the Son of God, taking our nature, displayed himself in bodily form to be seen and handled. For although he had previously poured out his power into the creatures, yet since man, alienated from God by sin, had lost the communication of life and saw death hanging over him on every side, he had to be restored to the communion of that Word in order to regain the hope of immortality. How little confidence can it give you to know that the Word of God, from whom you are at the greatest distance, contains within himself the fullness of life, while in yourself, whichever way you turn, you see nothing but death? But ever since that fountain of life began to dwell in our nature, he no longer lies hidden far from us, but displays himself openly for us to partake of him. Indeed, the very flesh in which he dwells he makes life-giving to us, so that by partaking of it we may feed for immortality. "I," he says, "am that bread of life"; "I am the living bread which came down from heaven"; "And the bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world" (John 6:48, 51). By these words he declares not only that he is life, inasmuch as he is the eternal Word of God who came down to us from heaven, but that by coming down he gave vigor to the flesh which he assumed, so that from it a communication of life to us might flow. Hence, too, he adds that his flesh is meat indeed and his blood is drink indeed; by this food believers are nourished to eternal life. The godly, therefore, have wonderful comfort in this, that they now find life in their own flesh. For they not only reach it by easy access, but have it freely set before them. Let them only throw open the door of their hearts to take it into their embrace, and they will obtain it.

9¶

The flesh of Christ, however, does not have such power in itself as to make us live, since by its own first condition it was subject to mortality, and even now, when endowed with immortality, it does not live by itself. Still it is properly said to be life-giving, because it is filled with the fullness of life for the purpose of passing it on to us. In this sense I understand our Savior's words as Cyril interprets them, "As the Father has life in himself, so has he given to the Son to have life in himself" (John 5:26). For there he is properly speaking not of the properties which he possessed with the Father from the beginning, but of those with which he was invested in the flesh in which he appeared. Accordingly, he shows that in his humanity also the fullness of life resides, so that everyone who communicates in his flesh and blood at the same time enjoys participation in life. The nature of this may be explained by a familiar example. Water is at one time drunk from the fountain, at another drawn, at another led away by channels to irrigate the fields; and yet it does not flow forth of itself for all these uses, but is taken from its source, which with perennial flow continually sends out a new and sufficient supply. So the flesh of Christ is like a rich and inexhaustible fountain, which pours into us the life flowing into it from the Godhead. Now who does not see that the communion of the flesh and blood of Christ is necessary to all who aspire to the heavenly life? Hence those passages of the apostle: the Church is the "body" of Christ, his "fullness." He is "the head," "from whom the whole body, fitly joined together and compacted by that which every joint supplies," "makes increase of the body" (Ephesians 1:23; 4:15, 16). Our bodies are the "members of Christ" (1 Corinthians 6:15). We see that all these things cannot possibly take place unless he cleaves to us wholly in body and spirit. But the very close connection which unites us to his flesh he illustrated with still more splendid terms when he said that we "are members of his body, of his flesh, and of his bones" (Ephesians 5:30). At length, to testify that the matter is too high for utterance, he concludes by exclaiming, "This is a great mystery" (Ephesians 5:32). It would therefore be extreme folly not to acknowledge the communion of believers with the body and blood of the Lord, a communion which the apostle declares to be so great that he chooses rather to marvel at it than to explain it.

10¶

The sum is that the flesh and blood of Christ feed our souls just as bread and wine maintain and support our bodily life. For there would be no fitness in the sign if our souls did not find their nourishment in Christ. And this could not be unless Christ truly became one with us and refreshed us by the eating of his flesh and the drinking of his blood. But though it seems an incredible thing that the flesh of Christ, while at so great a distance from us in place, should be food to us, let us remember how far the secret power of the Holy Spirit surpasses all our conceptions, and how foolish it is to try to measure its immensity by our feeble capacity. What our mind does not comprehend, therefore, let faith conceive, namely, that the Spirit truly unites things separated by space. That sacred communion of flesh and blood by which Christ pours his life into us, just as if it penetrated our bones and marrow, he testifies and seals in the Supper; and he does so not by presenting a vain or empty sign, but by exerting there an efficacy of the Spirit by which he fulfills what he promises. And truly he displays and offers the thing there signified to all who sit down at that spiritual feast, although it is received with benefit by believers only, who receive this great benefit with true faith and heartfelt gratitude. For this reason the apostle said, "The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, is it not the communion of the body of Christ?" (1 Corinthians 10:16). There is no ground to object that the expression is figurative and gives the sign the name of the thing signified. I admit, indeed, that the breaking of bread is a symbol, not the reality. But granting this, we rightly infer from the displaying of the symbol that the thing itself is displayed. For unless we would charge God with deceit, we will never presume to say that he holds out an empty symbol. Therefore, if by the breaking of bread the Lord truly represents the partaking of his body, there ought to be no doubt whatever that he truly presents and performs it. The rule which the godly ought always to observe is this: whenever they see the symbols instituted by the Lord, they should think and be surely persuaded that the truth of the thing signified is also present. For why does the Lord put the symbol of his body into your hands, except to assure you that you truly partake of him? If this is true, let us feel as fully assured that the visible sign is given to us as a seal of an invisible gift as that his body itself is given to us.

11¶

I hold, then (as has always been received in the Church, and is still taught by those who think rightly), that the sacred mystery of the Supper consists of two things: the bodily signs, which, presented to the eye, represent invisible things in a manner suited to our weak capacity; and the spiritual truth, which is at once figured and displayed by the signs. When I try to explain its nature in a familiar way, I am accustomed to set down three things: the thing meant, the matter which depends on it, and the power or efficacy which follows from both. The thing meant consists in the promises, which are in a manner included in the sign. By the matter, or substance, I mean Christ, with his death and resurrection. By the effect I understand redemption, justification, sanctification, eternal life, and all the other benefits which Christ bestows upon us. Moreover, though all these things have respect to faith, I leave no room for the quibble that when I say Christ is received by faith, I mean that he is received only by the intellect and imagination. He is offered by the promises, not that we may stop short at the sight or mere knowledge of him, but that we may enjoy true communion with him. And indeed I do not see how anyone can expect to have redemption and righteousness in the cross of Christ, and life in his death, without relying first of all on true communion with Christ himself. Those blessings could not reach us unless Christ first made himself ours. I say, then, that in the mystery of the Supper, by the symbols of bread and wine, Christ, his body and his blood, are truly displayed to us, in which he fulfilled all obedience in order to procure righteousness for us: first, that we might become one body with him; and secondly, that being made partakers of his substance, we might feel the result of this in the participation of all his blessings.

12¶

I now come to the extravagant mixtures which superstition has brought in. Here Satan has used all his wiles, drawing the minds of men away from heaven and filling them with the perverse error that Christ is attached to the element of bread. And first, we are not to dream of such a presence of Christ in the sacrament as the craftsmen of the Roman court have imagined, as if the body of Christ, locally present, were to be taken into the hand, chewed by the teeth, and swallowed by the throat. This was the form of recantation which Pope Nicholas dictated to Berengarius as a token of his repentance, a form expressed in terms so monstrous that the author of the Gloss exclaims that there is danger, if the reader is not especially careful, that he will be led by it into a worse heresy than that of Berengarius (Distinction 2, chapter Ego Berengarius). Peter Lombard, though he labors hard to excuse the absurdity, rather inclines to a different opinion. Since we cannot at all doubt that the body of Christ is bounded according to the unvarying rule of the human body, and is contained in heaven, where it was once received and will remain until it returns to judgment, we judge it altogether unlawful to bring it back under these corruptible elements, or to imagine it present everywhere. And indeed there is no need of this in order for us to partake of it, since the Lord by his Spirit bestows on us the blessing of being one with him in soul, body, and spirit. The bond of that union, therefore, is the Spirit of Christ, who joins us to him, and is a kind of channel by which everything that Christ has and is flows down to us. For if we see that the sun, in sending its rays upon the earth to generate, cherish, and invigorate its offspring, in a manner pours its substance into it, why should the radiance of the Spirit be less able to convey to us the communion of his flesh and blood? Therefore Scripture, when it speaks of our participation in Christ, attributes its whole efficacy to the Spirit. Instead of many passages, one will suffice. Paul, in the Epistle to the Romans (Romans 8:9-11), shows that the only way in which Christ dwells in us is by his Spirit. By this, however, he does not take away that communion of flesh and blood of which we are now speaking, but shows that it is owing to the Spirit alone that we possess Christ wholly and have him abiding in us.

13¶

The Schoolmen, horrified at this barbarous impiety, speak more modestly, though they do nothing more than amuse themselves with subtler delusions. They admit that Christ is not contained in the sacrament circumscriptively, or in a bodily manner; but afterward they devise a mode which they themselves do not understand and cannot explain to others. It comes to this, however: that Christ is to be sought in what they call the species of bread. What then? When they say that the substance of bread is converted into Christ, do they not attach him to the white color, which is all they leave of it? But they say that, though contained in the sacrament, he still remains in heaven, and has no other presence there than that of dwelling. But whatever terms they use to put a gloss on it, the sum of all is that what was formerly bread becomes Christ by consecration, so that Christ thereafter lies hidden under the color of bread. This they are not ashamed to state plainly. For Lombard's words are, "The body of Christ, which is visible in itself, lurks and lies covered after the act of consecration under the species of bread" (Lombard, Sentences, book 4, distinction 12). Thus the figure of the bread is nothing but a mask which hides the flesh from our sight. But there is no need of many conjectures to detect the snare which they meant to lay by these words, since the thing itself speaks plainly. It is easy to see how great a superstition not only the common people but the leaders also have labored under for many ages, and still labor under, in the papal churches. Caring little about true faith (by which alone we attain to the fellowship of Christ and become one with him), they think he is sufficiently present provided they have his carnal presence, which they have fabricated without any authority from the word. And yet those who put forth such monstrous dogmas, so far from being ashamed of the disgrace, assail us with venomous invectives for not subscribing to them.

18¶

But if we assume that the body and blood of Christ are attached to the bread and wine, then the one must necessarily be severed from the other. For as the bread is given separately from the cup, so the body, united to the bread, must be separated from the blood, enclosed in the cup. For since they affirm that the body is in the bread and the blood is in the cup, while the bread and wine are at some distance from each other in space, they cannot by any quibble escape the conclusion that the body must be separated from the blood. Their usual pretext, namely, that the blood is in the body, and the body in turn in the blood, by what they call concomitance, is worse than frivolous, since the symbols in which they are enclosed are thus kept distinct. But if we are carried up to heaven with our eyes and minds, that we may there behold Christ in the glory of his kingdom, then, as the symbols invite us to him whole and entire, so under the symbol of bread we must feed on his body, and under the symbol of wine drink separately of his blood, and so have the full enjoyment of him. For though he withdrew his flesh from us, and with his body ascended to heaven, he nevertheless sits at the right hand of the Father; that is, he reigns in the power and majesty and glory of the Father. This kingdom is not limited by any intervals of space, nor bounded by any dimensions. Christ can exert his power wherever he pleases, in earth and heaven; he can manifest his presence by the exercise of his power; he can always be present with his people, breathing into them his own life; he can live in them, sustain, confirm, and invigorate them, and keep them safe, just as if he were with them in the body. In short, he can feed them with his own body, the communion of which he pours into them. In this way the body and blood of Christ are presented to us in the sacrament.

19¶

The presence of Christ in the Supper we must hold to be such as neither fastens him to the element of bread, nor encloses him in bread, nor circumscribes him in any way (for this would plainly detract from his heavenly glory). And it must moreover be such as neither strips him of his proper dimensions, nor divides him by differences of place, nor assigns to him a body of boundless dimensions spread through heaven and earth. All these things are clearly contrary to his true human nature. Let us never allow ourselves to lose sight of these two restrictions. First, let nothing be said that detracts from the heavenly glory of Christ. This happens whenever he is brought under the corruptible elements of this world, or is fastened to any earthly creatures. Secondly, let no property be assigned to his body that is inconsistent with his human nature. This is done when it is either said to be infinite or made to occupy a variety of places at the same time. But when these absurdities are set aside, I willingly admit anything which helps to express the true and substantial communication of the body and blood of the Lord, as presented to believers under the sacred symbols of the Supper, understanding that they are received not merely by the imagination or intellect, but are enjoyed in reality as the food of eternal life. For the odium with which this view is regarded by the world, and the unjust prejudice incurred by its defense, there is no cause, unless it be the fearful bewitchments of Satan. What we teach on this subject is in perfect agreement with Scripture; it contains nothing absurd, obscure, or ambiguous; it is not unfavorable to true godliness and solid edification. In short, it has nothing in it to give offense, except that for some ages, while the ignorance and barbarism of the sophists reigned in the Church, the clear light and open truth were shamefully suppressed. And yet, since Satan, by means of turbulent spirits, is still in our own day exerting himself to the utmost to bring dishonor on this doctrine by every kind of slander and reproach, it is right to assert and defend it with the greatest care.

Before we go further, we must consider the ordinance itself as instituted by Christ, because the most plausible objection of our opponents is that we abandon his words. To free ourselves from the reproach with which they thus load us, the fittest course will be to begin with an interpretation of the words. Three Evangelists and Paul relate that our Savior took bread, and after giving thanks, broke it and gave it to his disciples, saying, Take, eat; this is my body, which is given, or broken, for you. Of the cup, Matthew and Mark say, "This is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many for the remission of sins" (Matthew 26:26; Mark 14:22). Luke and Paul say, "This cup is the new testament in my blood" (Luke 22:20; 1 Corinthians 11:25). The advocates of transubstantiation insist that by the pronoun "this" is denoted the appearance of bread, because the whole tenor of our Savior's address is an act of consecration, and there is no substance which can be pointed out. But if they hold so scrupulously to the words, then, since what our Savior gave to his disciples he declared to be his body, nothing is more foreign to the strict meaning of the words than the fiction that what was bread is now body. What Christ takes into his hands and gives to the apostles, he declares to be his body; but he had taken bread, and therefore who does not see that what is given is still bread? Hence nothing can be more absurd than to transfer what is affirmed of bread to the species of bread. Others, in interpreting the word "is" as equivalent to "is transubstantiated," resort to a gloss which is forced and violently twisted. They have no ground, therefore, for pretending that they are moved by reverence for the words. The use of the word "is" for being converted into something else is unknown to every language and nation. As for those who leave the bread in the Supper and affirm that it is the body of Christ, there is great diversity among them. To the reality which they affirm we have already referred, and will soon do so at greater length. But if they shun everything like metaphor, why do they leap from the simple pointing out made by Christ to modes of expression that are widely different? For there is a great difference between saying that the bread is the body and saying that the body is with the bread. But seeing it impossible to maintain the simple proposition that the bread is the body, they tried to escape the difficulty by hiding themselves under those forms of expression. Others, who are bolder, do not hesitate to assert that, strictly speaking, the bread is body, and in this way prove that they are truly men of the letter. If it is objected that the bread therefore is Christ, and, being Christ, is God, they will deny it, because the words of Christ do not expressly say so. But they gain nothing by their denial, since all agree that the whole Christ is offered to us in the Supper. It is intolerable blasphemy to affirm without figure of a fading and corruptible element that it is Christ. I now ask them whether they hold these two propositions to be of the same kind: Christ is the Son of God, and, Bread is the body of Christ. If they grant that they are different (and this, whether they will or not, they will be forced to do), let them tell us where the difference lies. All they can bring forward, I presume, is that the bread is called body in a sacramental manner. Hence it follows that the words of Christ are not subject to the common rule, and ought not to be tested grammatically. I ask all these rigid and obstinate exactors of the letter whether, when Luke and Paul call the cup the testament in blood, they do not express the same thing as in the previous clause, when they call the bread the body. There certainly was the same solemnity in the one part of the mystery as in the other; and since brevity is obscure, the longer sentence better clarifies the meaning. As often, therefore, as they contend from the one expression that the bread is body, I will bring forward a fitting interpretation from the longer expression: that it is a testament in the body. What then? Can we look for surer or more faithful interpreters than Luke and Paul? I have no intention, however, of detracting in any respect from the communication of the body of Christ, which I have acknowledged. I only meant to expose the foolish perverseness with which they wage a war of words. I understand the bread, on the authority of Luke and Paul, to be the body of Christ, because it is a covenant in the body. The covenant, ratified by the sacrifice of his death, would not avail us without the addition of that secret communication by which we are made one with Christ.

26¶

But since nothing will do more to confirm the faith of the godly than to show them that the doctrine we have laid down is taken from the pure word of God and rests on its authority, I will make this plain as briefly as I can. The body with which Christ rose is declared, not by Aristotle but by the Holy Spirit, to be finite, and to be contained in heaven until the last day. I am not unaware how confidently our opponents evade the passages quoted to this effect. Whenever Christ says that he will leave the world and go away (John 14:2, 28), they reply that this departure was nothing more than a change of mortal state. Were this so, Christ would not put the Holy Spirit in his place to supply, as they express it, the lack caused by his absence, since the Spirit does not succeed in his place, nor, on the other hand, does Christ himself descend from the heavenly glory to take on the condition of a mortal life. Certainly the coming of the Spirit and the ascension of Christ are set over against each other, and hence it necessarily follows that Christ dwells with us according to the flesh in the same way in which he sends his Spirit. Moreover, he distinctly says that he would not always be in the world with his disciples (Matthew 26:11). This saying also they think they dispose of admirably, as if it were a denial by Christ that he would always be poor and lowly, or subject to the necessities of a fading life. But this is plainly contrary to the context, since the reference is not to poverty and want, or the wretched condition of an earthly life, but to worship and honor. The disciples were displeased with the anointing by Mary, because they thought it a needless and useless expense, akin to luxury, and would therefore have preferred that the money which they thought wasted had been spent on the poor. Christ answers that he will not always be with them to receive such honor. No different explanation is given by Augustine, whose words are by no means ambiguous: "When Christ says, 'Me you have not always,' he spoke of his bodily presence. In regard to his majesty, in regard to his providence, in regard to his ineffable and invisible grace, what he said is fulfilled: 'Lo, I am with you always, even to the end of the world' (Matthew 28:20). But in regard to the flesh which the Word assumed, in regard to that which was born of the Virgin, in regard to that which was seized by the Jews, nailed to the tree, hung on the cross, wrapped in linen clothes, laid in the tomb, and manifested in the resurrection, 'Me you have not always.' Why? Because he conversed with his disciples in bodily presence for forty days, and, going out with them, ascended while they watched but did not follow. He is not here, for he sits there, at the right hand of the Father. And yet he is here, for the presence of his majesty is not withdrawn. Otherwise put, as regards the presence of his majesty, we have Christ always; while as regards his bodily presence, it was rightly said, 'Me you have not always.' In respect of bodily presence, the Church had him for a few days; now she holds him by faith, but does not see him with the eye" (Augustine, Tractates on John 50). Here (to note this briefly) he makes him present with us in three ways: in majesty, in providence, and in ineffable grace. Under this last I include that wondrous communion of his body and blood, provided we understand that it is brought about by the power of the Holy Spirit, and not by that fictitious enclosing of his body under the element, since our Lord declared that he had flesh and bones which could be handled and seen. "Going away" and "ascending" signify not that he had the appearance of one going away and ascending, but that he truly did what the words express. Someone will ask, Are we then to assign a certain region of heaven to Christ? I answer with Augustine that this is a curious and needless question, provided we believe that he is in heaven.

27¶

What then? Does not the very word ascension, so often repeated, signify removal from one place to another? This they deny, because by height, according to them, only the majesty of his rule is denoted. But what was the very manner of his ascending? Was he not carried up while the disciples looked on? Do not the Evangelists clearly relate that he was carried into heaven? These clever sophists reply that a cloud came between and took him out of their sight, to teach the disciples that he would not afterward be visible in the world. As if he ought not rather to have vanished in a moment, to make them believe in his invisible presence, or the cloud to have gathered around him before he moved a step! When he is carried aloft into the air, and the intervening cloud shows that he is no longer to be sought on earth, we safely infer that his dwelling is now in the heavens, as Paul also asserts, bidding us look for him from there (Philippians 3:20). For this reason the angels remind the disciples that it is vain to keep gazing up into heaven, because Jesus, who was taken up, would come in the same way as they had seen him ascend. Here the adversaries of sound doctrine escape, as they think, by the ingenious quibble that he will come in visible form, though he never departed from the earth, but remained invisibly among his people. As if the angels had hinted at a twofold presence, and had not simply made the disciples eyewitnesses of the ascent, so that no doubt might remain! It was just as if they had said, By ascending to heaven while you looked on, he has asserted his heavenly power; it remains for you to wait patiently until he comes again to judge the world. He has not entered into heaven to occupy it alone, but to gather you and all the godly along with him.

28¶

Since the advocates of this spurious dogma are not ashamed to honor it with the votes of the ancients, and especially of Augustine, I will briefly explain how perverse they are in the attempt. Godly and learned men have collected the passages, and therefore I am unwilling to plead a case already settled; anyone who wishes may consult their writings. I will not even collect from Augustine what might bear on the matter. That the dogma of those who place the body of Christ in the bread is not supported by passages from Augustine, or by the authority of Scripture, is proved here and in sections 29-31. There is no ambiguity in what he says, City of God, 16, chapter 27; On Psalms 26 and 46; Tractates on John 13, 102, 106, 107, etc.

29¶

Since they put so much confidence in this hiding place of invisible presence, let us see how well they conceal themselves in it. First, they cannot produce a syllable from Scripture to prove that Christ is invisible; but they take for granted what no sound man will admit, that the body of Christ cannot be given in the Supper unless covered with the mask of bread. This is the very point in dispute, so far is it from holding the place of a first principle. And while they chatter in this way, they are forced to give Christ a twofold body, because, according to them, it is visible in itself in heaven, but in the Supper is invisible by a special mode of dispensation. How fine a consistency this is may easily be judged, both from other passages of Scripture and from the testimony of Peter. Peter says that the heavens must receive, or contain, Christ until he comes again (Acts 3:21). These men teach that he is in every place, but without form. They say that it is unfair to subject a glorious body to the ordinary laws of nature. But this answer drags along with it the delirious dream of Servetus, which all godly minds rightly abhor, that his body was absorbed by his divinity. I do not say that this is their opinion; but if it is considered one of the properties of a glorified body to fill all things in an invisible manner, it is plain that the bodily substance is abolished, and no distinction is left between his Godhead and his human nature. Again, if the body of Christ is so multiform and varied that it appears in one place and is invisible in another, where is there anything of the nature of a body with its proper dimensions, and where is its unity? Far more correct is Tertullian, who contends that the body of Christ was natural and real, because its figure is set before us in the mystery of the Supper as a pledge and assurance of spiritual life (Tertullian, Against Marcion, book 4). The French adds, "Car la figure seroit fausse, si ce qu'elle represente n'estoit vray." That is: For the figure would be false if the thing which it represents were not real.

30¶

Even granting what they absurdly say about the invisible presence, it will still be necessary to prove the immensity, without which it is vain to try to enclose Christ under the bread. Unless the body of Christ can be everywhere without any boundaries of space, it is impossible to believe that he is hidden in the Supper under the bread. Hence they have been driven to bring in the monstrous dogma of ubiquity. But it has been demonstrated by strong and clear passages of Scripture, first, that his body is bounded by the dimensions of the human body; and secondly, that its ascension into heaven made it plain that it is not in all places, but on passing to a new place leaves the one it formerly occupied. The promise to which they appeal, "I am with you always, even to the end of the world," is not to be applied to the body. First, then, a perpetual union with Christ could not exist unless he dwells in us bodily, apart from the use of the Supper; and therefore they have no good ground for disputing so bitterly about the words of Christ in order to enclose him under the bread in the Supper. The French adds, "veu qu'ils confessent que nous l'avons aussi bien sans la Cene." That is: seeing they acknowledge that we have him just as well without the Supper.

French, "Il faisoit Jesus Christ homme en tant qu'il est Dieu, et Dieu en tant qu'il est homme." That is: He made Jesus Christ man insofar as he is God, and God insofar as he is man.

31¶

They are greatly mistaken in imagining that there is no presence of the flesh of Christ in the Supper unless it is placed in the bread. In this way they leave nothing for the secret working of the Spirit, which unites Christ himself to us. Christ does not seem to them to be present unless he comes down to us, as if we did not equally gain his presence when he raises us up to himself. The only question, therefore, concerns the mode: they place Christ in the bread, while we judge it unlawful to draw him down from heaven. Which of the two is more correct, let the reader judge. Only let them have done with the slander that Christ is withdrawn from his Supper if he does not lurk under the covering of bread. For since this mystery is heavenly, there is no need to bring Christ down to earth in order that he may be joined with us.

32¶

Now, should anyone ask me about the mode, I will not be ashamed to confess that it is too high a mystery either for my mind to comprehend or for my words to express; and, to speak more plainly, I rather feel it than understand it. Here, therefore, I embrace without controversy the truth of God, in which I can safely rest. He declares that his flesh is the meat and his blood the drink of my soul; I give my soul to him to be fed with such food. In his sacred Supper he bids me take, eat, and drink his body and blood under the symbols of bread and wine. I have no doubt that he truly gives them and that I receive them. Only, I reject the absurdities which appear unworthy of the heavenly majesty of Christ and are inconsistent with the reality of his human nature. For they must also be contrary to the word of God, which teaches both that Christ was received into the glory of the heavenly kingdom, so as to be exalted above all the circumstances of the world (Luke 24:26), and no less carefully ascribes to him the properties belonging to a true human nature. This ought not to seem incredible or contrary to reason (Irenaeus, book 4, chapter 34); because, as the whole kingdom of Christ is spiritual, so whatever he does in his Church is not to be tested by the wisdom of this world. Or, to use the words of Augustine, "this mystery is performed by man like the others, but in a divine manner; and on earth, but in a heavenly manner." Such, I say, is the bodily presence which the nature of the sacrament requires, and which we say is displayed here with such power and efficacy that it not only gives our minds undoubted assurance of eternal life, but also secures the immortality of our flesh, since it is now quickened by his immortal flesh and in a manner shines with his immortality. Those who are carried beyond this by their exaggerations do nothing by their extravagances but obscure the plain and simple truth. If anyone is not yet satisfied, I would have him consider here that we are speaking of the sacrament, every part of which ought to have reference to faith. Now by participation in the body, as we have explained, we nourish faith no less richly and abundantly than do those who drag Christ himself down from heaven. Still, I freely confess that I reject that mixture or transfusion of the flesh of Christ with our soul which they teach, because it is enough for us that Christ, out of the substance of his flesh, breathes life into our souls, indeed, pours his own life into us, though the real flesh of Christ does not enter into us. See Bernard, Sermons on the Song of Songs 74, 75; and Treatise on Grace and Free Choice.

33¶

The same view must be taken of communion, which, according to them, does not exist unless they swallow the flesh of Christ under the bread. But no slight insult is offered to the Spirit if we refuse to believe that it is by his incomprehensible working that we communicate in the body and blood of Christ. Indeed, if the nature of the mystery, as delivered to us and known to the ancient Church for four hundred years, had been considered as it deserves, there was more than enough to satisfy us, and the door would have been shut against many disgraceful errors. These errors have kindled fearful dissensions, by which the Church, both in ancient times and in our own, has been miserably troubled, as curious men insist on an extravagant mode of presence to which Scripture gives no support. And over a matter so foolishly and rashly devised they keep up a tumult, as if the enclosing of Christ under the bread were, so to speak, the beginning and end of godliness. It was of first importance to know how the body of Christ, once delivered for us, becomes ours, and how we become partakers of his shed blood, because this is to possess the whole Christ crucified, so as to enjoy all his blessings. But passing over these points, which were of such importance, indeed neglecting and almost suppressing them, they busy themselves only with this one perplexing question: How is the body of Christ hidden under the bread, or under the appearance of bread? They falsely pretend that all we teach about spiritual eating is opposed to true and what they call real eating, since we have regard only to the mode of eating. This mode, according to them, is carnal, since they enclose Christ under the bread; but according to us it is spiritual, inasmuch as the sacred working of the Spirit is the bond of our union with Christ. No better founded is the other objection, that we attend only to the fruit or effect which believers receive from eating the flesh of Christ. We said before that Christ himself is the matter of the Supper, and that the effect follows from this: that by the sacrifice of his death our sins are expiated, by his blood we are washed, and by his resurrection we are raised to the hope of life in heaven. But a foolish notion, of which Lombard was the author, perverts their minds, while they think that the sacrament is the eating of the flesh of Christ. His words are, "The forms of bread and wine are the sacrament and not the thing; the flesh and blood of Christ are the sacrament and the thing; his mystical flesh is the thing and not the sacrament" (Lombard, book 4, distinction 8). Again, a little after: "The thing signified and contained is the proper flesh of Christ; the thing signified and not contained is his mystical body." To his distinction between the flesh of Christ and the nourishing power which it possesses I assent; but his maintaining that it is a sacrament, and a sacrament contained under the bread, is an error not to be tolerated. From this has arisen that false interpretation of sacramental eating, because it was imagined that even the wicked and profane, however much estranged from Christ, eat his body. But the very flesh of Christ in the mystery of the Supper is no less a spiritual thing than eternal salvation. From this we infer that all who are without the Spirit of Christ can no more eat the flesh of Christ than drink wine that has no flavor. Certainly Christ is shamefully torn apart when his body, as lifeless and without any vigor, is prostituted to unbelievers. This is clearly contrary to his words, "He that eats my flesh and drinks my blood dwells in me, and I in him" (John 6:56). They object that he is not speaking there of sacramental eating. This I grant, provided they will not keep stumbling on this stone, that his flesh itself is eaten without any benefit. I should like to know how they keep it confined after they have eaten it. Here, in my opinion, they will find no way out. But they object that the ingratitude of man cannot in any respect detract from or interfere with faith in the promises of God. I grant, and I hold, that the power of the sacrament remains entire, however much the wicked may labor with all their might to destroy it. Still, it is one thing to be offered, another to be received. Christ gives this spiritual food and holds out this spiritual drink to all. Some eat eagerly; others disdainfully reject it. Will their rejection cause the food and drink to lose their nature? They will say that this comparison supports their opinion, namely, that the flesh of Christ, though it be without taste, is still flesh. But I deny that it can be eaten without the taste of faith; or (if it is more agreeable to speak with Augustine) I deny that men carry away more from the sacrament than they gather in the vessel of faith. Thus nothing is taken away from the sacrament; indeed, its reality and efficacy remain unimpaired, although the wicked, after partaking of it outwardly, go away empty. If again they object that it detracts from the expression, "This is my body," if the wicked receive corruptible bread and nothing besides, it is easy to answer that God does not will that his truth be recognized in the mere reception, but in the constancy of his goodness, since he is ready to perform, indeed liberally offers, to the unworthy what they reject. The integrity of the sacrament, an integrity which the whole world cannot violate, lies in this: that the flesh and blood of Christ are no less truly given to the unworthy than to the elect believers of God. And yet it is true that, just as rain falling on hard rock runs off because it cannot penetrate, so the wicked by their hardness repel the grace of God and prevent it from reaching them. We may add that it is no more possible to receive Christ without faith than it is for seed to sprout in the fire. They ask how Christ can have come for the condemnation of some unless they receive him unworthily. But this is absurd, since we nowhere read that they bring death upon themselves by receiving Christ unworthily, but by rejecting him. They are not helped by the parable in which Christ says that the seed which fell among thorns sprang up but was afterward choked (Matthew 13:7), because he is there speaking of the effect of a temporary faith, a faith which those who put Judas on the same footing as Peter in this respect do not think necessary for the eating of the flesh and the drinking of the blood of Christ. Indeed, their error is refuted by the same parable, when Christ says that some seed fell by the wayside and some on stony ground, and yet neither took root. Hence it follows that the hardness of believers is an obstacle which prevents Christ from reaching them. All who would have our salvation promoted by this sacrament will find nothing more fitting than to lead believers to the fountain, so that they may draw life from the Son of God. Its dignity is commended amply enough when we hold that it is a help by which we may be engrafted into the body of Christ, or, being already engrafted, may be more and more united to him, until the union is completed in heaven. They object that Paul could not have made them guilty of the body and blood of the Lord if they had not partaken of them (1 Corinthians 11:7). I answer that they were not condemned for having eaten, but only for having profaned the ordinance by trampling underfoot the pledge which they ought to have received with reverence, the pledge of sacred union with God.

35¶

This consideration will easily turn us away from that carnal adoration which some men have, with perverse rashness, brought into the sacrament, reasoning thus with themselves: If it is the body, then the soul and divinity, which go along with the body and cannot be separated from it, are there also; and therefore Christ must be adored there. First, if we deny their pretended concomitance, what will they do? For though they chiefly insist on the absurdity of separating the body of Christ from his soul and divinity, what sane and sober man can persuade himself that the body of Christ is Christ? They think that they establish this completely by their syllogisms. But since Christ speaks separately of his body and blood, without describing the mode of his presence, how can they arrive in a doubtful matter at the certainty they want? What then? Should their consciences at any time be troubled with some more serious fear, will they at once set them free and dissolve their fears by their syllogisms? In other words, when they see that no certainty is to be had from the word of God, in which alone our minds can rest, and without which they go astray the very moment they begin to reason; when they see themselves opposed by the doctrine and practice of the apostles, and supported by no authority but their own, how will they feel? To such feelings other sharp stings will be added. What? Was it a small matter to worship God under this form without any express command? In a matter relating to the true worship of God, were we to act so lightly without one word of Scripture? Had all their thoughts been kept in due subjection to the word of God, they certainly would have listened to what he himself has said, "Take, eat, and drink," and obeyed the command by which he bids us receive the sacrament, not worship it. Those who receive without adoration, as God commanded, are secure in knowing that they do not depart from the command. In beginning any work, nothing is better than this security. They have the example of the apostles, of whom we do not read that they prostrated themselves and worshiped, but that they sat down, took, and ate. They have the practice of the apostolic Church, where, as Luke relates, believers communicated not in adoration but in the breaking of bread (Acts 2:42). They have the doctrine of the apostles as taught to the Corinthian Church by Paul, who declares that what he delivered he had received from the Lord (1 Corinthians 11:23).

36¶

The purpose of these remarks is to lead godly readers to reflect how dangerous it is, in matters of such difficulty, to wander from the simple word of God to the dreams of our own brain. What has been said above should free us from all scruple in this matter. In order that the godly soul may rightly lay hold of Christ in the sacrament, it must rise to heaven. But if the office of the sacrament is to aid the weakness of the human mind, helping it to rise upward so as to perceive the height of spiritual mysteries, then those who stop short at the outward sign stray from the right path of seeking Christ. What then? Can we deny that the worship is superstitious when men prostrate themselves before bread in order to worship Christ in it? The Council of Nicaea undoubtedly meant to meet this evil when it forbade us to fix our humble attention on the visible signs. And for no other reason was it formerly the custom, before consecration, to call aloud on the people to lift up their hearts, sursum corda. Scripture itself also, besides carefully narrating the ascension of Christ, by which he withdrew his bodily presence from our sight and company in order to make us abandon all carnal thoughts of him, bids us, whenever it makes mention of him, to raise our minds upward and seek him in heaven, seated at the right hand of the Father (Colossians 3:2). According to this rule, we should rather have adored him spiritually in the heavenly glory than devised that perilous kind of adoration filled with gross and carnal ideas of God. Those, therefore, who devised the adoration of the sacrament not only dreamed it up themselves without any authority from Scripture, where no mention of it can be shown (and it would not have been omitted had it been pleasing to God), but, disregarding Scripture, they forsook the living God and fabricated a god for themselves after the lust of their own hearts. For what is idolatry if not to worship the gifts instead of the Giver? Here the sin is twofold. The honor stolen from God is transferred to the creature; and God, moreover, is dishonored by the pollution and profanation of his own goodness, while his holy sacrament is turned into an accursed idol. Let us, on the contrary, so that we may not fall into the same pit, wholly confine our eyes, ears, hearts, minds, and tongues to the sacred doctrine of God. For this is the school of the Holy Spirit, that best of teachers, in which such progress is made that, while nothing is to be acquired anywhere else, we must willingly be ignorant of whatever is not taught there.

37¶

Then, since superstition, once it has passed the proper bounds, has no end to its errors, men went much further. For they devised rites altogether foreign to the institution of the Supper, and to such a degree that they paid divine honors to the sign. They say that their veneration is paid to Christ. First, if this were done in the Supper, I would say that the only legitimate adoration is that which does not stop at the sign, but rises to Christ sitting in heaven. Now, on what pretext do they say that they honor Christ in that bread, when they have no promise of this kind? They consecrate the host, as they call it, carry it about in solemn procession, and formally display it to be gazed at, reverenced, and invoked. I ask by what power they think it rightly consecrated. They will quote the words, "This is my body." I, on the contrary, will object that it was said at the same time, "Take, eat." Nor will I count the other passage as nothing; for I hold that, since the promise is joined to the command, the former is so included under the latter that it cannot possibly be separated from it. This will be made clearer by an example. God gave a command when he said, "Call upon me," and added a promise, "I will deliver you" (Psalm 50:15). Should anyone call upon Peter or Paul and rest on this promise, will not all cry out that he does so in error? And what else, I ask, do those do who, disregarding the command to eat, fasten on the mutilated promise, "This is my body," in order to pervert it to rites foreign to the institution of Christ? Let us remember, therefore, that this promise has been given to those who keep the command connected with it, and that those who turn the sacrament to another end have no support from the word of God. We showed before how the mystery of the sacred Supper contributes to our faith in God. But since the Lord not only reminds us of this great gift of his goodness, as we explained before, but passes it, as it were, from hand to hand, and urges us to recognize it, he at the same time admonishes us not to be ungrateful for the kindness thus bestowed, but rather to proclaim it with fitting praise and celebrate it with thanksgiving. Accordingly, when he delivered the institution of the sacrament to the apostles, he taught them to do it in remembrance of him, which Paul interprets as "to show forth his death" (1 Corinthians 11:26). And this means that all should publicly and with one mouth confess that all our confidence of life and salvation is placed in our Lord's death, so that we ourselves may glorify him by our confession, and by our example stir up others also to give him glory. Here again we see what the aim of the sacrament is, namely, to keep us in remembrance of Christ's death. When we are commanded to show forth the Lord's death until he comes again, all that is meant is that we should proclaim with confession of the mouth what our faith has recognized in the sacrament, namely, that the death of Christ is our life. This is the second use of the sacrament, and it relates to outward confession.

38¶

Thirdly, the Lord intended it to be a kind of exhortation, than which no other could urge or animate us more strongly both to purity and holiness of life and also to love, peace, and concord. For there the Lord communicates his body so that he may become altogether one with us, and we with him. Moreover, since he has only one body, of which he makes us all partakers, we must necessarily all become one body by this participation. This unity is represented by the bread which is set forth in the sacrament. As it is made of many grains so mixed together that one cannot be distinguished from another, so our minds ought to be so heartily united as not to allow any dissension or division. I prefer to give this in the words of Paul: "The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, is it not the communion of the body of Christ? For we, being many, are one bread and one body, for we are all partakers of that one bread" (1 Corinthians 10:15, 16). We shall have profited admirably in the sacrament if this thought has been impressed and engraved on our minds: that none of our brethren is hurt, despised, rejected, injured, or in any way offended without our at the same time hurting, despising, and injuring Christ; that we cannot be at variance with our brethren without at the same time being at variance with Christ; that we cannot love Christ without loving our brethren; that the same care we take of our own body we ought to take of our brethren, who are members of our body; and that, as no part of our body suffers pain without its spreading to the other parts, so every evil which our brother suffers ought to stir our compassion. For this reason Augustine not unfittingly often calls this sacrament the bond of love. What stronger spur could be used to stir up mutual love than this: that Christ, presenting himself to us, not only invites us by his example to give and devote ourselves to one another, but, inasmuch as he makes himself common to all, also makes us all to be one in him?

39¶

This most admirably confirms what I said elsewhere, namely, that there cannot be a right administration of the Supper without the word. Any benefit which we derive from the Supper requires the word. Whether we are to be confirmed in faith, or exercised in confession, or roused to duty, there is need of preaching. Nothing, therefore, can be more preposterous than to turn the Supper into a dumb action. This is done under the tyranny of the Pope, where the whole effect of consecration is made to depend on the intention of the priest, as if it in no way concerned the people, to whom above all the mystery ought to have been explained. This error has arisen from not observing that the promises by which consecration is effected are intended not for the elements themselves but for those who receive them. Christ does not address the bread and tell it to become his body, but bids his disciples eat, and promises them the communion of his body and blood. And according to the order which Paul sets down, the promises are to be offered to believers along with the bread and the cup. So indeed it is. We are not to imagine some magical incantation, and think it enough to mutter the words as if they were heard by the elements; but we are to regard those words as a living sermon, which is to edify the hearers, penetrate their minds, be impressed and settled in their hearts, and exert its efficacy in the fulfillment of what it promises. For these reasons it is clear that the reserving of the sacrament, as some insist, so that an extraordinary distribution of it may be made to the sick, is useless. Either they will receive it without hearing the words of institution read, or the minister will join the true explanation of the mystery with the sign. In the silent dispensing there is abuse and defect. If the promises are recited and the mystery is explained, so that those who are to receive may receive with profit, there can be no doubt that this is the true consecration. What then becomes of that other consecration, the effect of which reaches even to the sick? But, they say, those who do so have the example of the early Church. I grant it; but in so important a matter, where error is so dangerous, nothing is safer than to follow the truth.

40¶

Moreover, as we see that this sacred bread of the Lord's Supper is spiritual food, sweet and savory no less than wholesome to the godly worshipers of God, who on tasting it feel that Christ is their life, and are moved to give thanks and urged to mutual love; so, on the other hand, it is turned into the most deadly poison to all whom it does not nourish and confirm in the faith, nor urge to thanksgiving and love. For just as bodily food, when received into a stomach subject to diseased humors, itself becomes spoiled and corrupted, and rather harms than nourishes, so this spiritual food also, if given to a soul polluted with malice and wickedness, plunges it into greater ruin, not indeed through any defect in the food, but because to the "defiled and unbelieving nothing is pure" (Titus 1:15), however much it may be sanctified by the blessing of the Lord. For, as Paul says, "Whoever shall eat this bread and drink this cup of the Lord unworthily shall be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord"; he "eats and drinks damnation to himself, not discerning the Lord's body" (1 Corinthians 11:27, 29). For men of this kind, who without any spark of faith and without any zeal for love rush forward like swine to seize the Lord's Supper, do not discern the Lord's body at all. For inasmuch as they do not believe that body to be their life, they put every possible insult upon it, stripping it of all its dignity, and they profane and defile it by receiving it in this way; inasmuch as, while estranged and alienated from their brethren, they dare to mix the sacred symbol of Christ's body with their dissensions. It is no thanks to them that the body of Christ is not rent and torn to pieces. They are therefore justly held guilty of the body and blood of the Lord, which they so vilely pollute with sacrilegious impiety. By this unworthy eating they bring judgment on themselves. For while they have no faith in Christ, yet by receiving the sacrament they profess to place their salvation in him alone, and to renounce all other confidence. Therefore they are their own accusers; they bear witness against themselves; they seal their own condemnation. Next, being divided and separated from their brethren, that is, from the members of Christ, by hatred and ill will, they have no part in Christ; and yet they declare that the only safety lies in communicating with Christ and being united to him. For this reason Paul commands a man to examine himself before he eats of that bread and drinks of that cup (1 Corinthians 11:28). By this, as I understand it, he means that each person should go down into himself and consider, first, whether with inward confidence of heart he leans on the salvation obtained by Christ, and acknowledges it with confession of the mouth; and secondly, whether with zeal for purity and holiness he aspires to imitate Christ; whether, after his example, he is ready to give himself to his brethren and to hold himself in common with those with whom he has Christ in common; whether, as he himself is regarded by Christ, he in turn regards all his brethren as members of his body, and, as his own members, desires to cherish, defend, and help them. This is not because the duties of faith and love can now be perfected in us, but because it is our duty to strive and seek with all our heart to increase our faith daily.

41¶

In seeking to prepare for eating worthily, men have often dreadfully harassed and tortured wretched consciences, and yet have in no degree reached the goal. They have said that those eat worthily who are in a state of grace. Being in a state of grace they have interpreted to mean being pure and free from all sin. By this definition all the men who ever have been or are upon the earth were barred from the use of this sacrament. For if we are to seek our worthiness from ourselves, it is all over with us; only despair and deadly ruin await us. Though we strive to the utmost, we will not only make no progress, but will be most unworthy precisely when we have labored most to make ourselves worthy. To cure this sore, they have devised a way of procuring worthiness, namely, that after we have made an examination as far as we can and taken account of all our actions, we should expiate our unworthiness by contrition, confession, and satisfaction. Of the nature of this expiation we have spoken in the proper place (Book 3, chapter 4, sections 2, 17, 27). As far as our present purpose is concerned, I say that such things give poor and fleeting comfort to alarmed and downcast consciences, struck with terror at their sins. For if the Lord by his prohibition admits none to partake of his Supper but the righteous and innocent, every man would need to be careful before feeling secure of that righteousness of his own which he is told God requires. But how are we to be assured that those who have done what lay in them have discharged their duty to God? And even if we were assured of this, who would venture to assure himself that he had done what lay in him? Thus, since there is no certain security for our worthiness, access to the Supper would always be shut off by the fearful prohibition, "He that eats and drinks unworthily eats and drinks damnation to himself."

42¶

It is now easy to judge what is the nature, and who is the author, of that doctrine which prevails in the Papacy, and which by its inhuman severity deprives and robs wretched sinners, oppressed with sorrow and trembling, of the consolation of this sacrament, a sacrament in which all that is delightful in the gospel was set before them. Certainly the devil could have had no quicker way of destroying men than by thus bewitching them, and so shutting them out from the taste and savor of this food with which their most merciful Father in heaven had been pleased to feed them. Therefore, so that we may not rush over such a precipice, let us remember that this sacred feast is medicine to the sick, comfort to the sinner, and bounty to the poor; while to the healthy, the righteous, and the rich, if any such could be found, it would be of no value. For while Christ is given to us in it as food, we perceive that without him we fail, pine, and waste away, just as hunger destroys the vigor of the body. Next, since he is given as life, we perceive that without him we are certainly dead. Therefore the best and only worthiness which we can bring to God is to offer him our own vileness and, if I may so speak, our unworthiness, so that his mercy may make us worthy; to despair of ourselves, so that we may be consoled in him; to humble ourselves, so that we may be lifted up by him; to accuse ourselves, so that we may be justified by him; to aspire, moreover, to the unity which he commends in the Supper; and, as he makes us all one in himself, to desire that we all have one soul, one heart, one tongue. If we ponder and meditate on these things, we may be shaken, but we will never be overwhelmed by such thoughts as these: How shall we, who are without all good, polluted by the defilements of sin, and half dead, worthily eat the body of the Lord? We shall rather consider that we who are poor are coming to a generous giver, the sick to a physician, the sinful to the author of righteousness, and finally the dead to him who gives life. We shall consider that the worthiness which God commands consists especially in faith, which places all things in Christ and nothing in ourselves, and in love, a love which, though imperfect, it may be enough to offer to God so that he may increase it, since it cannot be rendered in full. Some, agreeing with us in holding that worthiness consists in faith and love, have widely erred about the measure of worthiness, demanding a perfection of faith to which nothing can be added, and a love equal to that which Christ showed toward us. And in this way, just like the other party, they bar all men from access to this sacred feast. For if their view were well founded, everyone who receives must receive unworthily, since all without exception are guilty and chargeable with imperfection. And certainly it would be too stupid, not to say idiotic, to require for receiving the sacrament a perfection which would make the sacrament vain and superfluous. For it was not instituted for the perfect, but for the infirm and weak, to stir up, arouse, spur on, and exercise the feeling of faith and love, and at the same time to correct the deficiency of both.

43¶

As to the outward form of the ordinance, whether or not believers are to take the bread into their hands and divide it among themselves, or each is to eat what is given to him; whether they are to return the cup to the deacon or hand it to their neighbor; whether the bread is to be leavened or unleavened, and the wine red or white, is of no consequence. These things are indifferent and left free to the Church, though it is certain that it was the custom of the ancient Church for all to receive it into their hands. And Christ said, "Take this, and divide it among yourselves" (Luke 22:17). History relates that leavened and ordinary bread was used before the time of Alexander, the Bishop of Rome, who was the first to take delight in unleavened bread; for what reason I do not see, unless it was to draw the wondering eyes of the common people by the novelty of the spectacle rather than to train them in sound religion. I appeal to all who have the least zeal for godliness whether they do not plainly perceive both how much more brightly the glory of God is displayed here, and how much more abundant spiritual consolation is felt by believers, than in these stiff and theatrical follies, which have no other use than to impose on the gaping crowd. They call it restraining the people by religion, when, dull and bewitched, they are drawn this way and that by superstition. Should anyone choose to defend such inventions on the ground of antiquity, I am not unaware how ancient is the use of chrism and exorcism in baptism, and how, not long after the age of the apostles, the Supper was tainted with corruption. Such, indeed, is the forwardness of human self-confidence, which cannot restrain itself, but is always playing and running riot in the mysteries of God. But let us remember that God sets so high a value on obedience to his word that he would have us judge his angels and the whole world by it. With all this mass of ceremonies set aside, the sacrament might be celebrated in the most fitting manner if it were administered to the Church very often, at least once a week. The service should begin with public prayer; next a sermon should be preached; then the minister, having placed bread and wine on the table, should read the institution of the Supper. He should next explain the promises which are given in it, and at the same time keep back from communion all those who are barred by the prohibition of the Lord. He should afterward pray that the Lord, in the kindness with which he has bestowed this sacred food on us, would also form and instruct us to receive it with faith and gratitude, and, since we are unworthy of ourselves, would make us worthy of the feast by his mercy. Here either a psalm should be sung or something read, while the faithful communicate in order at the sacred feast, the minister breaking the bread and giving it to the people. When the Supper is ended, an exhortation should be given to sincere faith and confession of faith, to love, and to lives befitting Christians. Lastly, thanks should be offered and the praises of God sung. This done, the Church should be dismissed in peace.

44¶

What we have said so far about the sacrament abundantly shows that it was not instituted to be received once a year, and that perfunctorily (as is now the common custom), but so that all Christians might have it in frequent use and often call to mind the sufferings of Christ, thereby sustaining and confirming their faith; stirring themselves up to sing the praises of God and proclaim his goodness; and cherishing and bearing witness toward one another to that mutual love, the bond of which they see in the unity of the body of Christ. As often as we communicate in the symbol of our Savior's body, as if a pledge were given and received, we bind ourselves to one another in all the duties of love, so that none of us may do anything to offend his brother, or leave undone anything by which he can help him when need demands and opportunity arises. That such was the practice of the apostolic Church we are told by Luke in the Acts, when he says that "they continued steadfastly in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship, and in breaking of bread, and in prayers" (Acts 2:42). Thus we ought always to make sure that no meeting of the Church is held without the word, prayer, the administration of the Supper, and alms. We may gather from Paul that this was the order observed by the Corinthians, and it is certain that this was the practice for many ages afterward. Hence by the ancient canons attributed to Anacletus and Calixtus, after the consecration was made, all were to communicate who did not wish to be outside the bounds of the Church. And in those ancient canons which bear the name of Apostolic, it is said that those who do not remain to the end and do not partake of the sacred communion are to be corrected, as causing disturbance to the Church. In the Council of Antioch it was decreed that those who enter the church, hear the Scriptures, and abstain from communion are to be removed from the Church until they amend their fault. And although in the first Council of Toulouse this was softened, or at least stated in milder terms, yet there also it was decreed that those who, after hearing the sermon, never communicated were to be admonished, and if they still abstained after admonition, were to be excluded.

45¶

By these enactments holy men wished to retain and secure the practice of frequent communion as handed down by the apostles themselves; a practice which, though most wholesome to believers, they saw gradually falling into disuse through the negligence of the people. Of his own age Augustine testifies, "The sacrament of the unity of our Lord's body is in some places provided daily, and in others at certain intervals, at the Lord's table; and at that table some partake to life and others to destruction" (Augustine, Tractate 26, on John 6). And in the first Letter to Januarius he says, "Some communicate daily in the body and blood of the Lord; others receive it on certain days. In some places not a day passes on which it is not offered; in others it is offered only on the Sabbath and the Lord's day; in others on the Lord's day only." But since, as we have said, the people were sometimes slack, holy men urged them with severe rebukes, so that they might not seem to wink at their sluggishness. Of this we have an example in Chrysostom on the Epistle to the Ephesians (Homily 26): "It was not said to him who dishonored the feast, Why have you not taken your seat? but, 'How did you come in here?' (Matthew 22:12). Whoever does not partake of the sacred rites is wicked and shameless in being present. If someone invited to a feast should come in, wash his hands, take his seat, and seem to prepare to eat, and then taste nothing, would he not, I ask, insult both the feast and the host? So you, standing among those who prepare themselves by prayer to take the sacred food, profess to be one of their number by the mere fact that you do not go away, and yet you do not partake. Would it not have been better not to have appeared at all? I am unworthy, you say. Then neither were you worthy of the communion of prayer, which is the preparation for taking the sacred mystery."

46¶

Most assuredly, the custom which prescribes communion once a year is an invention of the devil, by whatever instrument it may have been brought in. They say that Zephyrinus was the author of the decree, though it is not possible to believe that it was the same as we now have it. It may be that, as times then were, he did not provide badly for the Church by his ordinance. For there can be no doubt that at that time the sacred Supper was administered to the faithful at every meeting, nor can it be doubted that a great part of them communicated. But since it scarcely ever happened that all could communicate at the same time, and since it was necessary that those who lived among the profane and idolaters should testify their faith by some outward symbol, this holy man, with a view to order and government, had appointed that day, so that on it the whole of Christendom might make a confession of its faith by partaking of the Lord's Supper. Later generations perverted the ordinance of Zephyrinus, which was otherwise good, when they made a fixed law of one communion a year. The result is that almost all, once they have communicated, sleep on securely, as if they were discharged for all the rest of the year. It ought to have been far otherwise. Every week at least the table of the Lord ought to have been spread for the company of Christians, and the promises declared on which we might then feed spiritually. No one, indeed, ought to be forced, but all ought to be exhorted and urged on; and the torpor of the sluggish ought also to be rebuked, so that all, like starving men, might come to the feast. It was not without cause, therefore, that I complained at the outset that this practice had been brought in by the cunning of the devil; a practice which, in prescribing one day in the year, makes the whole year one of sloth. We see, indeed, that this perverse abuse had already crept in by the time of Chrysostom; but we also see at the same time how much it displeased him. For he complains in bitter terms, in the passage I quoted just now, that there is so great an inequality in this matter that they did not come often at other times of the year, even when prepared, but only at Easter, though unprepared. Then he exclaims, "O custom! O presumption! In vain, then, is the daily offering made; in vain do we stand at the altar. There is no one who partakes along with us." So far is he from having approved the practice by lending it his authority.

47¶

From the same forge came another decree, which snatched or stole half of the Supper from the greater part of the people of God, namely, the symbol of blood. Forbidden to laymen and the profane (such are the titles they give to God's heritage), it became the private possession of a few shaven and anointed men. The edict of the eternal God is that all are to drink. This an upstart dares to set aside and repeal by a new and contrary law, proclaiming that not all are to drink. And so that such legislators may not seem to fight against their God without any reason, they make a pretext of the dangers that might arise if the sacred cup were given to all without distinction; as if these had not been foreseen and provided for by the eternal wisdom of God. Then they reason cleverly, to be sure, that the one is sufficient for the two. For if the body is, as they say, the whole Christ, who cannot be separated from his body, then the blood includes the body by concomitance. Here we see how far our reason agrees with God, once it begins in any degree to rage and run wild with loosened reins. The Lord, pointing to the bread, says, "This is my body." Then, pointing to the cup, he calls it his blood. The audacity of human reason objects and says, The bread is the blood, and the wine is the body; as if the Lord had distinguished his body from his blood, both by words and by signs, without reason; and as if it had ever been heard that the body of Christ or the blood is called God and man. Certainly, if he had meant to designate himself wholly, he might have said, "It is I," according to the manner of speaking in Scripture, and not, "This is my body," "This is my blood." But wishing to help the weakness of our faith, he set the cup apart from the bread, to show that he suffices no less for drink than for food. Now if one part is taken away, we can find only half of the elements in what remains. Therefore, even if it were true, as they pretend, that the blood is in the bread, and on the other hand the body in the cup, by concomitance, they still deprive the godly of that confirmation of faith which Christ delivered as necessary. Bidding farewell, therefore, to their subtleties, let us keep the benefit which, by the ordinance of Christ, is obtained through a double pledge.

Thirdly, why did our Savior say of the bread simply, "Take, eat," and of the cup, "Drink all of it," as if he had deliberately intended to guard against the cunning of Satan? Fourthly, if, as they would have it, the Lord honored only priests with his Supper, what man would ever have dared to call strangers, whom the Lord had excluded, to partake of it, and to partake of a gift which was not in his power to give, without any command from him who alone could give it? A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin opens the Supper as a true communion — Christ does not offer empty signs but genuinely feeds his people on his crucified body and shed blood, lifting them by the Spirit to feast on him, so that the bread and cup are not bare memorials but a real participation in the life of the risen Lord. A word for 2026: in a hurried, distracted age that has thinned the Table to a ritual or swelled it into superstition, the believer must come again with reverence and hunger, neither doubting the grace truly given nor presuming upon it. Examine yourself, as Scripture commands, but do not stay away in despair — the Supper is medicine for the weak, not a reward for the strong. Gather as a household within the gathered church and teach your children what this meal means, that the Christ who gave himself is here offered to faith. Let the Table steady you through the year — a weekly or monthly anchor, the nearness of Christ made tangible to a wandering heart.
