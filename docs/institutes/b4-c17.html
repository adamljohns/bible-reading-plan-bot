1¶

Once God has received us into his family, it is not so that he may treat us as servants, but as sons. He plays the part of a kind and attentive parent, providing for our support throughout the whole course of our lives. And not content with this, he has been pleased to assure us of his continued generosity by giving us a pledge. To this end he has given another sacrament to his Church by the hand of his only-begotten Son: a spiritual feast, at which Christ testifies that he himself is the living bread (John 6:51) on which our souls feed for a true and blessed immortality. Now the knowledge of this great mystery is utterly necessary, and in proportion to its importance it demands a careful explanation. And Satan, in order to rob the Church of this priceless treasure, long ago introduced first a mist and then darkness to obscure its light. He stirred up strife and contention to turn the minds of simple people away from any taste for this sacred food, and in our own age too he has tried the same trick. So I will first give a plain summary suited to the capacity of those who lack understanding, and then go on to clear up those difficulties by which Satan has tried to ensnare the world. First, then, the signs are bread and wine, which represent the invisible food we receive from the body and blood of Christ. For just as God, when he regenerates us in baptism, grafts us into the fellowship of his Church and makes us his own by adoption, so, as we have said, he acts as a provident parent by continually supplying the food with which he sustains and preserves us in the life into which he has begotten us by his Word. Moreover, Christ is the only food of our soul, and so our heavenly Father invites us to him, that, refreshed by communion with him, we may again and again gather new strength until we reach the immortality of heaven. But this mystery of the secret union of Christ with believers is by nature beyond our understanding, so he displays its figure and image in visible signs suited to our capacity. Indeed, by giving us, as it were, deposits and tokens, he makes it as certain to us as if we had seen it with our own eyes. The familiarity of the comparison gives it access to even the dullest minds, showing that souls are fed by Christ just as bodily life is sustained by bread and wine. So now we understand the purpose of this mystical blessing: it is to assure us that the body of Christ was once sacrificed for us, so that we may now feed on it, and, feeding, may feel within ourselves the power of that one sacrifice; and that his blood was once shed for us so as to be our perpetual drink. This is the force of the promise that is added: "Take, eat; this is my body, which is broken for you" (Matthew 26:26, and so on). We are commanded to take and eat the body that was once offered for our salvation, so that, seeing ourselves made partakers of it, we may safely conclude that the power of that death will be effective in us. This is why he calls the cup the covenant in his blood. For the covenant that he once ratified by his blood he in a sense renews, or rather continues, so far as the strengthening of our faith is concerned, every time he holds out his sacred blood as drink to us.

2¶

Devout souls can draw great confidence and delight from this sacrament, since it is a testimony that they form one body with Christ, so that everything that is his they may call their own. From this it follows that we can confidently assure ourselves that the eternal life of which he himself is the heir is ours, and that the kingdom of heaven, into which he has entered, can no more be taken from us than from him. On the other hand, it assures us that we cannot be condemned for our sins, since he absolves us from their guilt, having been pleased to have them charged to himself as if they were his own. This is the wonderful exchange made by his boundless goodness. Having become with us the Son of Man, he has made us with himself sons of God. By his own descent to the earth he has prepared our ascent to heaven. Having taken our mortality, he has given us his immortality. Having taken on our weakness, he has made us strong in his strength. Having submitted to our poverty, he has transferred to us his riches. Having taken upon himself the burden of the unrighteousness that weighed us down, he has clothed us with his righteousness.

3¶

Of all these things we have a complete assurance in this sacrament, which allows us to conclude with certainty that they are as truly held out to us as if Christ were set in bodily presence before our eyes, or handled by our own hands. For these are words that can never lie or deceive: "Take, eat, drink. This is my body, which is broken for you; this is my blood, which is shed for the forgiveness of sins." When he bids us take, he signals that it is ours. When he bids us eat, he signals that it becomes one substance with us. When he says of his body that it was broken, and of his blood that it was shed for us, he shows that both were not so much his own as ours, because he took up and laid down both, not for his own benefit, but for our salvation. And we ought to observe carefully that the chief, and almost the whole, power of the sacrament lies in these words: "It is broken for you; it is shed for you." It would not matter much to us that the body and blood of the Lord are now distributed, had they not once been offered up for our redemption and salvation. This is why they are represented under bread and wine, so that we may learn that they are not only ours, but are meant to nourish our spiritual life. That is, as we noted earlier, by the bodily things produced in the sacrament we are led, by a kind of analogy, to spiritual things. So when bread is given as a symbol of the body of Christ, we must at once think of this comparison. As bread nourishes, sustains, and protects our bodily life, so the body of Christ is the only food to invigorate and keep the soul alive. When we see wine set forth as a symbol of blood, we must think that the same use that wine serves for the body is spiritually given by the blood of Christ; and its use is to foster, refresh, strengthen, and gladden. For if we duly consider what benefit we have gained from the breaking of his sacred body and the shedding of his blood, we will clearly perceive that these properties of bread and wine, according to this analogy, most fittingly represent it when they are given to us.

4¶

Therefore the chief part of a sacrament is not simply to hold out the body of Christ to us with no higher consideration, but rather to seal and confirm that promise by which he testifies that his flesh is food indeed and his blood drink indeed, nourishing us to eternal life, and by which he affirms that he is the bread of life, of which whoever eats will live forever. To seal and confirm that promise, I say, and in order to do so, it sends us to the cross of Christ, where that promise was carried out and fulfilled in every part. For we do not eat Christ rightly and savingly except as crucified, while with living awareness we perceive the power of his death. When he called himself the bread of life, he did not take that title from the sacrament, as some perversely interpret it. Rather, such as he was given to us by the Father, such he showed himself to be when, becoming a partaker of our human mortality, he made us partakers of his divine immortality; when, offering himself in sacrifice, he took our curse upon himself so that he might cover us with his blessing; when by his death he devoured and swallowed up death; when in his resurrection he raised our corruptible flesh, which he had put on, to glory and incorruption.

5¶

It only remains that the whole of this become ours by application. This is done by means of the gospel, and more clearly by the sacred Supper, where Christ offers himself to us with all his blessings, and we receive him by faith. The sacrament, therefore, does not make Christ become the bread of life for the first time; rather, while it reminds us that Christ was made the bread of life so that we may continually feed on him, it gives us a taste and relish for that bread and makes us feel its power. For it assures us, first, that whatever Christ did or suffered was done to give us life; and, second, that this giving of life is eternal, so that by it we are ceaselessly nourished, sustained, and preserved in life. For just as Christ would not have been the bread of life to us if he had not been born, if he had not died and risen again, so he could not now be the bread of life if the power and fruit of his birth, death, and resurrection were not eternal. Christ has expressed all this elegantly in these words: "The bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world" (John 6:51). Doubtless he means that his body will be as bread with respect to the spiritual life of the soul, because it was to be delivered to death for our salvation, and that he extends it to us for food when he makes us partakers of it by faith. So he once gave himself to become bread, when he gave himself to be crucified for the redemption of the world; and he gives himself daily, when in the word of the gospel he offers himself to be received by us as the one who was crucified, when he seals that offer by the sacred mystery of the Supper, and when he accomplishes inwardly what he points to outwardly. Now, two faults are to be avoided here. We must not, by setting too low a value on the signs, separate them from the meanings to which they are in some way attached; nor must we, by praising them too highly, seem somewhat to obscure the mysteries themselves. That Christ is the bread of life by which believers are nourished to eternal life, no one is so utterly devoid of religion as to deny. But not all agree about the manner of partaking of him. For there are some who define the eating of the flesh of Christ and the drinking of his blood as being, in a word, nothing more than believing in Christ himself. But Christ seems to me to have meant to teach something more definite and more sublime in that noble discourse where he commends the eating of his flesh: namely, that we are given life by truly partaking of him, which he signified by the terms eating and drinking, so that no one would suppose that the life we obtain from him is obtained by simple knowledge. For just as it is not the sight but the eating of bread that gives nourishment to the body, so the soul must partake of Christ truly and thoroughly, that by his power it may grow up into spiritual life. Meanwhile, we admit that this is nothing other than the eating of faith, and that no other eating can be imagined. But there is this difference between their way of speaking and mine. According to them, to eat is merely to believe; whereas I maintain that the flesh of Christ is eaten by believing, because it is made ours by faith, and that this eating is the effect and fruit of faith. Or, if you want it stated more plainly: according to them, eating is faith, whereas to me it seems rather to be a consequence of faith. The difference is small in words, but not small in reality. For although the apostle teaches that Christ dwells in our hearts by faith (Ephesians 3:17), no one will interpret that dwelling to be faith itself. Everyone sees that it explains the admirable effect of faith, because it is owing to faith that believers have Christ dwelling in them. In this way the Lord was pleased, by calling himself the bread of life, to teach not only that our salvation is stored up in faith in his death and resurrection, but also that, by virtue of true communication with him, his life passes into us and becomes ours, just as bread, when taken as food, gives strength to the body.

6¶

When Augustine, whom they claim as their patron, wrote that we eat by believing, all he meant was to indicate that this eating is of faith, and not of the mouth. This I do not deny; but at the same time I add that by faith we embrace Christ, not as appearing at a distance, but as uniting himself to us, he being our head and we his members. I do not entirely disapprove of that way of speaking; I only deny that it is a full interpretation, if they mean by it to define what it is to eat the flesh of Christ. I see that Augustine repeatedly used this form of expression, as when he said (On Christian Doctrine, Book 3), "'Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man' is a figurative expression, commanding us to have communion with our Lord's passion, and to treasure in our memory, sweetly and usefully, that his flesh was crucified and wounded for us." So also when he says, "These three thousand men who were converted at the preaching of Peter (Acts 2:41), by believing, drank the blood which they had cruelly shed." See Augustine, Homilies on John, 31 and 40, and so on; Chrysostom, Homilies to the People of Antioch, 60, 61; and Homilies on Mark, 89.

7¶

I am not satisfied with the view of those who, while acknowledging that we have some kind of communion with Christ, only make us partakers of the Spirit, leaving out all mention of flesh and blood. As if it were said to no purpose at all that his flesh is food indeed and his blood drink indeed; that we have no life unless we eat that flesh and drink that blood; and so on. Therefore, if it is clear that full communion with Christ goes beyond their description, which is too narrow, I will try to show briefly how far it extends, before going on to speak of the opposite fault of excess. For I will have a longer argument with these exaggerating teachers, who, in keeping with their crude ideas, invent an absurd manner of eating and drinking, and, after stripping Christ of his flesh, transform him into a phantom. If indeed it is lawful to put this great mystery into words at all, it is a mystery that I feel, and therefore freely confess that I am unable to grasp with my mind. So far am I from wishing anyone to measure its loftiness by my feeble capacity. On the contrary, I urge my readers not to confine their understanding within those too-narrow limits, but to try to rise far higher than I can lead them. For whenever this subject is considered, after I have done my utmost, I feel that I have spoken far below its worth. And though the mind is more powerful in thought than the tongue is in expression, even the mind is overcome and overwhelmed by the greatness of the subject. So all that remains is to break out in wonder at the mystery, which the mind plainly cannot grasp, nor the tongue express. I will, however, give a summary of my view as best I can, not doubting its truth, and therefore trusting that it will not be rejected by godly hearts.

8¶

First of all, the Scriptures teach us that Christ was from the beginning the living Word of the Father, the source and origin of life, from which all things should always receive life. This is why John at one point calls him the Word of life, and at another says that in him was life, meaning that even then, pervading all creatures, he instilled into them the power of breathing and living. He afterward adds that the life was at last made manifest, when the Son of God, taking our nature, showed himself in bodily form to be seen and handled. For although he had previously spread his power throughout creation, yet because man, alienated from God by sin, had lost the communication of life and saw death threatening him on every side, he had to be restored to fellowship with that Word in order to regain the hope of immortality. How little confidence can it give you to know that the Word of God, from which you are at the greatest distance, contains within himself the fullness of life, while in yourself, in whatever direction you turn, you see nothing but death? But ever since that fountain of life began to dwell in our nature, he no longer lies hidden far away from us, but offers himself openly for us to share in. Indeed, the very flesh in which he dwells he makes life-giving to us, so that by partaking of it we may feed for immortality. "I," he says, "am that bread of life"; "I am the living bread which came down from heaven"; "And the bread that I will give is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world" (John 6:48, 51). By these words he declares not only that he is life, since he is the eternal Word of God who came down to us from heaven, but that, by coming down, he gave life to the flesh he assumed, so that from it a communication of life might flow to us. So too he adds that his flesh is food indeed and his blood drink indeed: by this food believers are nurtured to eternal life. The godly, then, have wonderful comfort in this, that they now find life in their own flesh. For they not only reach it by easy access, but have it set before them freely. Let them only throw open the door of their hearts to take it into their embrace, and they will obtain it.

9¶

The flesh of Christ, however, does not have such power in itself as to make us live, since by its own original condition it was subject to mortality, and even now, endowed with immortality, it does not live by itself. Still, it is properly called life-giving, since it is filled with the fullness of life for the purpose of transmitting that life to us. In this sense I understand our Saviour's words, as Cyril interprets them: "As the Father has life in himself, so has he given to the Son to have life in himself" (John 5:26). For there he is properly speaking, not of the properties he possessed with the Father from the beginning, but of those with which he was clothed in the flesh in which he appeared. Accordingly, he shows that in his humanity also the fullness of life resides, so that everyone who shares in his flesh and blood at the same time enjoys participation in life. The nature of this can be explained by a familiar example. Water is at one time drunk straight from the fountain, at another drawn up, at another led off by channels to irrigate the fields, and yet it does not flow forth of itself for all these uses, but is taken from its source, which, with a never-ending flow, again and again sends forth a fresh and sufficient supply. In the same way, the flesh of Christ is like a rich and inexhaustible fountain, which pours into us the life flowing forth from the Godhead into itself. Now, who does not see that communion in the flesh and blood of Christ is necessary for all who aspire to the heavenly life? Hence those passages of the apostle: the Church is the "body" of Christ, his "fullness." He is "the head," "from whom the whole body, fitly joined together and held together by what every joint supplies, makes increase of the body" (Ephesians 1:23; 4:15-16). Our bodies are the "members of Christ" (1 Corinthians 6:15). We perceive that none of these things can possibly happen unless he clings to us wholly in body and spirit. But the very close connection that unites us to his flesh he illustrated with still more striking words, when he said that we "are members of his body, of his flesh, and of his bones" (Ephesians 5:30). At last, to testify that the matter is too high for words, he concludes by exclaiming, "This is a great mystery" (Ephesians 5:32). It would therefore be extreme folly not to acknowledge the communion of believers with the body and blood of the Lord, a communion that the apostle declares to be so great that he chooses rather to marvel at it than to explain it.

10¶

The sum of it is that the flesh and blood of Christ feed our souls just as bread and wine maintain and support our bodily life. For there would be no fitness in the sign if our souls did not find their nourishment in Christ. And this could not happen unless Christ truly became one with us and refreshed us by the eating of his flesh and the drinking of his blood. But although it seems an incredible thing that the flesh of Christ, though so far from us in terms of place, should be food to us, let us remember how far the secret power of the Holy Spirit surpasses all our conceptions, and how foolish it is to want to measure its immensity by our feeble capacity. Therefore, what our mind does not grasp, let faith conceive: namely, that the Spirit truly unites things separated by space. That sacred communion of flesh and blood, by which Christ pours his life into us just as if it penetrated our bones and marrow, he testifies and seals in the Supper. And he does this not by presenting an empty or meaningless sign, but by exerting there a power of the Spirit by which he fulfills what he promises. And truly the thing signified there he displays and offers to all who sit down at that spiritual feast, although it is received with benefit by believers only, who receive this great gift with true faith and heartfelt gratitude. For this reason the apostle said, "The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, is it not the communion of the body of Christ?" (1 Corinthians 10:16). There is no ground to object that the expression is figurative and gives the sign the name of the thing signified. I admit, indeed, that the breaking of bread is a symbol, not the reality itself. But, this being admitted, we rightly conclude from the showing of the symbol that the thing itself is shown. For unless we would charge God with deceit, we will never dare to say that he holds out an empty symbol. Therefore, if by the breaking of bread the Lord truly represents the partaking of his body, there ought to be no doubt at all that he truly displays and grants it. The rule the godly should always observe is this: whenever they see the symbols instituted by the Lord, to think and feel surely persuaded that the truth of the thing signified is also present. For why does the Lord put the symbol of his body into your hands, except to assure you that you truly partake of him? If this is true, let us feel just as assured that the visible sign is given to us as a seal of an invisible gift, as we are sure that his body itself is given to us.

I hold, then (as has always been received in the Church, and is still taught by those who think rightly), that the sacred mystery of the Supper consists of two things: the bodily signs, which, presented to the eye, represent invisible things in a way suited to our weak capacity; and the spiritual truth, which is at once pictured and displayed by the signs. When I try to explain its nature plainly, I am accustomed to set down three things: the thing meant, the matter that depends on it, and the power or effect that follows from both. The thing meant consists in the promises, which are in a sense included in the sign. By the matter, or substance, I mean Christ, with his death and resurrection. By the effect, I understand redemption, justification, sanctification, eternal life, and all the other benefits that Christ bestows on us. Moreover, though all these things have respect to faith, I leave no room for the quibble that, when I say Christ is grasped by faith, I mean that he is only grasped by the intellect and imagination. He is offered by the promises, not that we may stop short at the sight or mere knowledge of him, but that we may enjoy true communion with him. And indeed, I do not see how anyone can expect to have redemption and righteousness in the cross of Christ, and life in his death, without first of all trusting in true communion with Christ himself. Those blessings could not reach us unless Christ first made himself ours. I say, then, that in the mystery of the Supper, by the symbols of bread and wine, Christ, his body and his blood, are truly displayed to us; that in them he fulfilled all obedience in order to obtain righteousness for us; first, that we might become one body with him; and second, that, being made partakers of his substance, we might feel the result of this fact in sharing in all his blessings.

12¶

I now come to the exaggerated additions that superstition has introduced. Here Satan has used all his cunning, drawing the minds of men away from heaven and steeping them in the perverse error that Christ is fastened to the element of bread. And first, we are not to dream of such a presence of Christ in the sacrament as the schemers of the Roman court have imagined, as if the body of Christ, locally present, were to be taken into the hand, chewed by the teeth, and swallowed down the throat. This was the form of recantation that Pope Nicholas dictated to Berengar as a token of his repentance, a form stated in terms so monstrous that the author of the Gloss exclaims that there is danger, if the reader is not especially careful, that it will lead him into a worse heresy than Berengar's own (Distinction 2, chapter "I, Berengar"). Peter Lombard, though he labors hard to excuse the absurdity, rather inclines to a different opinion. As we cannot doubt at all that the body is bounded according to the invariable rule of the human body, and is contained in heaven, where it was once received, and where it will remain until it returns for judgment, so we regard it as utterly unlawful to bring it back under these corruptible elements, or to imagine it present everywhere. And indeed there is no need of this for us to partake of it, since the Lord by his Spirit grants us the blessing of being one with him in soul, body, and spirit. The bond of that connection, therefore, is the Spirit of Christ, who unites us to him and is a kind of channel by which everything that Christ has and is comes down to us. For if we see that the sun, in sending forth its rays upon the earth to generate, cherish, and invigorate its offspring, in a sense pours its substance into it, why should the radiance of the Spirit be any less in conveying to us communion in Christ's flesh and blood? This is why Scripture, when it speaks of our participation with Christ, refers its whole power to the Spirit. Instead of many passages, one will be enough. Paul, in the Epistle to the Romans (Romans 8:9-11), shows that the only way Christ dwells in us is by his Spirit. By this, however, he does not take away that communion of flesh and blood of which we now speak, but shows that it is owing to the Spirit alone that we possess Christ wholly and have him abiding in us.

The Schoolmen, horrified at this barbarous impiety, speak more modestly, though they do nothing more than amuse themselves with subtler delusions. They admit that Christ is not contained in the sacrament in a bounded or bodily way, but they then devise a method that they themselves do not understand and cannot explain to others. It comes to this, however: that Christ may be sought in what they call the appearances of bread. What of it? When they say that the substance of bread is changed into Christ, do they not attach him to the white color, which is all they leave of it? But they say that, though he is contained in the sacrament, he still remains in heaven, and has no other presence there than that of dwelling. But whatever terms they use to make their gloss, the sum of it all is that what was formerly bread becomes Christ by consecration, so that Christ thereafter lies hidden under the color of bread. This they are not ashamed to state outright. For Lombard's words are: "The body of Christ, which is visible in itself, lurks and lies covered, after the act of consecration, under the appearances of bread" (Lombard, Sentences, Book 4, Distinction 12). Thus the figure of the bread is nothing but a mask that hides the flesh from our sight. But there is no need of many guesses to detect the snare they meant to lay by these words, since the thing itself speaks plainly. It is easy to see how great is the superstition under which not only the common people but the leaders also have labored for many ages, and still labor, in the Popish Churches. Little concerned about true faith (by which alone we attain to fellowship with Christ and become one with him), provided they have his bodily presence, which they have fabricated without authority from the Word, they think he is sufficiently present. So we see that all they have gained by their clever subtlety is to make bread to be regarded as God.

They could never have been so shamefully deceived by Satan's tricks if they had not first been bewitched by the mistaken idea that the body of Christ, hidden under the bread, is carried by the physical mouth down into the stomach. The reason for this brutish notion was that, for them, consecration worked just like a magic spell. They missed the principle that bread is a sacrament only to those to whom the word is addressed, just as the water of baptism is not changed in itself, but begins to be to us what it was not before, as soon as the promise is joined to it. This will become clearer from the example of a similar sacrament. The water that gushed from the rock in the wilderness was to the Israelites a token and sign of the very same thing that is pictured to us in the Supper by the wine. For Paul declares that they drank the same spiritual drink. But that water was also drunk by the herds and flocks of the people. From this it is easy to conclude that, in the earthly elements, when they are put to a spiritual use, no other change takes place than one that concerns us, since they become to us seals of God's promises. Moreover, since it is God's purpose, as I have repeatedly stressed, to lift us up to himself by suitable means, those who do indeed call us to Christ, but to a Christ hidden invisibly under the bread, wickedly defeat that purpose by their perverseness. For it is impossible for the human mind to free itself from the vastness of space and rise up to Christ, who is above the heavens. What nature denied them, they tried to gain by a harmful remedy. Staying on the earth, they felt no need to draw near to Christ in heaven. This was the necessity that drove them to transform the body of Christ. In Bernard's day, though a harsher way of speaking had taken hold, transubstantiation was not yet acknowledged. And in every earlier age, the comparison on everyone's lips was that a spiritual reality is joined with the bread and wine in this sacrament. As for their terms, they think they answer cleverly, though they bring forward nothing relevant to the matter at hand. The rod of Moses, they say, when it was turned into a serpent, still keeps its former name and is called a rod, even though it takes on the name of a serpent. And so, they argue, it is just as likely that, even though the bread passes into a new substance, it is still called by a stretch of language, and not unfittingly, what it still appears to the eye to be. But what resemblance, real or apparent, do they find between that famous miracle and their fictitious illusion, which no eye on earth has ever witnessed? The magicians, by their tricks, had convinced the Egyptians that they had a divine power, above the ordinary course of nature, to change created things. Moses comes forward, and after exposing their deceptions, he shows that the unconquerable power of God is on his side, since his rod swallows up all the other rods. But since that change was visible to the eye, we have already noted that it has no bearing on the matter at hand. Shortly afterward the rod visibly took its own form again. We may add that we do not even know whether this was a momentary change of substance. Compare together Ambrose, On those who are initiated into the mysteries (chapter 9), and Augustine, On the Trinity, Book 3, chapter 10, and it will be seen that both stand opposed to transubstantiation.

Some, in order to cling stubbornly to the error they once rashly adopted, do not hesitate to claim that the dimensions of Christ's flesh are no more confined than those of heaven and earth. His birth as an infant, they say, his growth, his being stretched out on the cross, his being shut up in the tomb, were all brought about by a kind of arrangement, so that he might carry out the acts of being born, of dying, and of other human deeds. So too, they say, his being seen in his usual bodily appearance after the resurrection, his ascension into heaven, and his appearing after his ascension to Stephen and to Paul, were the effect of the same arrangement, so that it might be made plain to human eyes that he was established as King in heaven. But what is this except to summon Marcion back from his grave? For there can be no doubt that the body of Christ, if it were made like this, was a phantom, or something illusory. Others use a somewhat more subtle escape: that the body given in the sacrament is glorious and immortal, and that therefore there is nothing absurd in its being contained under the sacrament in various places, or in no place at all, and in no particular form. But I ask, what did Christ give to his disciples the day before he suffered? Do not the words say that he gave his mortal body, which was to be handed over to death shortly afterward? But, they say, he had earlier displayed his glory to the three disciples on the mountain. That is true. But his purpose there was to give them, for the moment, a taste of immortality. Even so, they cannot find there a twofold body, but only the one body he had taken on, now clothed in new glory. When he distributed his body at the first Supper, the hour was near in which he was 'stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted.' He was so far from intending at that moment to display the glory of his resurrection. And here, what a door is opened to Marcion, if the body of Christ was seen humble and mortal in one place, and glorious and immortal in another! And yet, if their opinion were well founded, the same thing would happen every day, because they are forced to admit that the body of Christ, which is in itself visible, lurks invisibly under the symbol of bread. And yet those who put out such monstrous teachings, far from being ashamed of the disgrace, attack us with bitter abuse because we will not subscribe to them.

But suppose we grant that the body and blood of Christ are attached to the bread and wine. Then the one must necessarily be severed from the other. For just as the bread is given separately from the cup, so the body, joined to the bread, must be separated from the blood, which is contained in the cup. For since they affirm that the body is in the bread and the blood is in the cup, while the bread and the wine are at some distance from each other in space, they cannot by any quibble escape the conclusion that the body must be separated from the blood. Their usual excuse, that the blood is in the body and the body again in the blood, by what they call concomitance, is worse than empty, since the symbols in which they are contained are kept distinct in this way. But if we are carried up to heaven with our eyes and our minds, so that we may there behold Christ in the glory of his kingdom, then, just as the symbols invite us to him in his wholeness, so under the symbol of bread we must feed on his body, and under the symbol of wine drink his blood separately, and in this way have the full enjoyment of him. For though he withdrew his flesh from us and ascended with his body into heaven, he nevertheless sits at the right hand of the Father, that is, he reigns in the power, majesty, and glory of the Father. This kingdom is not limited by any distances of space, nor bounded by any dimensions. Christ can exert his power wherever he pleases, in earth and in heaven. He can make his presence felt by the exercise of his power. He can always be present with his people, breathing his own life into them, living in them, sustaining, strengthening, and reviving them, and keeping them safe, just as if he were with them in the body. In short, he can feed them with his own body, the communion of which he pours into them. In this way the body and blood of Christ are held out to us in the sacrament.

19¶

We must hold the presence of Christ in the Supper to be of such a kind that it neither fastens him to the element of bread, nor encloses him within bread, nor confines him in any way; for this would plainly take away from his heavenly glory. And further, it must be of such a kind that it neither strips him of his proper dimensions, nor divides him by differences of place, nor assigns to him a body of boundless extent, spread through heaven and earth. All these things are plainly at odds with his true human nature. Let us never allow ourselves to lose sight of these two limits. First, let there be nothing that takes away from the heavenly glory of Christ. This happens whenever he is brought down under the perishable elements of this world, or fastened to any earthly creatures. Second, let no property be assigned to his body that is inconsistent with his human nature. This is done when his body is either said to be infinite, or made to occupy a number of places at the same time. But once these absurdities are set aside, I gladly admit anything that helps to express the true and substantial communication of the body and blood of the Lord, as it is held out to believers under the sacred symbols of the Supper. We are to understand that they are received not by the imagination or the intellect alone, but are truly enjoyed as the food of eternal life. As for the hatred with which this view is regarded by the world, and the unjust prejudice it incurs when it is defended, there is no cause for it except the dreadful enchantments of Satan. What we teach on this subject is in perfect agreement with Scripture. It contains nothing absurd, obscure, or ambiguous. It is not unfavorable to true godliness and solid edification. In short, there is nothing in it to give offense, except that, for some ages, while the ignorance and barbarism of the sophists ruled in the Church, the clear light and open truth were unworthily suppressed. And yet, because Satan, by means of troublesome spirits, is still in our own day straining to the utmost to bring this teaching into dishonor with every kind of slander and reproach, it is right to assert and defend it with the greatest care.

20¶

Before we go any further, we must consider the ordinance itself as Christ instituted it, because the most plausible objection our opponents make is that we abandon his words. To free ourselves from the charge with which they thus burden us, the best course will be to begin with an interpretation of the words. Three of the Gospel writers, along with Paul, relate that our Savior took bread, and after giving thanks, broke it and gave it to his disciples, saying, 'Take, eat: this is my body, which is given,' or 'broken, for you.' Concerning the cup, Matthew and Mark say, 'This is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many for the remission of sins.' Luke and Paul say, 'This cup is the new testament in my blood.' The defenders of transubstantiation insist that the pronoun 'this' refers to the appearance of bread, because, they say, the whole flow of our Savior's words is an act of consecration, and there is no substance that can be pointed to. But if they hold so strictly to the words, then, since what our Savior gave to his disciples he declared to be his body, nothing is further from the strict meaning of the words than their fiction that what was bread is now body. What Christ takes into his hands and gives to the apostles he declares to be his body; but he had taken bread, and therefore who does not see that what is given is still bread? Hence nothing can be more absurd than to transfer what is said of bread to the appearance of bread. Others, interpreting the word 'is' as equivalent to 'is transubstantiated,' resort to a gloss that is forced and violently twisted. They have no ground, then, for pretending that they are moved by reverence for the words. The use of the word 'is' to mean 'being converted into something else' is unknown to every language and nation. As for those who leave the bread in the Supper, yet affirm that it is the body of Christ, there is great variety among them. Those who speak more moderately, though they press the letter, 'This is my body,' afterward abandon that strictness and remark that it amounts to saying that the body of Christ is with the bread, in the bread, and under the bread. To the reality they affirm we have already referred, and will return at greater length shortly. For now I am only considering the words by which, they say, they are prevented from admitting that the bread is called body because it is a sign of the body. But if they shun everything that looks like a metaphor, why do they leap from Christ's plain pointing to forms of expression that are quite different? For there is a great difference between saying that the bread is the body, and saying that the body is with the bread. But seeing that they cannot maintain the simple statement that the bread is the body, they tried to escape the difficulty by hiding under those forms of expression. Others, who are bolder, do not hesitate to assert that, strictly speaking, the bread is body, and in this way they show that they are truly men of the letter. If it is objected that the bread, then, is Christ, and being Christ, is God, they will deny it, because the words of Christ do not expressly say so. But they gain nothing by their denial, since all agree that the whole Christ is offered to us in the Supper. It is intolerable blasphemy to affirm, without any figure, of a fading and perishable element, that it is Christ. I now ask them whether they hold these two statements to be identical: 'Christ is the Son of God,' and 'Bread is the body of Christ.' If they grant that they are different (and, whether they like it or not, they will be forced to do so), let them tell us where the difference lies. All they can bring forward, I suppose, is that the bread is called body in a sacramental way. From this it follows that the words of Christ are not subject to the ordinary rule, and ought not to be tested by grammar. I ask all these rigid and stubborn enforcers of the letter whether, when Luke and Paul call the cup the testament in the blood, they do not express the same thing as in the earlier clause, when they call the bread the body. There was certainly the same solemnity in the one part of the mystery as in the other, and since brevity is obscure, the fuller sentence better brings out the meaning. As often, then, as they argue from the one expression that the bread is body, I will draw a fitting interpretation from the fuller expression, that it is a testament in the body. What then? Can we look for surer or more faithful interpreters than Luke and Paul? I have no intention, however, of detracting in any way from the communication of the body of Christ, which I have already acknowledged. I only meant to expose the foolish stubbornness with which they wage a war of words. The bread I understand, on the authority of Luke and Paul, to be the body of Christ, because it is a covenant in the body. If they dispute this, their quarrel is not with me but with the Spirit of God. However often they may repeat that reverence for the words of Christ will not let them give a figurative interpretation to what is spoken plainly, that excuse cannot justify them in rejecting all the contrary arguments we bring forward. Meanwhile, as I have already observed, it is right to attend to the force of what is meant by a testament in the body and blood of Christ. The covenant, ratified by the sacrifice of death, would be of no benefit to us without the addition of that secret communication by which we are made one with Christ.

But since nothing will be more effective in confirming the faith of the godly than to show them that the doctrine we have set down is drawn from the pure word of God and rests on its authority, I will make this plain with as much brevity as I can. The body with which Christ rose is declared, not by Aristotle but by the Holy Spirit, to be finite, and to be contained in heaven until the last day. I am well aware how confidently our opponents evade the passages quoted to this effect. Whenever Christ says that he will leave the world and go away, they reply that this departure was nothing more than a change of his mortal condition. If that were so, Christ would not have put the Holy Spirit in his place to make up, as they put it, for the lack caused by his absence, since the Spirit does not take his place, nor, on the other hand, does Christ come down from his heavenly glory to take up the condition of a mortal life. Surely the coming of the Spirit and the ascension of Christ are set over against each other, and from this it necessarily follows that Christ dwells with us according to the flesh in the same way as that in which he sends his Spirit. Moreover, he plainly says that he would not always be in the world with his disciples. This saying too they think they cleverly dispose of, as if Christ were denying that he would always be poor and lowly, or subject to the needs of a fading life. But this plainly contradicts the context, since the reference is not to poverty and want, or the wretched condition of earthly life, but to worship and honor. The disciples were displeased with the anointing by Mary, because they thought it a needless and useless expense, close to extravagance, and would therefore have preferred that the price they considered wasted should have been spent on the poor. Christ answers that he will not always be with them to receive such honor. Augustine gives no different explanation, and his words are by no means unclear. When Christ says, 'Me ye have not always,' he was speaking of his bodily presence. With respect to his majesty, with respect to his providence, with respect to his unspeakable and invisible grace, what he said is fulfilled: 'Lo, I am with you always, even unto the end of the world.' But with respect to the flesh that the Word assumed, with respect to that which was born of the Virgin, with respect to that which was seized by the Jews, nailed to the tree, hung on the cross, wrapped in linen cloths, laid in the tomb, and made manifest in the resurrection, 'Me ye have not always.' Why? Because for forty days he conversed with his disciples in bodily presence, and, going out with them, he ascended, while they looked on but did not follow. He is not here, for he sits there, at the right hand of the Father. And yet he is here, for the presence of his majesty has not been withdrawn. So, as regards the presence of his majesty, we have Christ always; while, as regards his bodily presence, it was rightly said, 'Me ye have not always.' In bodily presence the Church had him for a few days; now she holds him by faith, but does not see him with the eye. Here, to note this briefly, Augustine makes him present with us in three ways: in majesty, in providence, and in unspeakable grace. Under this last I include that wonderful communion of his body and blood, provided we understand that it is brought about by the power of the Holy Spirit, and not by that fictitious enclosing of his body under the element, since our Lord declared that he had flesh and bones that could be handled and seen. 'Going away' and 'ascending' indicate, not that he merely had the appearance of one going away and ascending, but that he truly did what the words express. Now, if anyone asks me about the manner of it, I will not be ashamed to confess that it is too high a mystery for my mind to grasp or my words to express; and, to speak more plainly, I feel it rather than understand it. Therefore, in the truth of God, on which I can safely rest, I embrace it here without dispute. He declares that his flesh is the food, and his blood the drink, of my soul; I give my soul to him to be fed with such food. In his sacred Supper he bids me take, eat, and drink his body and blood under the symbols of bread and wine. I have no doubt that he truly gives them, and that I receive them. Only, I reject those absurdities that appear unworthy of the heavenly majesty of Christ and inconsistent with the reality of his human nature, since they must also be at odds with the Word of God. For the Word teaches both that Christ was received into the glory of the heavenly kingdom, so as to be exalted above all the conditions of this world (Luke 24:26), and, no less carefully, ascribes to him the properties belonging to a true human nature. This ought not to seem incredible or contrary to reason (Irenaeus, Book 4, chapter 34); for, as the whole kingdom of Christ is spiritual, so whatever he does in his Church is not to be measured by the wisdom of this world. Or, to use the words of Augustine, this mystery is performed by a man like the others, but in a divine manner; and on earth, but in a heavenly manner. Such, I say, is the bodily presence the nature of the sacrament requires, and which we say is here displayed with such power and effectiveness that it not only gives our minds undoubted assurance of eternal life, but also secures the immortality of our flesh, since our flesh is now made alive by his immortal flesh, and in a manner shines in his immortality. Those who carry themselves beyond this with their exaggerations accomplish nothing by their extravagant claims except to obscure the plain and simple truth. If anyone is still not satisfied, I would have him consider for himself that we are speaking of the sacrament, every part of which ought to have reference to faith. Now, by partaking of his body, as we have explained, we nourish faith no less richly and abundantly than do those who drag Christ himself down from heaven. Still, I freely confess that I reject the mixing or transfusing of the flesh of Christ into our soul that they teach, because it is enough for us that Christ, out of the substance of his flesh, breathes life into our souls — indeed, pours his own life into us — even though the actual flesh of Christ does not enter into us. Indeed, if the nature of the mystery, as it was handed down to us and known to the ancient Church for four hundred years, had been considered as it deserves, there would have been more than enough to satisfy us, and the door would have been shut against many shameful errors. These errors have kindled fearful conflicts by which the Church, both in ancient times and in our own, has been miserably troubled, as inquisitive men insist on an extravagant manner of presence for which Scripture gives no warrant. And over a matter so foolishly and rashly invented, they keep up an uproar, as if the enclosing of Christ under the bread were, so to speak, the beginning and end of godliness. It was of first importance to know how the body of Christ, once delivered up for us, becomes ours, and how we become partakers of his shed blood, because this is to possess the whole of Christ crucified, so as to enjoy all his blessings. But passing over these matters of such great importance — indeed, neglecting and almost suppressing them — they busy themselves only with this one troublesome question: how is the body of Christ hidden under the bread, or under the appearance of bread? They falsely pretend that everything we teach about spiritual eating is opposed to true and, as they call it, real eating, when in fact we differ only over the manner of eating. According to them this is carnal, since they enclose Christ under the bread; but according to us it is spiritual, since the sacred working of the Spirit is the bond of our union with Christ. No better founded is their other objection — that we attend only to the fruit or effect that believers receive from eating the flesh of Christ. We have already said that Christ himself is the substance of the Supper, and that the effect follows from this: that by the sacrifice of his death our sins are atoned for, by his blood we are washed, and by his resurrection we are raised to the hope of life in heaven. But a foolish notion, of which Lombard was the author, distorts their minds, for they think that the sacrament is the eating of the flesh of Christ. His words are: "The sacrament, and not the thing, is the forms of bread and wine; the sacrament and the thing are the flesh and blood of Christ; the thing and not the sacrament is his mystical flesh" (Lombard, Book 4, Distinction 8). And again, a little later: "The thing signified and contained is the actual flesh of Christ; the thing signified but not contained is his mystical body." To his distinction between the flesh of Christ and the power of nourishing that it possesses, I agree; but his maintaining that it is a sacrament, and a sacrament contained under the bread, is an error not to be tolerated. From this has arisen that false interpretation of sacramental eating, because it was imagined that even the wicked and profane, however far estranged from Christ, eat his body. But the flesh of Christ itself in the mystery of the Supper is no less a spiritual matter than eternal salvation. From this we conclude that all who are devoid of the Spirit of Christ can no more eat the flesh of Christ than they can drink wine that has no taste. Surely Christ is shamefully torn apart when his body, as though lifeless and without any power, is prostituted to unbelievers. This is plainly contrary to his words: "He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood dwells in me, and I in him" (John 6:56). They object that he is not there speaking of sacramental eating. This I grant, provided they will not keep stumbling over this stone — that his flesh itself is eaten without any benefit. I should like to know how they hold it in once they have eaten it. Here, in my judgment, they will find no way out. But they object that the ingratitude of man cannot in any respect detract from, or interfere with, faith in the promises of God. I admit and hold that the power of the sacrament remains whole, however the wicked may labor with all their might to destroy it. Still, it is one thing to be offered, another to be received. Christ gives this spiritual food and holds forth this spiritual drink to all. Some eat eagerly; others contemptuously reject it. Will their rejection cause the food and drink to lose their nature? They will say that this comparison supports their opinion — namely, that the flesh of Christ, though it be without taste, is still flesh. But I deny that it can be eaten without the taste of faith; or, if it is more agreeable to speak with Augustine, I deny that men carry away more from the sacrament than they gather in the vessel of faith. So nothing is taken away from the sacrament; indeed, its reality and effectiveness remain unimpaired, even though the wicked, after partaking of it outwardly, go away empty. If, again, they object that it detracts from the saying "This is my body" if the wicked receive corruptible bread and nothing more, it is easy to answer that God wills his truth to be recognized, not in the bare receiving, but in the constancy of his goodness, since he is ready to perform — indeed, freely offers to the unworthy — what they reject. The integrity of the sacrament, an integrity the whole world cannot violate, lies here: that the flesh and blood of Christ are no less truly given to the unworthy than to the elect believers of God. And yet it is true that, just as rain falling on hard rock runs off because it cannot penetrate, so the wicked, by their hardness, repel the grace of God and keep it from reaching them. We may add that it is no more possible to receive Christ without faith than it is for seed to sprout in fire. They ask how Christ can have come for the condemnation of some unless they unworthily receive him. But this is absurd, since we nowhere read that they bring death upon themselves by receiving Christ unworthily, but by rejecting him. They get no help from the parable in which Christ says that the seed which fell among thorns sprang up but was afterward choked (Matt. 13:7), because there he is speaking of the effect of a temporary faith — a faith that those who place Judas, in this respect, on the same footing as Peter do not think necessary to the eating of the flesh and the drinking of the blood of Christ. Indeed, their error is refuted by that same parable, when Christ says that some seed fell on the path, and some on stony ground, and yet neither took root. From this it follows that the hardness of those who profess belief is an obstacle that keeps Christ from reaching them. All who would have our salvation furthered by this sacrament will find nothing more fitting than to lead believers to the fountain, that they may draw life from the Son of God. Its dignity is amply enough commended when we hold that it is a help by which we may be grafted into the body of Christ, or, being already grafted in, may be more and more united to him, until that union is completed in heaven. They object that Paul could not have made them guilty of the body and blood of the Lord if they had not partaken of them (1 Cor. 11:27). I answer that they were not condemned for having eaten, but only for having profaned the ordinance by trampling underfoot the pledge they ought to have received with reverence — the pledge of sacred union with God.

The aim of these remarks is to lead godly readers to reflect how dangerous it is, in matters of such difficulty, to wander from the simple Word of God to the dreams of our own brains. What has been said above should free us from all anxiety in this matter. For the godly soul to truly lay hold of Christ in the sacrament, it must rise to heaven. But if the purpose of the sacrament is to aid the weakness of the human mind, helping it to rise upward so as to perceive the height of spiritual mysteries, then those who stop short at the outward sign stray from the right path of seeking Christ. What then? Can we deny that the worship is superstitious when men fall down before bread in order to worship Christ in it? The Council of Nicaea undoubtedly intended to meet this evil when it forbade us to fix our humble attention on the visible signs. And for no other reason was it formerly the custom, before consecration, to call aloud to the people to lift up their hearts — sursum corda. Scripture itself, too, besides carefully recounting the ascension of Christ, by which he withdrew his bodily presence from our eyes and company so as to make us abandon all carnal thoughts of him, whenever it mentions him directs us to lift up our minds and seek him in heaven, seated at the right hand of the Father (Col. 3:2). According to this rule, we ought rather to have adored him spiritually in the heavenly glory than to have devised that perilous kind of adoration filled with gross and carnal ideas of God. Those, therefore, who devised the adoration of the sacrament not only dreamed it up on their own, without any authority from Scripture, where no mention of it can be shown — for it would not have been left out, had it been pleasing to God — but, disregarding Scripture, they forsook the living God and fabricated a god for themselves after the craving of their own hearts. For what is idolatry, if not to worship the gifts in place of the giver? Here the sin is twofold: the honor robbed from God is transferred to the creature, and God is, moreover, dishonored by the pollution and profaning of his own goodness, while his holy sacrament is turned into a detestable idol. Let us, on the contrary, that we may not fall into the same pit, wholly confine our eyes, ears, hearts, minds, and tongues to the sacred teaching of God. For this is the school of the Holy Spirit, that best of masters, in which such progress is made that, while nothing is to be learned anywhere else, we must willingly be ignorant of whatever is not taught there.

Nor will I treat the other clause as nothing; for I hold that, since the promise is joined to the command, the promise is so included under the command that it cannot possibly be separated from it. This will be made clearer by an example. God gave a command when he said, "Call upon me," and added a promise, "I will deliver you" (Ps. 50:15). If anyone were to call upon Peter or Paul and base it on this promise, would not all cry out that he does so in error? Let us remember, then, that this promise has been given to those who keep the command joined to it, and that those who turn the sacrament to another end have no support from the Word of God. We have already shown how the mystery of the sacred Supper contributes to our faith in God. But since the Lord not only reminds us of this great gift of his goodness, as we explained earlier, but in a manner passes it from hand to hand and presses us to recognize it, he at the same time warns us not to be ungrateful for the kindness thus bestowed, but rather to proclaim it with the praise that is fitting, and to celebrate it with thanksgiving. Accordingly, when he delivered the institution of the sacrament to the apostles, he taught them to do it in remembrance of him — which Paul interprets as "to show forth his death" (1 Cor. 11:26). Here, again, we see the aim of the sacrament — namely, to keep us in remembrance of Christ's death. When we are ordered to show forth the Lord's death until he comes again, all that is meant is that we should, with confession of the mouth, proclaim what our faith has recognized in the sacrament — namely, that the death of Christ is our life. This is the second use of the sacrament, and it relates to outward confession.

Third, the Lord intended it to be a kind of exhortation — than which no other could more strongly urge or rouse us — both to purity and holiness of life, and also to charity, peace, and concord. Whether we are to be strengthened in faith, or trained in confession, or stirred up to our duty, there is need of preaching. Nothing, therefore, can be more absurd than to turn the Supper into a silent action. This is done under the tyranny of the Pope, where the whole effect of consecration is made to depend on the intention of the priest, as if it in no way concerned the people, to whom above all the mystery ought to have been explained. This error has arisen from not observing that those promises by which consecration is effected are intended, not for the elements themselves, but for those who receive them. Christ does not address the bread and tell it to become his body, but bids his disciples eat, and promises them the communion of his body and blood. And, following the order Paul sets down, the promises are to be offered to believers along with the bread and the cup. So indeed it is. We are not to imagine some magical incantation and think it enough to mutter the words, as though the elements heard them; rather, we are to regard those words as a living sermon, meant to build up the hearers, to penetrate their minds, to be impressed and seated in their hearts, and to exert its power in fulfilling what it promises. For these reasons it is clear that the setting apart of the sacrament, which some insist on, so that an extra portion of it may be carried to the sick, is useless. Either they will receive it without hearing the words of institution read, or the minister will join the true explanation of the mystery to the sign. In the silent dispensing of it, there is both abuse and defect. If the promises are recited, and the mystery is expounded, so that those who are to receive may receive it to their benefit, there can be no doubt that this is the true consecration. I admit it; but in a matter of such importance, where error is so dangerous, nothing is safer than to follow the truth.

40¶

We see that this sacred bread of the Lord's Supper is spiritual food. To those who worship God in godliness, it is sweet and good to taste, and no less wholesome. When they taste it, they feel that Christ is their life. They are moved to give thanks, and they are stirred up to love one another. But on the other hand, this same bread becomes the most harmful poison to everyone whom it does not nourish and strengthen in faith, and whom it does not move to thanksgiving and love. For just as bodily food, when it enters a stomach already burdened with disease, becomes itself spoiled and corrupted, and harms rather than nourishes, so this spiritual food also, if it is given to a soul polluted with malice and wickedness, plunges that soul into greater ruin. This is not because of any defect in the food, but because, as Scripture says, "to those who are defiled and unbelieving, nothing is pure" (Titus 1:15), however much the food may be sanctified by the Lord's blessing. For as Paul says, "Whoever eats this bread and drinks this cup of the Lord unworthily shall be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord," and "eats and drinks judgment to himself, not discerning the Lord's body" (1 Cor. 11:27, 29).

People of this kind, who without any spark of faith and without any zeal for love rush forward like swine to seize the Lord's Supper, do not at all discern the Lord's body. Since they do not believe that this body is their life, they put every possible insult upon it. They strip it of all its dignity, and they profane and defile it by the way they receive it, because while they are alienated and estranged from their brothers and sisters, they dare to mix the sacred symbol of Christ's body with their quarrels. It is no thanks to them that the body of Christ is not torn to pieces. This is why they are justly held guilty of the body and blood of the Lord, which with sacrilegious irreverence they so vilely pollute.

By eating unworthily in this way, they bring judgment upon themselves. For though they have no faith in Christ, yet by receiving the sacrament they profess to rest their salvation in him alone and to renounce all other confidence. So they become their own accusers; they bear witness against themselves; they seal their own condemnation. And then, divided and cut off from their brothers and sisters by hatred and ill will, that is, cut off from the members of Christ, they have no part in Christ, and yet they declare that the only safety is to share in communion with Christ and be joined to him.

For this reason Paul commands a person to examine himself before he eats of that bread and drinks of that cup (1 Cor. 11:28). By this, as I understand it, he means that each individual should look within himself and consider several things. First, whether with inward confidence of heart he relies on the salvation obtained by Christ, and confesses it with his mouth. Second, whether with zeal for purity and holiness he strives to imitate Christ; whether, following his example, he is ready to give himself to his brothers and sisters, and to hold himself as one in common with those with whom he holds Christ in common; whether, just as he himself is regarded by Christ, he in turn regards all his brothers and sisters as members of his own body, and like his own members desires to cherish, defend, and help them. This is not because the duties of faith and love can now be made perfect in us, but because we ought to strive and seek, with all our heart, to grow daily in our faith.

By this definition, every person who ever has been or now is upon the earth would be shut out from the use of this sacrament. For if we are to seek our worthiness in ourselves, it is all over with us; nothing awaits us but despair and deadly ruin. Though we struggle to the utmost, we will not only make no progress, but we will be most unworthy at the very moment we have labored hardest to make ourselves worthy.

To heal this sore, they devised a way of obtaining worthiness, namely, that after we have examined ourselves as far as we can and taken account of all our actions, we are to make up for our unworthiness by contrition, confession, and satisfaction. I have spoken of the true nature of this kind of expiation in its proper place (Book 3, chapter 4, sections 2, 17, 27). As far as our present subject is concerned, I say that such things give poor and fleeting comfort to alarmed and downcast consciences that are struck with terror at their sins. For if the Lord, by his prohibition, admits no one to share in his Supper except the righteous and the innocent, every person would need to be cautious before feeling secure of that righteousness of his own which he is told God requires. But how are we to be assured that those who have done what was in their power have fulfilled their duty to God? Even if we were assured of this, who would dare to assure himself that he had done what lay within him? So, since there is no sure security for our worthiness, access to the Supper would always be barred by the dreadful sentence, "He who eats and drinks unworthily eats and drinks judgment to himself."

42¶

It is now easy to judge what the nature of this teaching is, and who its author is, the teaching that prevails in the papacy, which by its inhuman harshness deprives and robs wretched sinners, weighed down with sorrow and trembling, of the comfort of this sacrament, a sacrament in which all that is delightful in the gospel was set before them. Surely the devil could have no quicker way of destroying people than by deceiving them in this manner, and so shutting them out from the taste and savor of this food with which their most merciful Father in heaven was pleased to feed them. Therefore, so that we may not plunge over such a cliff, let us remember that this sacred feast is medicine for the sick, comfort for the sinner, and generosity for the poor; while to the healthy, the righteous, and the rich, if any such could be found, it would be of no value at all.

For while Christ is given to us there for food, we recognize that without him we fail, waste away, and perish, just as hunger destroys the strength of the body. And since he is given for life, we recognize that without him we are certainly dead. Therefore the best and only worthiness we can bring to God is to offer him our own vileness and, if I may put it this way, our unworthiness, so that his mercy may make us worthy; to despair of ourselves, so that we may be comforted in him; to humble ourselves, so that we may be lifted up by him; to accuse ourselves, so that we may be justified by him; and moreover to aspire to that unity which he commends to us in the Supper, so that, as he makes us all one in himself, we may desire to have all one soul, one heart, one tongue.

If we weigh and meditate on these things, we may be shaken, but we will never be overwhelmed by thoughts such as these: How shall we, who are devoid of all good, polluted by the defilements of sin, and half dead, worthily eat the body of the Lord? We shall instead consider that we, who are poor, are coming to a generous giver; sick, to a physician; sinful, to the author of righteousness; in short, dead, to him who gives life. We shall consider that the worthiness which God commands consists especially in faith, which places all things in Christ and nothing in ourselves, and in love, a love which, even though it is imperfect, it may be enough to offer to God, so that he may increase it, since it cannot be fully rendered.

Some, who agree with us in holding that worthiness consists in faith and love, have nevertheless gone badly astray about the measure of that worthiness. They demand a perfection of faith to which nothing can be added, and a love equal to the love Christ showed toward us. And in this way, just like the other group, they shut out all people from access to this sacred feast. For if their view were well founded, everyone who receives would receive unworthily, since all, without exception, are guilty and chargeable with imperfection. And surely it would be too foolish, not to say senseless, to require for the receiving of the sacrament a perfection that would make the sacrament empty and unnecessary. For the sacrament was not instituted for the perfect, but for the weak and feeble, to stir up, awaken, urge on, and exercise the feeling of faith and love, and at the same time to correct the deficiency of both.

43¶

As for the outward form of the ordinance, it makes no difference whether or not believers take the bread into their hands and divide it among themselves, or each eats what is given to him; whether they return the cup to the deacon or hand it to their neighbor; whether the bread is leavened or unleavened, and the wine red or white. These things are indifferent, and are left free to the Church, although it is certain that it was the custom of the ancient Church for all to receive into their hand. And Christ said, "Take this and divide it among yourselves" (Luke 22:17). History tells us that leavened, ordinary bread was used until the time of Alexander, the bishop of Rome, who was the first to take pleasure in unleavened bread. I cannot see for what reason, unless it was to draw the wondering eyes of the people by the novelty of the spectacle, rather than to train them in sound religion. I appeal to all who have the least zeal for godliness: do they not plainly perceive both how much more brightly the glory of God is displayed here, and how much more abundant spiritual comfort is felt by believers, than in these stiff and theatrical follies, which have no other use than to impress the gaping crowd? They call it restraining the people by religion, when in fact the people, dazed and bewildered, are dragged this way and that by superstition.

Should anyone choose to defend such inventions by their age, I am well aware how ancient is the use of chrism and exorcism in baptism, and how, not long after the age of the apostles, the Supper was corrupted by adulteration. Such, indeed, is the boldness of human presumption, which cannot hold itself in check, but is always toying and trifling with the mysteries of God. But let us remember that God sets such value on obedience to his Word that by it he would have us judge his angels and the whole world.

If all this mass of ceremonies were set aside, the sacrament could be celebrated in the most fitting way, if it were given to the Church very often, at least once a week. It should begin with public prayer. Next, a sermon should be preached. Then the minister, having placed bread and wine on the table, should read the institution of the Supper. He should then explain the promises that are given in it, and at the same time keep back from communion all those who are barred by the prohibition of the Lord. Afterward he should pray that the Lord, with the same kindness with which he has bestowed this sacred food upon us, would also shape and instruct us to receive it with faith and gratitude, and that, since we are of ourselves unworthy, he would by his mercy make us worthy of the feast. Here either a psalm should be sung, or something read, while the faithful, in order, share in the sacred feast, the minister breaking the bread and giving it to the people. When the Supper is ended, an exhortation should be given to sincere faith and confession of faith, to love, and to lives befitting Christians. Lastly, thanks should be offered, and the praises of God should be sung. When this is done, the church should be dismissed in peace.

44¶

What we have said so far about the sacrament shows abundantly that it was not instituted to be received once a year, and that carelessly (as is now commonly the custom), but so that all Christians might have it in frequent use, and might often call to mind the sufferings of Christ, thereby supporting and strengthening their faith. By it they stir themselves up to sing the praises of God and to proclaim his goodness; and they cherish and demonstrate toward one another that mutual love whose bond they see in the unity of the body of Christ. As often as we share in the symbol of our Savior's body, as if a pledge were given and received, we bind ourselves to one another in all the duties of love, so that none of us may do anything to offend a brother or sister, or fail to do anything by which we can help them when necessity demands and opportunity allows.

That this was the practice of the apostolic Church, Luke informs us in the Acts, when he says that "they continued steadfastly in the apostles' teaching and fellowship, and in the breaking of bread, and in prayers" (Acts 2:42). So we ought always to make sure that no meeting of the Church is held without the Word, prayer, the dispensing of the Supper, and almsgiving. We can gather from Paul that this was the order observed by the Corinthians, and it is certain that this was the practice for many ages afterward. So, by the ancient canons that are attributed to Anacletus and Calixtus, after the consecration was made, all were to share in communion who did not wish to be outside the bounds of the Church. And in those ancient canons that bear the name Apostolic, it is said that those who do not stay to the end, and do not partake of the sacred communion, are to be corrected, as causing disturbance to the Church. In the Council of Antioch it was decreed that those who come into the church, hear the Scriptures, and abstain from communion are to be removed from the Church until they amend their fault. And although in the first Council of Toulouse this was softened, or at least stated in milder terms, yet there too it was decreed that those who, after hearing the sermon, never communicated were to be admonished, and if they still abstained after admonition, they were to be excluded.

45¶

By these decrees, holy men wished to keep and ensure the practice of frequent communion, as it had been handed down by the apostles themselves. They saw that this practice, while it was most beneficial to believers, was gradually falling out of use through the negligence of the people. Of his own age, Augustine testifies: "The sacrament of the unity of our Lord's body is, in some places, provided daily, and in others at certain intervals, at the Lord's table; and at that table some partake to life, and others to destruction" (Augustine, Tractate 26 on John 6). And in his first letter to Januarius he says: "Some communicate daily in the body and blood of the Lord; others receive it on certain days. In some places, not a day passes on which it is not offered; in others, it is offered only on the Sabbath and the Lord's day; in others, on the Lord's day only."

But since, as we have said, the people were sometimes negligent, holy men pressed them with severe rebukes, so that they might not seem to wink at their laziness. We have an example of this in Chrysostom, on the letter to the Ephesians (Homily 26). "It was not said to the man who dishonored the feast, 'Why have you not taken your seat?' but 'How did you come in?' (Mt. 22:12). Whoever does not partake of the sacred rites is wicked and shameless in being present. Suppose someone who was invited to a feast were to come in, wash his hands, take his seat, and seem to prepare to eat, and then taste nothing. Would he not, I ask, insult both the feast and the host? So you, standing among those who prepare themselves by prayer to receive the sacred food, profess to be one of their number by the mere fact that you do not leave, and yet you do not partake. Would it not have been better not to have shown up at all? You say, 'I am unworthy.' Then neither were you worthy of the communion of prayer, which is the preparation for receiving the sacred mystery."

46¶

Most certainly, the custom that prescribes communion once a year is an invention of the devil, by whatever means it may have been introduced. They say that Zephyrinus was the author of the decree, though it is not possible to believe that it was the same as we now have it. It may be that, given the times then, he did not, by his ordinance, advise badly for the Church. For there can be no doubt that at that time the sacred Supper was given to the faithful at every meeting, and there can be no doubt that a great many of them communicated. But since it almost never happened that all could communicate at the same time, and since it was necessary that those who were mixed among the profane and the idolaters should testify to their faith by some external symbol, this holy man, with a view to order and government, appointed that day, so that on it the whole of Christendom might give a confession of their faith by sharing in the Lord's Supper.

The ordinance of Zephyrinus, which was otherwise good, later generations perverted, when they made it a fixed law of one communion in the year. The result is that almost everyone, once they have communicated, sleep on secure, as if they were free of all obligation for the rest of the year. It ought to have been far otherwise. At least each week the table of the Lord ought to have been spread for the company of Christians, and the promises declared on which we might then spiritually feed. No one, indeed, ought to be forced, but all ought to be exhorted and stirred up; and the dullness of the sluggish ought also to be rebuked, so that all, like people starving, should come to the feast.

It was not without cause, then, that I complained at the outset that this practice had been introduced by the cunning of the devil, a practice which, by prescribing one day in the year, makes the whole year a year of laziness. We can see, indeed, that this corrupt abuse had already crept in by the time of Chrysostom; but at the same time we can also see how much it displeased him. For he complains in bitter terms, in the passage I quoted just now, that there was so great an inequality in this matter, that they did not approach often, at other times of the year, even when prepared, but only at Easter, though unprepared. Then he exclaims: "O custom! O presumption! In vain, then, is the daily offering made; in vain do we stand at the altar. There is no one who partakes along with us." So far is he from having approved the practice by lending his authority to it.

47¶

From the same forge came another decree, which snatched, or rather robbed, half of the Supper from the greater part of the people of God, namely, the symbol of the blood. Forbidden to the laity and the profane (such are the titles they give to God's heritage), it became the special possession of a few shaven and anointed individuals. The decree of the eternal God is that all are to drink. An upstart dares to make this obsolete and to abolish it by a new and contrary law, proclaiming that all are not to drink. And so that such lawmakers may not seem to fight against their God without any reason, they make a pretext of the dangers that might happen if the sacred cup were given to all without distinction, as if these dangers had not been foreseen and provided for by the eternal wisdom of God.

Then they reason cleverly, so they suppose, that the one is enough for the two. For if the body is, as they say, the whole Christ, who cannot be separated from his body, then the blood is included with the body by concomitance. Here we see how far our human sense falls in step with God whenever, to any degree at all, it begins to rage and run wild with the reins loosened. The Lord, pointing to the bread, says, "This is my body." Then, pointing to the cup, he calls it his blood. The audacity of human reason objects and says: the bread is the blood, the wine is the body, as if the Lord had distinguished his body from his blood, both by words and by signs, for no reason, and as if it had ever been heard that the body of Christ, or his blood, is called both God and man. Certainly, if he had meant to designate himself wholly, he might have said, "It is I," according to the way Scripture speaks, and not, "This is my body," "This is my blood." But wishing to help the weakness of our faith, he set the cup apart from the bread, to show that he is no less sufficient as drink than as food.

Now, if one part is taken away, we can find only half of the elements in what remains. Therefore, even if it were true, as they pretend, that the blood is in the bread, and, on the other hand, the body in the cup, by concomitance, yet they deprive godly people of that confirmation of faith which Christ delivered as necessary. Bidding farewell, then, to their subtleties, let us hold fast to the benefit that, by the ordinance of Christ, is obtained through a double pledge.

But why argue about a fact that is perfectly well known? Look at all the Greek and Latin writers. Passages of the same kind appear everywhere. Nor did this practice fall out of use as long as there was one particle of integrity in the Church. Gregory, whom you may justly call the last bishop of Rome, says that it was still observed in his own age.

50¶

Third, why did our Savior say of the bread simply, "Take, eat," but of the cup, "Drink of it, all of you," as if he had deliberately intended to guard against the cunning of Satan? Fourth, if, as they will have it, the Lord honored only priests with his Supper, what man would ever have dared to call to it strangers whom the Lord had excluded, and to give them a share in a gift that was not in his own power to give, without any command from the one who alone could give it? Indeed, what presumption do they show in our own day in distributing the symbol of Christ's body to the common people, if they have no command or example from the Lord?

Fifth, did Paul lie when he said to the Corinthians, "I received from the Lord that which I also delivered to you" (1 Cor. 11:23)? What he delivered, he afterward declares to be this: that all should share in both symbols without distinction. But if Paul received from the Lord that all were to be admitted without distinction, let those who drive away almost the whole people of God consider from whom they have received their practice, since they cannot now pretend to have their authority from God, with whom there is no "yes and no" (2 Cor. 1:19, 20). And yet they dare to cloak these abominations with the name of the Church, and defend them under that pretext, as if those antichrists were the Church, who so recklessly trample under foot, lay waste, and abolish the teaching and the ordinances of Christ; or as if the apostolic Church, in which religion flourished in full vigor, were not the Church.
