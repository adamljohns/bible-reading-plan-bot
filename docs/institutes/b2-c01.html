Book 2 — Argument. ARGUMENT. The First Part of the Apostles’ Creed—viz. the knowledge of God the Creator, being disposed of, we now come to the Second Part, which relates to the knowledge of God as a Redeemer in Christ. The subjects treated of accordingly are, first , the Occasion of Redemption—viz. Adam’s fall; and, secondly , Redemption itself. The first five chapters are devoted to the former subject, and the remainder to the latter. Under the Occasion of Redemption, the Fall is considered not only in a general way, but also specially in its effects. Hence the first four chapters treat of original sin, free will, the corruption of human nature, and the operation of God in the heart. The fifth chapter contains a refutation of the arguments usually urged in support of free will. The subject of redemption may be reduced to five particular heads: I. The character of him in whom salvation for lost man must be sought, Chap. 6. II. How he was manifested to the world, namely, in a twofold manner. First, under the Law. Here the Decalogue is expounded, and some other points relating to the law discussed, Chap. 7 and 8. Secondly, under the Gospel. Here the resemblance and difference of the two dispensations are considered, Chap. 9, 10, 11. III. What kind of person Christ was, and behaved to be, in order to perform the office of Mediator—viz. God and man in one person, Chap. 12, 13, 14. IV. For what end he was sent into the world by the Father. Here Christ’s prophetical, kingly, and priestly offices are considered, Chap. 15. V. In what way, or by what successive steps, Christ fulfilled the office of our Redeemer, Chap. 16. Here are considered his crucifixion, death, burial, descent to hell, resurrection, ascension to heaven, and seat at the right hand of the Father, together with the practical use of the whole doctrine. Chapter 17 contains an answer to the question, Whether Christ is properly said to have merited the grace of God for us.

Through the Fall and Revolt of Adam, the Whole Human Race Made Accursed and Degenerate — Of Original Sin

I. How necessary the knowledge of ourselves is, its nature, the danger of mistake, its leading parts, sect. 1, 2, 3. II. The causes of Adam’s fearful fall, sect. 4. III. The effects of the fall extending to Adam’s posterity, and all the creatures, sect. 5, to the end of the Chapter, where the nature, propagation, and effect of original sin are considered.

  3. 3. Different views taken by carnal wisdom and by conscience, which appeals to divine justice as its standard. The knowledge of ourselves, consisting of two parts, the former of which having already been discussed, the latter is here considered.
  4. 4. In considering this latter part, two points to be considered; 1. How it happened that Adam involved himself and the whole human race in this dreadful calamity. This the result not of sensual intemperance, but of infidelity (the source of other heinous sins), which led to revolt from God, from whom all true happiness must be derived. An enumeration of the other sins produced by the infidelity of the first man.

This depravation communicated to the whole posterity of Adam, but not in the way supposed by the Pelagians and Celestians.

1¶

It was not without reason that the ancient proverb so strongly commended to man the knowledge of himself. For if it is thought disgraceful to be ignorant of the things that belong to the business of life, much more disgraceful is ignorance of ourselves, by which we miserably deceive ourselves in matters of the highest importance, and so walk blindfold. But the more useful the precept is, the more careful we must be not to apply it wrongly, as we see certain philosophers have done. For when they urge man to know himself, they give as the reason that he may not be ignorant of his own excellence and dignity. They want him to see nothing in himself but what will fill him with vain confidence and puff him up with pride. But self-knowledge consists in this: first, that when we reflect on what God gave us at our creation, and still graciously continues to give, we perceive how great the excellence of our nature would have been had it remained whole, and at the same time remember that we have nothing of our own, but depend entirely on God, from whom we hold at his pleasure whatever he has seen fit to bestow; second, that when we view our miserable condition since Adam's fall, all confidence and boasting are overthrown, we blush for shame, and are made truly humble. For as God at first formed us in his own image, so that he might lift our minds to the pursuit of virtue and the contemplation of eternal life, so, to keep us from carelessly burying those noble qualities that distinguish us from the lower animals, it is important to know that we were endowed with reason and intelligence in order that we might cultivate a holy and honorable life, and regard a blessed immortality as the goal for which we were destined. At the same time, it is impossible to think of our first dignity without being at once reminded of the sad spectacle of our disgrace and corruption, ever since we fell from our original state in the person of our first parent. In this way we become dissatisfied with ourselves and truly humble, while we are kindled with new desires to seek after God, in whom each of us may regain those good qualities of which all are found to be utterly destitute.

2¶

In examining ourselves, the search that divine truth commands, and the knowledge that it requires, are such as to turn us away from everything like confidence in our own powers, to leave us without any ground for boasting, and so to incline us to submission. This is the course we must follow if we would reach the true goal, both in thought and in practice. I am well aware how much more attractive is the view that invites us to dwell on our good qualities rather than to look at what must overwhelm us with shame, namely, our miserable poverty and disgrace. Nothing is more welcome to the human mind than flattery, and so, when it is told that its gifts are of a high order, it is apt to be excessively credulous. It is not strange, then, that the greater part of mankind have gone so badly astray in this matter. Because of the inborn self-love by which all are blinded, we most willingly persuade ourselves that we do not possess a single quality that deserves to be hated; and so, without any support from outside, the very foolish idea gains general belief that man is entirely sufficient of himself for all the purposes of a good and happy life. If some are inclined to think more modestly, and grant something to God so that they may not seem to claim everything as their own, still, in making the division, they apportion matters so that the chief ground of confidence and boasting always remains with themselves. Then, if a discourse is delivered that flatters the pride springing up of its own accord in man's inmost heart, nothing seems more delightful. Accordingly, in every age, the one who is most eager to extol the excellence of human nature is received with the loudest applause. But whatever this proclaiming of human excellence may be, by teaching man to rest in himself it does nothing more than charm by its sweetness, and at the same time so deceive as to drown in perdition all who agree with it. For what does it profit us to go forward in vain confidence, to deliberate, resolve, plan, and attempt what we think suited to our purpose, and at the very outset to prove lacking and destitute both of sound understanding and of true virtue, while we still confidently press on until we rush headlong into destruction? Yet this is the best that can happen to those who put confidence in their own powers. Whoever, therefore, listens to those teachers who merely occupy us with contemplating our good qualities, so far from making progress in self-knowledge, will be plunged into the most destructive ignorance.

3¶

While revealed truth agrees with the general consent of mankind in teaching that the second part of wisdom consists in knowing ourselves, they differ greatly as to the method by which this knowledge is to be gained. In the judgment of the flesh, a man thinks his self-knowledge complete when, with overconfidence in his own understanding and integrity, he takes courage and spurs himself on to virtuous deeds, and when, declaring war on vice, he does his utmost to attain to what is honorable and fair. But he who tests himself by the standard of divine justice finds nothing to inspire him with confidence; and so, the more thorough his self-examination, the deeper his despondency. Giving up all dependence on himself, he feels that he is utterly incapable of rightly ordering his conduct. It is not God's will, however, that we should forget the first dignity that he bestowed on our first parents, a dignity that may well stir us up to the pursuit of goodness and justice. It is impossible for us to think of our first origin, or of the end for which we were created, without being moved to meditate on immortality and to seek the kingdom of God. But such meditation, so far from raising our spirits, rather casts them down and makes us humble. For what is our origin? One from which we have fallen. What is the end of our creation? One from which we have altogether strayed, so that, weary of our miserable lot, we groan, and in groaning sigh for a dignity now lost. When we say that man should see nothing in himself that can raise his spirits, we mean that he possesses nothing in which he can proudly pride himself. Hence, in considering the knowledge that man ought to have of himself, it seems proper to divide it in this way: first, to consider the end for which he was created, and the qualities, by no means contemptible ones, with which he was endowed, so urging him to meditate on divine worship and the future life; and second, to consider his faculties, or rather his lack of faculties, a lack which, once perceived, will destroy all his confidence and cover him with confusion. The aim of the first view is to teach him what his duty is; of the second, to make him aware how far he is able to perform it. We shall treat of both in their proper order.

4¶

Since the act that God punished so severely must have been not a trivial fault but a heinous crime, it will be necessary to consider the particular nature of the sin that brought about Adam's fall and provoked God to inflict such fearful vengeance on the whole human race. The common idea that it was sensual intemperance is childish. The sum and substance of all virtues could not consist in abstaining from a single fruit amid a general abundance of every delicacy that could be desired, when the earth, in its happy fruitfulness, yielded not only abundance but also endless variety. We must, therefore, look deeper than sensual intemperance. The prohibition against touching the tree of the knowledge of good and evil was a test of obedience, so that Adam, by keeping it, might prove his willing submission to the command of God. For the very name shows that the purpose of the precept was to keep him content with his lot, and not to allow him arrogantly to aspire beyond it. The promise, which gave him hope of eternal life as long as he should eat of the tree of life, and, on the other hand, the fearful threat of death the moment he should taste of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, were meant to test and exercise his faith. Hence it is not hard to infer in what way Adam provoked the wrath of God. Augustine, indeed, is not far from the mark when he says (On Psalm 19) that pride was the beginning of all evil, because, had not man's ambition carried him higher than he was permitted to go, he might have continued in his first estate. A fuller definition, however, must be drawn from the kind of temptation that Moses describes. When, by the subtlety of the devil, the woman faithlessly abandoned the command of God, her fall plainly had its origin in disobedience. Paul confirms this when he says that by the disobedience of one man all were destroyed. At the same time, it is to be observed that the first man revolted against the authority of God, not only in allowing himself to be ensnared by the wiles of the devil, but also by despising the truth and turning aside to lies. Surely, when the word of God is despised, all reverence for him is gone. His majesty cannot be rightly honored among us, nor his worship kept whole, unless we hang, as it were, upon his lips. Unbelief, therefore, was at the root of the revolt. From unbelief, in turn, sprang ambition and pride, together with ingratitude, because Adam, by longing for more than was allotted to him, showed contempt for the great generosity with which God had enriched him. It was surely monstrous impiety that a son of the earth should think it a small thing to have been made in the likeness of God unless he were also made the equal of God. If the apostasy by which man withdraws from the authority of his Maker, and even insolently shakes off his allegiance to him, is a foul and detestable crime, it is useless to make light of the sin of Adam. Nor was it simple apostasy. It was joined with a foul insult to God, as the guilty pair agreed with Satan's slanders when he charged God with malice, envy, and falsehood. In short, unbelief opened the door to ambition, and ambition was the parent of rebellion, as man cast off the fear of God and gave free rein to his lust. Hence Bernard rightly says that in the present day a door of salvation is opened to us when we receive the gospel with our ears, just as death was admitted by the same entrance when it was thrown open to Satan. Adam would never have dared to show any resistance to the command of God if he had not disbelieved his word. The strongest curb to keep all his affections under proper restraint would have been the belief that nothing was better than to cultivate righteousness by obeying the commands of God, and that the highest possible happiness was to be loved by him. The latter clause of this sentence is omitted in the French.

5¶

As Adam's spiritual life would have consisted in remaining united and bound to his Maker, so estrangement from him was the death of his soul. Nor is it strange that he who perverted the whole order of nature in heaven and earth corrupted his race by his revolt. "The whole creation groaneth," says St. Paul, "being made subject to vanity, not willingly" (Romans 8:20, 22). If the reason is asked, there can be no doubt that creation bears part of the punishment deserved by man, for whose use all other creatures were made. Therefore, since through man's fault a curse has spread above and below, over all the regions of the world, there is nothing unreasonable in its extending to all his offspring. After the heavenly image in man was effaced, he was not only punished himself by the withdrawal of the ornaments in which he had been clothed, namely, wisdom, virtue, justice, truth, and holiness, and by having put in their place those dreadful plagues, blindness, impotence, vanity, impurity, and unrighteousness, but he also involved his posterity and plunged them into the same wretchedness. This is the hereditary corruption to which early Christian writers gave the name of Original Sin, meaning by the term the depravation of a nature that was formerly good and pure. The subject gave rise to much discussion, since nothing is further from common understanding than that the fault of one should make all guilty, and so become a common sin. This seems to be the reason why the oldest teachers of the church only touch on the point obscurely, or at least do not explain it as clearly as it required. This timidity, however, could not prevent the rise of a Pelagius with his profane fiction, that Adam sinned only to his own hurt and did no hurt to his posterity. By thus craftily hiding the disease, Satan tried to make it incurable. But when it was clearly proved from Scripture that the sin of the first man passed to all his posterity, they resorted to the quibble that it passed by imitation and not by propagation. The orthodox, therefore, and especially Augustine, labored to show that we are not corrupted by wickedness acquired later, but bring an inborn corruption from the very womb. It was the height of impudence to deny this. But no one will wonder at the presumption of the Pelagians and Celestians who has learned from the writings of that holy man how extreme the effrontery of these heretics was. Surely there is no ambiguity in David's confession, "I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me" (Psalm 51:5). His aim in the passage is not to throw blame on his parents; rather, to commend all the more the goodness of God toward him, he rightly repeats the confession of his impurity from his very birth. Since it is clear that there was nothing peculiar in David's case, it follows that it is only an instance of the common lot of the whole human race. All of us, therefore, descending from an impure seed, come into the world tainted with the contagion of sin. Indeed, before we see the light of the sun, we are in God's sight defiled and polluted. "Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? Not one," says the Book of Job (Job 14:4).

6¶

We see, then, that the impurity of parents is passed on to their children, so that all, without exception, are depraved from their origin. The beginning of this depravity will not be found until we go back to the first parent of all as the fountainhead. We must, therefore, hold it as certain that, with regard to human nature, Adam was not merely an ancestor but, as it were, a root, and that accordingly, by his corruption, the whole human race was deservedly corrupted. This is plain from the contrast the Apostle draws between Adam and Christ: "Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned; even so might grace reign through righteousness unto eternal life by Jesus Christ our Lord" (Romans 5:19–21). To what quibble will the Pelagians resort here? That the sin of Adam was spread by imitation! Is the righteousness of Christ, then, of benefit to us only so far as it is an example held out for us to imitate? Can anyone tolerate such blasphemy? But if, beyond all dispute, the righteousness of Christ, and with it life, is ours by communication, it follows that both of these were lost in Adam so that they might be recovered in Christ, while sin and death were brought in by Adam so that they might be abolished in Christ. There is no obscurity in the words, "As by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous." Accordingly, the relation between the two is this: as Adam, by his ruin, involved and ruined us, so Christ, by his grace, restored us to salvation. In this clear light of truth I see no need for a longer or more laborious proof. So too, in the First Epistle to the Corinthians, when Paul wishes to confirm believers in the confident hope of the resurrection, he shows that the life lost in Adam is recovered in Christ (1 Corinthians 15:22). Having already declared that all died in Adam, he now also openly testifies that all are infected with the taint of sin. Condemnation, indeed, could not reach those who are entirely free from blame. But his meaning cannot be made clearer than from the other half of the sentence, in which he shows that the hope of life is restored in Christ. Everyone knows that the only way this is done is when, by a wonderful communication, Christ pours into us the power of his own righteousness, as it is said elsewhere, "The Spirit is life because of righteousness" (1 Corinthians 15:22). Therefore, the only explanation that can be given of the expression "in Adam all died" is that by sinning he not only brought disaster and ruin on himself, but also plunged our nature into the same destruction; and that not merely by one fault, in a matter that does not concern us, but by the corruption into which he himself fell, he infected his whole seed. Paul could never have said that all are "by nature the children of wrath" (Ephesians 2:3) if they had not been cursed from the womb. And it is obvious that the nature referred to there is not nature as God created it, but as corrupted in Adam; for it would have been utterly incongruous to make God the author of death. Adam, therefore, when he corrupted himself, passed on the contagion to all his posterity. For a heavenly Judge, our Savior himself, declares that all are by birth corrupt and depraved when he says that "that which is born of the flesh is fleshy" (John 3:6), and that therefore the gate of life is closed to all until they have been regenerated.

7¶

To understand this subject, there is no need for an anxious discussion (which perplexed the ancient teachers in no small degree) as to whether the soul of the child comes by transmission from the soul of the parent. The French is, "Assavoir, si l'ame du fils procede de la substance de l'ame paternelle, veu que c'est en l'ame que reside le peché originel." That is, whether the soul of the child is derived from the substance of the soul of the parent, seeing that it is in the soul that original sin resides.

The French is, "Les enfans ne descendent point de la generation spirituelle qui les serviteurs de Diu ont du S. Esprit, mais de la generation charnelle qu'ils ont d'Adam." Children descend not from the spiritual generation that the servants of God have of the Holy Spirit, but from the carnal generation that they have of Adam.

8¶

But so that the very thing of which we speak may not be unknown or doubtful, it will be proper to define original sin. (Calvin, on the Council of Trent, 1, Decree of Session 5.) I do not intend, however, to discuss all the definitions that different writers have adopted, but only to bring forward the one that seems to me most in accord with the truth. Original sin, then, may be defined as a hereditary corruption and depravity of our nature, extending to all the parts of the soul, which first makes us liable to the wrath of God, and then produces in us works that Scripture calls works of the flesh. This corruption is repeatedly designated by Paul by the term sin. The French adds, "Sans adjouster Originel" — without adding Original.

The French is, "Car en ce qui est d't, que par Adam nous sommes fait redevables au jugement de Dieu, ce ne'st pas a dire que nous soyons innocens, et que sans avoir merité aucune peine nous portions la folleenchere de son peché: mais pourceque par sa transgression nous sommes tous enveloppés de confusion, il est dit nous avoir tous obligez." For when it is said that by Adam we are made liable to the judgment of God, the meaning is not that we are innocent, and that without having deserved any punishment we are made to pay dearly for his sin, but that because by his transgression we are all covered with confusion, he is said to have bound us all.

In many passages, and especially in his treatise De Peccatorum Merit. et Remiss., book 3, chapter 8.

9¶

I have said, therefore, that all the parts of the soul were possessed by sin from the time Adam revolted from the fountain of righteousness. For not only did the lower appetites entice him, but abominable impiety seized on the very citadel of the mind, and pride pierced to his inmost heart (Romans 7:12; Book 4, chapter 15, sections 10–12), so that it is foolish and meaningless to confine the corruption that came from it to what are called sensual impulses, or to call it a stimulus that allures, excites, and drags into sin only the one part they call sensuality. Here Peter Lombard has shown gross ignorance (Lombard, book 2, distinction 31). When he inquires into the seat of corruption, he says it is in the flesh (as Paul declares), not properly, indeed, but because it is more apparent in the flesh. As if Paul had meant that only a part of the soul, and not the whole nature, was opposed to supernatural grace! Paul himself leaves no room for doubt when he says that corruption does not dwell in one part only, but that no part is free from its deadly taint. For, speaking of corrupt nature, he not only condemns the disordered motions of the appetites, but in particular declares that the understanding is given over to blindness and the heart to depravity (Ephesians 4:17, 18). The third chapter of the Epistle to the Romans is nothing but a description of original sin. The same thing appears more clearly from the manner of renewal. For the spirit, which is set against the old man and the flesh, denotes not only the grace by which the sensual or lower part of the soul is corrected, but includes a complete reformation of all its parts (Ephesians 4:23). And accordingly, Paul commands not only that gross appetites be suppressed, but that we be renewed in the spirit of our mind (Ephesians 4:23), as he elsewhere tells us to be transformed by the renewing of our mind (Romans 12:2). It follows that the part in which the dignity and excellence of the soul are most conspicuous has not only been wounded, but so corrupted that mere healing is not enough. There must be a new nature. How far sin has seized on both the mind and the heart we shall soon see. Here I wished only to note briefly that the whole man, from the crown of the head to the sole of the foot, is so flooded, as it were, that no part remains free from sin, and therefore everything that proceeds from him is imputed as sin. So Paul says that all carnal thoughts and affections are enmity against God, and therefore death (Romans 8:7).

10¶

Let us have done, then, with those who dare to write the name of God on their vices because we say that men are born corrupt. The divine workmanship, which they ought to look for in the nature of Adam while it was still whole and uncorrupted, they absurdly expect to find in their depravity. The blame for our ruin rests with our own carnality, not with God, its only cause being our fall from our original condition. And let no one clamor here that God might have provided better for our safety by preventing Adam's fall. This objection, which is hateful to every godly mind because of the daring presumption it implies, belongs to the mystery of predestination, which will be considered later in its own place (Tertullian, De Præscript.; Calvin, book On Predestination). Meanwhile, let us remember that our ruin is to be blamed on our own depravity, so that we may not insinuate a charge against God himself, the Author of nature. It is true that nature has received a mortal wound, but there is a great difference between a wound inflicted from outside and one inherent in our first condition. It is plain that this wound was inflicted by sin; and therefore we have no ground of complaint except against ourselves. Scripture teaches this carefully. For the Preacher says, "Lo, this only have I found, that God made man upright; but they have sought out many inventions" (Ecclesiastes 7:29). Since man, by the kindness of God, was made upright, but by his own infatuation fell away into vanity, his destruction is plainly to be blamed on himself alone (Athanasius, Oration Against the Idols).

11¶

We say, then, that man is corrupted by a natural viciousness, but not by one that proceeded from nature. In saying that it did not proceed from nature, we mean that it was rather something that came upon man from outside than a property of his substance assigned to him from the beginning. The French is, "Nous nions qu'elle soit de nature, afin de monstrer que c'est plutot une qualité survenue ˆ l'homme qu'une proprieté de sa substance, laquelle ait eté dés le commencement enracinée en lui;" — we deny that it is of nature, in order to show that it is rather a quality added to man than a property of his substance, which has been rooted in him from the beginning.

Self-knowledge, Calvin insists, must begin with the wreckage Adam left us: not a soul slightly tarnished but a whole nature accursed and degenerate, original sin propagated to every child by descent, not merely caught by imitation. This is unfashionable in 2026, an age catechized to tell our children they are good as they are and need only believe in themselves — but it is the truest mercy, for no one seeks a Savior he does not need. Strip away the flattering self-admiration the world preaches and let conscience answer to divine justice; only then does grace become precious. In the home this means raising children who know their hearts are bent from the cradle, not to crush them but so that the cross will one day astonish them. We are not basically fine. We are ruined and beloved — and the gospel speaks only to those honest enough to own the first before they marvel at the second.
