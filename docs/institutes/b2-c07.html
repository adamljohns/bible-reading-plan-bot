1¶

From the whole course of the observations now made, we may infer that the Law was not added about four hundred years after the death of Abraham in order to lead the chosen people away from Christ, but, on the contrary, to keep them in suspense until his coming; to kindle their desire and confirm their expectation, so that they might not lose heart through the long delay. By the Law I understand not only the Ten Commandments, which contain a complete rule of life, but the whole system of religion delivered by the hand of Moses. Moses was not appointed as a Lawgiver to do away with the blessing promised to the race of Abraham; on the contrary, we see that he constantly reminds the Jews of the free covenant which had been made with their fathers, and of which they were heirs, as if he had been sent for the purpose of renewing it. This is shown most clearly by the ceremonies. For what could be more vain or frivolous than for men to reconcile themselves to God by offering him the foul smell produced by burning the fat of beasts? Or to wash away their own impurities by sprinkling themselves with water or blood? In short, the whole legal worship, if considered by itself apart from the types and shadows of corresponding truth, is a mere mockery. Therefore, both in Stephen's address (Acts 7:44) and in the Epistle to the Hebrews, great weight is rightly given to the passage in which God says to Moses, "Look that thou make them after the pattern which was showed thee in the mount" (Exodus 25:40). Had there not been some spiritual end to which they were directed, the Jews, in observing them, would have deluded themselves as much as the Gentiles did in their vanities. Profane men, who have never made religion their serious study, cannot hear of such a multitude of rites without disgust. They not only wonder why God wearied his ancient people with such a mass of ceremonies, but they despise and ridicule them as childish toys. They do this because they do not attend to the end; and if the legal figures are separated from that end, they cannot escape the charge of vanity. But the type shows that God did not enjoin sacrifice in order to occupy his worshipers with earthly exercises, but rather in order to raise their minds to something higher. This is clear even from his own nature. Being a spirit, he is pleased only with spiritual worship. The same thing is attested by the many passages in which the Prophets accuse the Jews of stupidity for imagining that mere sacrifices have any value in the sight of God. Did they mean by this to detract in any respect from the Law? By no means; but as interpreters of its true meaning, they wished in this way to turn the attention of the people to the end which they ought to have kept in view, but from which they generally wandered. From the grace offered to the Jews we may certainly infer that the law was not a stranger to Christ. Moses declared the end of the adoption of the Israelites to be that they should be "a kingdom of priests, and an holy nation" (Exodus 19:6). This they could not attain without a greater and more excellent atonement than the blood of beasts. For what could be less in accordance with reason than that the sons of Adam, who because of hereditary taint are all born slaves of sin, should be raised to royal dignity and in this way made partakers of the glory of God, if this noble distinction were not derived from some other source? How, moreover, could the priestly office flourish among those whose vices made them abominable in the sight of God, if they were not consecrated in a holy head? Therefore Peter elegantly transposes the words of Moses, teaching that the fullness of grace, of which the Jews had a foretaste under the Law, is displayed in Christ: "Ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9). The transposition of the words indicates that those to whom Christ has appeared in the Gospel have obtained more than their fathers, inasmuch as they are all endowed with priestly and royal honor, and can therefore, trusting in their Mediator, appear with boldness in the presence of God.

2¶

It should be observed, by the way, that the kingdom which was at length set up in the family of David is part of the Law, and is included under the dispensation of Moses. From this it follows that, both in the whole tribe of Levi and in the descendants of David, Christ was set before the eyes of the Israelites as in a double mirror. For, as I observed just now (section 1), in no other way could those who were the slaves of sin and death, and defiled with corruption, be either kings or priests. Hence appears the perfect truth of Paul's statement, "The law was our schoolmaster to bring us unto Christ," "till the seed should come to whom the promise was made" (Galatians 3:24, 19). For since Christ had not yet been made intimately known to the Jews, they were like children whose weakness could not bear a full knowledge of heavenly things. How they were led to Christ by the ceremonial law has already been touched on, and it may be made clearer by several passages in the Prophets. Although they were required to approach God daily with new sacrifices in order to appease him, Isaiah nevertheless promises that all their sins would be expiated by one single sacrifice, and Daniel agrees with this (Isaiah 53:5; Daniel 9:26, 27). The priests appointed from the tribe of Levi entered the sanctuary, but it was once said of a single priest, "The Lord has sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever, after the order of Melchizedek" (Psalm 110:4). The anointing with oil was then visible, but Daniel in a vision declares that there will be another anointing. Not to dwell on this, the author of the Epistle to the Hebrews proves clearly and at length, from the fourth to the eleventh chapter, that the ceremonies were vain and of no value except as bringing us to Christ. With regard to the Ten Commandments, we must in the same way attend to the statement of Paul that "Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to every one that believeth" (Romans 10:4); and again, that ministers of the new testament were "not of the letter, but of the spirit: for the letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life" (2 Corinthians 3:6). The former passage indicates that it is vain to teach righteousness by precept until Christ bestows it by free imputation and by the regeneration of the Spirit. Hence he rightly calls Christ the end or fulfilling of the Law, because it would do us no good to know what God demands if Christ did not come to the aid of those who are laboring and oppressed under an unbearable yoke and burden. In another place he says that the Law "was added because of transgressions" (Galatians 3:19), so that it might humble men under a sense of their condemnation. Moreover, since this is the only true preparation for Christ, the statements, though made in different words, agree perfectly with each other. But because he had to dispute with perverse teachers who claimed that men merited justification by the works of the Law, he was sometimes obliged, in refuting their error, to speak of the Law in a more restricted sense, merely as law, though in other respects the covenant of free adoption is included under it.

3¶

But in order that a sense of guilt may drive us to seek pardon, it is important to know how our being instructed in the Moral Law makes us more inexcusable. If it is true that a perfect righteousness is set before us in the Law, it follows that the complete keeping of it is perfect righteousness in the sight of God; that is, a righteousness by which a man may be considered and pronounced righteous at the divine tribunal. Therefore Moses, after proclaiming the Law, does not hesitate to call heaven and earth to witness that he had set life and death, good and evil, before the people. Nor can it be denied that the reward of eternal salvation, as promised by the Lord, awaits perfect obedience to the Law (Deuteronomy 30:19). Again, however, it is important to understand in what way we perform that obedience for which we rightly cherish the hope of that reward. For what use is it to see that the reward of eternal life depends on keeping the Law, unless it also appears whether it is in our power to attain eternal life in that way? Here, then, the weakness of the Law is shown; for in none of us is that righteousness of the Law to be seen, and therefore, being shut out from the promises of life, we fall again under the curse. I state not only what happens, but what must necessarily happen. Since the doctrine of the Law surpasses our capacity, a man may indeed look from a distance at the promises held out, but he cannot derive any benefit from them. The only thing left for him, therefore, is to form from their excellence a better estimate of his own misery, as he considers that the hope of salvation is cut off and that he is threatened with certain death. On the other hand, those fearful threatenings, which strike not at a few individuals but at every individual without exception, rise up; they rise up, I say, and pursue us with inexorable severity, so that nothing but instant death is presented to us by the Law.

4¶

Therefore, if we look only to the Law, the result must be dejection, confusion, and despair, since by it we are all cursed and condemned, while we are kept far away from the blessedness which it holds out to those who keep it. Is the Lord, then, you will ask, only toying with us? Is it not next to mockery to hold out the hope of happiness, to invite and exhort us to it, to declare that it is set before us, while all the while the entrance to it is barred and completely shut? I answer: although the promises, insofar as they are conditional, depend on a perfect obedience to the Law, which is nowhere to be found, they have not been given in vain. For when we have learned that the promises would be fruitless and of no avail unless God accepted us out of his free goodness, without any regard to our works, and when, having learned this, we embrace by faith the goodness thus offered in the gospel, the promises, with all their attached conditions, are fully accomplished. For God, while bestowing all things on us freely, crowns his goodness by not disdaining our imperfect obedience; he forgives its deficiencies, accepts it as if it were complete, and so bestows on us the full measure of what the Law has promised. But since this point will be discussed more fully in treating of justification by faith, we shall not pursue it further at present.

5¶

What has been said about the impossibility of keeping the Law it will be proper to explain and confirm briefly, since the general opinion is that nothing could be more absurd. Hence Jerome has not hesitated to pronounce an anathema against it. See among the works of Justin, Quæst. 103; and Hieronymus ad Ctesiphont adv. Pelegianos, where he seems both to admit and to deny the same proposition.

Book 2, chapter 12, section 4; and Book 3, chapter 4, section 27; and chapter 11, section 23.

6¶

To make the whole matter clearer, let us take a brief view of the office and use of the Moral Law. This office and use seems to me to consist of three parts. First, by displaying the righteousness of God, that is, the righteousness which alone is acceptable to God, it admonishes everyone of his own unrighteousness, informs him of it, convicts him, and finally condemns him. This is necessary in order that man, who is blind and intoxicated with self-love, may be brought both to know and to confess his weakness and impurity. For until his vanity is made perfectly plain, he is puffed up with a foolish confidence in his own powers, and he can never be brought to feel their feebleness so long as he measures them by a standard of his own choosing. As soon, however, as he begins to compare them with the requirements of the Law, he has something to tame his presumption. However high his opinion of his own powers may be, he immediately feels that they pant under the heavy load, then totter and stumble, and finally fall and give way. He, then, who is schooled by the Law lays aside the arrogance which formerly blinded him. In the same way he must be cured of pride, the other disease from which we have said he suffers. So long as he is allowed to appeal to his own judgment, he substitutes a hypocritical righteousness for a real one, and, content with this, sets up certain artificial observances in opposition to the grace of God. But once he is forced to weigh his conduct in the balance of the Law, he renounces all reliance on this imagined righteousness and sees that he is an infinite distance from holiness, and, on the other hand, that he teems with countless vices from which he formerly seemed free. The recesses in which concupiscence lies hidden are so deep and winding that they easily escape our notice; and hence the Apostle had good reason for saying, "I had not known lust, except the law had said, Thou shalt not covet." For if it is not dragged out of its hiding places, it destroys miserably in secret before its fatal sting is detected.

7¶

Thus the Law is a kind of mirror. As in a mirror we discover any stains on our face, so in the Law we see, first, our impotence; then, as a consequence of it, our iniquity; and finally the curse, as the consequence of both. He who has no power to follow righteousness is necessarily plunged into the mire of iniquity, and this iniquity is immediately followed by the curse. Accordingly, the greater the transgression of which the Law convicts us, the more severe the judgment to which we are exposed. To this effect is the Apostle's declaration that "by the law is the knowledge of sin" (Romans 3:20). By these words he points out only the first office of the Law, as it is experienced by sinners not yet regenerated. In keeping with this, it is said that "the law entered that the offence might abound," and accordingly that it is "the ministration of death," that it "worketh wrath" and kills (Romans 5:20; 2 Corinthians 3:7; Romans 4:15). For there can be no doubt that the clearer the consciousness of guilt, the greater the increase of sin, because then a rebellious spirit against the Lawgiver is added to the transgression. All that remains for the Law is to arm the wrath of God for the destruction of the sinner; for by itself it can do nothing but accuse, condemn, and destroy him. Thus Augustine says, "If the Spirit of grace is absent, the law is present only to convict and slay us." August. de Corrept. et Gratia. Ambros. Lib. 1 de Jac. et cap. 6 de Vita Beat.

8¶

But while the unrighteousness and condemnation of all are attested by the law, it does not follow (if we make proper use of it) that we must at once give up all hope and rush headlong into despair. No doubt it has some such effect on the reprobate, but this is owing to their obstinacy. With the children of God the effect is different. The Apostle testifies that the law pronounces its sentence of condemnation in order "that every mouth may be stopped, and all the world may become guilty before God" (Romans 3:19). In another place, however, the same Apostle declares that "God has concluded them all in unbelief," not that he might destroy all or allow all to perish, but that "he might have mercy upon all" (Romans 11:32). In other words, it is so that, stripping themselves of a foolish opinion of their own virtue, they may see how wholly they depend on the hand of God; that, feeling how naked and destitute they are, they may take refuge in his mercy, rely on it, and cover themselves entirely with it, renouncing all righteousness and merit and clinging to mercy alone, as it is offered in Christ to all who long and look for it in true faith. In the precepts of the law God is seen as the rewarder only of perfect righteousness (a righteousness which all lack), and, on the other hand, as the stern avenger of wickedness. But in Christ his face shines forth full of grace and gentleness toward poor, unworthy sinners.

9¶

There are many passages in Augustine on the usefulness of the law in leading us to implore divine help. Thus he writes to Hilary, August. Ep. 89, Quæst. 2; ad Assell. Ep. 200; ad Innocent. Ep. 95; Lib. de Corrept. et Gratia ad Valent.; in Ps. 70 et 117; Item, Concio. 27.

10¶

The second office of the Law is, by means of its fearful threatenings and the resulting dread of punishment, to restrain those who, unless they are forced, have no regard for what is right and just. Such persons are restrained not because their mind is inwardly moved and affected, but because, as if a bridle were laid on them, they keep their hands from outward acts and inwardly hold in check the depravity which would otherwise break out insolently. It is true that they are not on this account either better or more righteous in the sight of God. For although, restrained by terror or shame, they do not dare to carry out what their mind has conceived, nor give full rein to their raging lust, their heart is by no means trained to fear and obedience. On the contrary, the more they restrain themselves, the more they are inflamed, the more they rage and boil, ready for any act or outbreak whatever, were it not for the terror of the law. And not only so, but they thoroughly detest the law itself and curse the Lawgiver, so that if they could, they would most willingly annihilate him, because they cannot bear either his commanding what is right or his punishing those who despise his Majesty. The feeling of all who are not yet regenerate, though livelier in some and less so in others, is that in keeping the law they are not led by willing submission but dragged by the force of fear. Nevertheless, this forced and extorted righteousness is necessary for the good of society, whose peace is secured by a provision without which all things would be thrown into tumult and confusion. Indeed, this tutoring is not without its use even for the children of God, who before their effectual calling, being without the Spirit of holiness, freely indulge the lusts of the flesh. When they are deterred by the fear of divine vengeance from open outbreaks, then, though they profit little for the present because their mind is not subdued, they are still in some measure trained to bear the yoke of righteousness, so that when they are called they are not like mere novices, studying a discipline of which they previously knew nothing. This office seems to be especially in the Apostle's view when he says, "That the law is not made for a righteous man, but for the lawless and disobedient, for the ungodly and for sinners, for unholy and profane, for murderers of fathers and murderers of mothers, for manslayers, for whoremongers, for them that defile themselves with mankind, for men-stealers, for liars, for perjured persons, and if there be any other thing that is contrary to sound doctrine" (1 Timothy 1:9, 10). He thus indicates that it is a restraint on unruly lusts that would otherwise burst all bounds.

11¶

To both may be applied the Apostle's declaration in another place, that "The law was our schoolmaster to bring us unto Christ" (Galatians 3:24); for there are two classes of persons whom it leads to Christ by its training. Some (of whom we spoke first), from excessive confidence in their own virtue or righteousness, are unfit to receive the grace of Christ until they are completely humbled. The law does this by making them aware of their misery, and so disposing them to long for what they previously imagined they did not need. Others need a bridle to restrain them from giving full scope to their passions and thereby utterly losing all desire for righteousness. For where the Spirit of God does not rule, the lusts sometimes break out so violently as to threaten to drown the soul subject to them in forgetfulness and contempt of God; and so they would, if God did not intervene with this remedy. Those, therefore, whom he has destined to the inheritance of his kingdom, if he does not regenerate them at once, he keeps in fear through the works of the law until the time of his visitation; not, indeed, that pure and chaste fear which his children ought to have, but a fear useful to the extent of instructing them in true piety according to their capacity. Of this we have so many proofs that there is not the least need of an example. For all who have remained for some time in ignorance of God will confess, from their own experience, that the law had the effect of keeping them in some degree in the fear and reverence of God, until, being regenerated by his Spirit, they began to love him from the heart.

12¶

The third use of the Law, which is also the principal use and more closely connected with its proper end, concerns believers, in whose hearts the Spirit of God already flourishes and reigns. For although the Law is written and engraved on their hearts by the finger of God, that is, although they are so moved and stirred by the Spirit that they desire to obey God, there are two ways in which they still profit by the Law. For it is the best instrument for enabling them daily to learn with greater truth and certainty what the will of the Lord is which they aspire to follow, and for confirming them in this knowledge; just as a servant who desires with all his soul to prove himself to his master must still observe and take care to find out his master's dispositions, so that he may conduct himself in keeping with them. Let none of us think ourselves exempt from this necessity, for no one has yet attained such a degree of wisdom that he may not, by the daily instruction of the Law, advance to a purer knowledge of the divine will. Then, because we need not only doctrine but also exhortation, the servant of God will derive this further benefit from the Law: by meditating on it frequently, he will be stirred to obedience and confirmed in it, and so drawn away from the slippery paths of sin. In this way the saints must press onward, since, however great the eagerness with which they hasten toward righteousness under the Spirit, they are held back by the sluggishness of the flesh and make less progress than they ought. The Law acts like a whip to the flesh, urging it on as men do a lazy, sluggish donkey. Even in the case of a spiritual man, inasmuch as he is still burdened with the weight of the flesh, the Law is a constant goad, pricking him forward when he would indulge in sloth. David had this use in view when he pronounced this high praise of the Law: "The law of the Lord is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the Lord is sure, making wise the simple. The statutes of the Lord are right, rejoicing the heart: the commandment of the Lord is pure, enlightening the eyes" (Psalm 19:7, 8). Again, "Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path" (Psalm 119:105). The whole psalm abounds in passages to the same effect. Such passages are not inconsistent with those of Paul, which show not the usefulness of the law to the regenerate but what it is able to bestow by itself. The Psalmist's purpose is to celebrate the benefits which the Lord, by means of his law, bestows on those whom he inwardly inspires with a love of obedience. And he refers not to the bare precepts only, but also to the promise attached to them, which alone makes sweet what in itself is bitter. For what is less attractive than the law when, by its demands and threats, it overawes the soul and fills it with terror? David especially shows that in the law he saw the Mediator, without whom it gives no pleasure or delight.

13¶

Some unskilled persons, failing to attend to this, boldly cast off the whole law of Moses and do away with both its Tables, imagining that it is unchristian to hold to a doctrine which contains the ministration of death. Far from our thoughts be this profane notion. Moses has admirably shown that the Law, which can produce nothing but death in sinners, ought to have a better and more excellent effect on the righteous. When he was about to die, he addressed the people in this way: "Set your hearts unto all the words which I testify among you this day, which ye shall command your children to observe to do, all the words of this law. For it is not a vain thing for you; because it is your life" (Deuteronomy 32:46, 47). If it cannot be denied that the Law contains a perfect pattern of righteousness, then, unless we are to have no proper rule of life at all, it must be impious to cast it off. There are not various rules of life, but one perpetual and unbending rule; and therefore, when David describes the righteous as spending their whole lives in meditating on the Law (Psalm 1:2), we must not confine to a single age an occupation which is most fitting for all ages, even to the end of the world. Nor are we to be deterred from its instructions or to shun them because the holiness which it prescribes is stricter than we are able to render so long as we carry about the prison of the body. It no longer plays toward us the part of a hard taskmaster who will not be satisfied without full payment; rather, in the perfection to which it exhorts us, it points out the goal at which, throughout the whole course of our lives, it is no less our interest than our duty to aim. It is well if we press onward in this way. Our whole life is a race, and after we have finished our course, the Lord will enable us to reach that goal to which at present we can only aspire in desire.

14¶

With regard to believers, the law has the force of exhortation, not to bind their consciences with a curse, but to urge them from time to time to shake off sluggishness and to chastise imperfection. For this reason many, when they wish to express this exemption from the curse, say that with regard to believers the Law (I still mean the Moral Law) is abrogated: not that the things which it enjoins are no longer right to be observed, but only that it is not to believers what it formerly was; in other words, that it does not condemn and destroy them by terrifying and confounding their consciences. It is certainly true that Paul shows in clear terms that there is such an abrogation of the Law. And that the same was preached by our Lord appears from the fact that he would not have refuted the opinion that he was destroying the Law if it had not been prevalent among the Jews. Since such an opinion could not have arisen at random without some pretext, there is reason to suppose that it originated in a false interpretation of his doctrine, in the same way that all errors generally arise from a perversion of the truth. But lest we stumble against the same stone, let us distinguish accurately between what has been abrogated in the Law and what still remains in force. When the Lord declares that he came not to destroy the Law but to fulfill it (Matthew 5:17), and that until heaven and earth pass away not one jot or tittle shall remain unfulfilled, he shows that his coming was not meant to detract in any degree from the keeping of the Law. And rightly so, since the very purpose of his coming was to remedy the transgression of the Law. Therefore the doctrine of the Law has not been infringed by Christ, but remains, so that by teaching, admonishing, rebuking, and correcting, it may fit and prepare us for every good work.

15¶

What Paul says about the abrogation of the Law plainly applies not to the Law itself, but merely to its power of binding the conscience. For the Law not only teaches but also imperiously demands. If obedience is not rendered, indeed, if it falls short in any degree, the Law thunders forth its curse. For this reason the Apostle says that "as many as are of the works of the law are under the curse: for it is written, Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things which are written in the book of the law to do them" (Galatians 3:10; Deuteronomy 27:26). He describes as being under the works of the Law those who do not place righteousness in that forgiveness of sins by which we are freed from the rigor of the Law. He therefore shows that we must be freed from the fetters of the Law if we are not to perish miserably under them. But what fetters? Those of rigid and severe exaction, which remits not one iota of the demand and leaves no transgression unpunished. To redeem us from this curse, Christ was made a curse for us; for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree (Deuteronomy 21:23, compared with Galatians 3:13; 4:4). In the following chapter, indeed, he says that "Christ was made under the law, in order that he might redeem those who are under the law"; but the meaning is the same. For he immediately adds, "That we might receive the adoption of sons." What does this mean? That we might not be subject to bondage all our lifetime, with our consciences oppressed by the fear of death. Meanwhile, it must always remain an indubitable truth that the Law has lost none of its authority, but must always receive from us the same respect and obedience.

16¶

The case of the ceremonies is different, since these have been abrogated not in their effect but only in their use. Though Christ by his coming put an end to their use, this is so far from detracting from their sacredness that it rather commends and illustrates it. For just as these ceremonies would have given God's ancient people nothing but an empty show if the power of Christ's death and resurrection had not been prefigured by them, so, if their use had not ceased, it would be impossible today to understand for what purpose they were instituted. Accordingly Paul, in order to prove that observing them was not only superfluous but also harmful, says that they "are a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Christ" (Colossians 2:17). We see, therefore, that the truth is made clearer by their abolition than if Christ, who has been openly revealed, were still represented by them as at a distance and as under a veil. By the death of Christ the veil of the temple was not torn in vain, since the living and express image of heavenly things, which had begun to be dimly foreshadowed, is now brought fully into view, as the author of the Epistle to the Hebrews describes (Hebrews 10:1). To the same effect our Savior declares that "the law and the prophets were until John: since that time the kingdom of God is preached, and every man presseth into it" (Luke 16:16); not that the holy fathers were left without the preaching of the hope of salvation and eternal life, but because they saw only at a distance and under a shadow what we now behold in full light. Why it was necessary for the Church to rise higher than these elements is explained by John the Baptist when he says, "The law was given by Moses, but grace and truth came by Jesus Christ" (John 1:17). For though it is true that expiation was promised in the ancient sacrifices, and the ark of the covenant was a sure pledge of the fatherly favor of God, the whole would have been illusory had it not been founded on the grace of Christ, in which true and eternal stability is found. It must be held as a fixed point that, though the legal rites have ceased to be observed, their end serves to show more clearly how great their usefulness was before the coming of Christ, who, while he abolished their use, sealed their force and effect by his death.

17¶

There is a little more difficulty in the following passage of Paul: "You, being dead in your sins and the uncircumcision of your flesh, has he quickened together with him, having forgiven you all trespasses; blotting out the handwriting of ordinances that was against us, which was contrary to us, and took it out of the way, nailing it to his cross," etc. (Colossians 2:13, 14). He seems to extend the abolition of the Law considerably further, as if we had nothing to do with its commands. Some err in interpreting this simply of the Moral Law, as implying the abolition not of its commands but of its inexorable rigor. Others, examining Paul's words more carefully, see that they properly apply to the Ceremonial Law, and show that Paul repeatedly uses the term ordinance in this sense. Thus he writes to the Ephesians: "He is our peace, who has made both one, and has broken down the middle wall of partition between us; having abolished in his flesh the enmity, even the law of commandments contained in ordinances; for to make in himself of twain one new man" (Ephesians 2:14). There can be no doubt that he is treating there of ceremonies, since he speaks of "the middle wall of partition" which separated Jews and Gentiles. I therefore hold that the former view is mistaken; but at the same time it does not seem to me that the latter fully reaches the Apostle's meaning. For I cannot admit that the two passages are perfectly parallel. Since his purpose was to assure the Ephesians that they were admitted to fellowship with the Jews, he tells them that the obstacle which formerly stood in the way had been removed. This obstacle lay in the ceremonies. For the rites of washing and sacrifice by which the Jews were consecrated to the Lord separated them from the Gentiles. But who does not see that in the Epistle to the Colossians a loftier mystery is referred to? No doubt a question is raised there about the Mosaic observances, to which false apostles were trying to bind the Christian people. But just as in the Epistle to the Galatians he takes a higher view of this controversy and in a way traces it to its source, so he does in this passage also. For if the only thing considered in rites is the necessity of observing them, what was the use of calling them a handwriting which was contrary to us? Besides, how could the blotting out of it be set down as almost the whole sum of redemption? Therefore the very nature of the case plainly shows that something more inward is referred to here. I cannot doubt that I have arrived at the genuine interpretation, provided I am allowed to assume what Augustine has somewhere most truly affirmed, indeed derived from the very words of the Apostle: namely, that in the Jewish ceremonies there was more a confession of sins than an expiation of them. For what more was done in sacrifice by those who put purifications in their own place than to confess that they knew they deserved death? What did these purifications testify except that they themselves were impure? By these means, therefore, the handwriting of both their guilt and their impurity was renewed again and again. But the attestation of these things was not the removal of them. Therefore the Apostle says that Christ is "the mediator of the new testament, by means of death, for the redemption of the transgressions that were under the first testament" (Hebrews 9:15). Rightly, therefore, does the Apostle describe these handwritings as against the worshipers and contrary to them, since by means of them their impurity and condemnation were openly sealed. There is nothing contrary to this in the fact that they were partakers of the same grace as ourselves. This they obtained through Christ, and not through the ceremonies, which the Apostle there contrasts with Christ, showing that by their continued use the glory of Christ was obscured. We see how ceremonies, considered in themselves, are elegantly and fittingly called handwritings, and contrary to the salvation of man, inasmuch as they were a kind of formal instrument attesting his liability. On the other hand, when false apostles wished to bind them on the Christian Church, Paul, entering more deeply into their meaning, with good reason warned the Colossians how seriously they would fall back if they allowed a yoke to be imposed on them in that way. By doing so they would at the same time deprive themselves of all benefit from Christ, who by his eternal sacrifice offered once for all had abolished those daily sacrifices, which were indeed powerful to attest sin but could do nothing to destroy it.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin teaches that the law was never given to hold a people to itself but to keep the hope of salvation alive in Christ until his coming — a schoolmaster that exposes our wretchedness, drives us from despair to mercy, and bends the whole weight of Moses toward the Redeemer. For a household in 2026, this guards against two opposite ditches: the legalism that turns family worship into a ledger of performance, and the lawlessness that waves away the commandments as though grace had abolished them. When you teach your children the Ten Words at the table, teach them as Calvin did — a mirror that shows their guilt so that they flee, not to their own resolve, but to the One the law was always pointing toward. The same threefold use still stands: it restrains the world that surrounds us, it convicts the conscience that grows comfortable, and it instructs the believer already redeemed. Hold the law high, and hold it always toward Christ — never as a cage, always as a road that ends at the cross.
