Christ Clothed With the True Substance of Human Nature

The heads of this chapter are, I. The orthodoxy doctrine as to the true humanity of our Saviour, proved from many passages of Scripture, sec. 1. II. Refutation of the impious objections of the Marcionites, Manichees, and similar heretics, sec. 2–4.

1¶

Concerning the divinity of Christ, which has been established elsewhere by clear and solid proofs, I take it that it would be superfluous to treat it again. It remains, therefore, to see how, clothed with our flesh, he fulfilled the office of Mediator. In ancient times the reality of his human nature was attacked by the Manichees and the Marcionites, the latter imagining a phantom in place of the body of Christ, and the former dreaming that he had been clothed with heavenly flesh. The passages of Scripture that contradict both are many and strong. The blessing is not promised in a heavenly seed, or in the mask of a man, but in the seed of Abraham and Jacob; nor is the everlasting throne promised to an airy man, but to the Son of David and the fruit of his loins. Hence, when manifested in the flesh, he is called the Son of David and of Abraham, not because he was born of a virgin and yet created in the air, but because, as Paul explains, he was "made of the seed of David, according to the flesh" (Romans 1:3), just as the same apostle says elsewhere that he came from the Jews (Romans 9:5). Therefore our Lord himself, not content with the name of man, often calls himself the Son of man, wishing to express more clearly that he was a man by true human descent. Since the Holy Spirit has declared, so often, by so many instruments, with so much care and plainness, a matter that is not obscure in itself, who could have thought that mortals would have the effrontery to darken it with their glosses? Many other passages are at hand, if one wished to bring forward more: for instance, that saying of Paul, that "God sent forth his Son, made of a woman" (Galatians 4:4), and countless others, which show that he was subject to hunger, thirst, cold, and the other weaknesses of our nature. But from the many we must chiefly select those that may help to build up our minds in true faith, as when it is said, "Verily, he took not on him the nature of angels, but he took on him the seed of Abraham," "that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death" (Hebrews 2:16, 14). Again, "Both he that sanctifieth and they who are sanctified are all of one: for which cause he is not ashamed to call them brethren." "Wherefore in all things it behoved him to be made like unto his brethren, that he might be a merciful and faithful high priest" (Hebrews 2:11, 17). Again, "We have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities" (Hebrews 4:15), and the like. To the same effect is the passage we referred to a little earlier, in which Paul expressly declares that the sins of the world had to be expiated in our flesh (Romans 8:3). And certainly everything the Father conferred on Christ belongs to us for this reason, that "he is the head," and that from him the whole body is "fitly joined together, and compacted by that which every joint supplieth" (Ephesians 4:16). Indeed, in no other way could it hold true, as it is said, that the Spirit was given to him without measure (John 1:16), and that out of his fullness we have all received; since nothing could be more absurd than that God, in his own essence, should be enriched by a gift added from outside. For this reason also Christ himself says elsewhere, "For their sakes I sanctify myself" (John 17:19).

2¶

The passages they bring forward to confirm their error are absurdly twisted, and they gain nothing by their frivolous subtleties when they try to do away with what I have just set against them. Marcion imagines that Christ took on a phantom instead of a body, because it is said elsewhere that he was made in the likeness of man and found in fashion as a man. In this he entirely overlooks what Paul is discussing in that place (Philippians 2:7). His aim is not to show what kind of body Christ took on, but that, when he might rightly have asserted his divinity, he was pleased to display nothing but the marks of a lowly and despised man. For, in order to urge us to submission by his example, he shows that, although as God he might have displayed to the world the brightness of his glory, he gave up his right and voluntarily emptied himself; that he took the form of a servant and, content with that humble condition, allowed his divinity to be hidden under a veil of flesh. Here, beyond question, he is explaining not what Christ was, but how he acted. Indeed, from the whole context it is easily gathered that it was in the true nature of man that Christ humbled himself. For what is meant by the words, he was "found in fashion as a man," except that for a time, instead of shining with divine glory, only the human form appeared, in a lowly and abject condition? Nor would the words of Peter, that he was "put to death in the flesh, but quickened by the Spirit" (1 Peter 3:18), hold true unless the Son of God had become weak in the nature of man. Paul explains this more clearly when he declares that "he was crucified through weakness" (2 Corinthians 13:4). And from this comes his exaltation; for it is expressly said that Christ obtained new glory after he humbled himself. This could fittingly apply only to a man endowed with a body and a soul. Manes dreams of an airy body because Christ is called the second Adam, the Lord from heaven. But the apostle is not speaking there of the essence of his body as heavenly, but of the spiritual life which, flowing from Christ, gives us life (1 Corinthians 15:47). This life Paul and Peter, as we have seen, distinguish from his flesh. Indeed, that passage admirably confirms the orthodox doctrine of the human nature of Christ. If his body were not of the same nature as ours, there would be no soundness in the argument Paul presses with such earnestness: if Christ is risen, we shall rise also; if we do not rise, neither has Christ risen. Whatever the quibbles by which the ancient Manichees or their modern disciples try to evade this, they cannot succeed. It is a frivolous and contemptible evasion to say that Christ is called the Son of man because he was promised to men, since it is obvious that in the Hebrew idiom the Son of man means a true man; and Christ no doubt kept the idiom of his own tongue. The last clause of the sentence is omitted in the French.

3¶

The passages in which Christ is called the seed of Abraham and the fruit of the loins of David these people, with no less folly than wickedness, wrap up in allegory. Had the term seed been used allegorically, Paul would surely not have failed to point it out when he affirms clearly, and without a figure, that the promise was not given "to seeds, as of many; but as of one, And to thy seed, which is Christ" (Galatians 3:16). With similar absurdity they pretend that he was called the Son of David for no other reason than that he had been promised, and was at last manifested in due time. For Paul, after calling him the Son of David, immediately adds "according to the flesh," and so certainly designates his nature. So also (Romans 9:5), while declaring him to be "God blessed for ever," he mentions separately that "as concerning the flesh, he was descended from the Jews." Again, if he had not truly been begotten of the seed of David, what is the meaning of the expression that he is the "fruit of his loins," or what is the meaning of the promise, "Of the fruit of thy body will I set upon thy throne" (Psalm 132:11)? Moreover, their way of handling the genealogy of Christ as given by Matthew is mere sophistry. For although Matthew lists the ancestors not of Mary but of Joseph, yet since he was speaking of a matter generally understood at the time, he considers it enough to show that Joseph was descended from the seed of David, since it is certain that Mary was of the same family. Luke goes still further, showing that the salvation brought by Christ is common to the whole human race, since Christ, the author of salvation, is descended from Adam, the common father of us all. I admit, indeed, that the genealogy proves Christ to be the Son of David only as descended from the Virgin. But the new Marcionites, to put a gloss on their heresy, namely, to prove that the body Christ took on was without substance, too confidently maintain that the term seed applies only to males, thus overturning the elementary principles of nature. But since this discussion does not belong to theology, and the arguments they bring forward are too empty to require any labored refutation, I will not touch on matters that belong to philosophy and the art of medicine. It will be enough to dispose of the objection drawn from the statement of Scripture that Aaron and Jehoiadah married wives from the tribe of Judah, and that in this way the distinction of tribes would have been confused if proper descent could come through the female. It is well known that, with regard to civil order, descent is reckoned through the male; and yet his superiority does not prevent the female from having her proper share in the descent. This solution applies to all the genealogies. When Scripture gives a list of individuals, it often mentions males only. Must we therefore say that females count for nothing? Why, even children know that they are classed with men. For this reason wives are said to give children to their husbands, the name of the family always remaining with the males. Then, just as the male sex has this privilege, that sons are considered of noble or common birth according to the condition of their fathers, so on the other hand, in slavery, the condition of the child is determined by that of the mother; as lawyers say, partus sequitur ventrem. From this we may infer that offspring is partly procreated from the seed of the mother. By the common custom of nations, mothers are considered progenitors, and the divine law agrees with this, for it could have had no ground for forbidding the marriage of an uncle with his niece if there were no blood relationship between them. It would also be lawful for a brother and sister born of the same mother to marry each other when their fathers are different. But while I grant that the power assigned to the woman is passive, I hold that the same thing is affirmed of her and of the male without distinction. Christ is not said to have been made by a woman, but of a woman (Galatians 4:4). But some of this herd, laying aside all shame, publicly ask whether we mean to maintain that Christ was procreated from the proper seed of a Virgin. Latin, "An dicere velimus ex semine menstruali virginis procreatur esse Christum."

4¶

The absurdities they wish to fasten on us are mere childish slanders. They consider it base and dishonoring to Christ to have derived his descent from men, because in that case he could not be exempted from the common law which includes the whole offspring of Adam, without exception, under sin. But this difficulty is easily resolved by Paul's contrast: "As by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin," "even so by the righteousness of one the free gift came upon all men unto justification of life" (Romans 5:12, 18). Corresponding to this is another passage: "The first man is of the earth, earthy: the second man is the Lord from heaven" (1 Corinthians 15:47). Accordingly, the same apostle, teaching in another passage that Christ was sent "in the likeness of sinful flesh, that the righteousness of the law might be fulfilled in us," expressly sets him apart from the common lot, as being true man and yet without fault and corruption (Romans 8:3). It is childish trifling to maintain that, if Christ is free from all taint and was begotten of the seed of Mary by the secret working of the Spirit, it follows that it is not the seed of the woman that is impure, but only that of the man. We do not hold Christ to be free from all taint merely because he was born of a woman without union with a man, but because he was sanctified by the Spirit, so that his generation was pure and spotless, such as it would have been before Adam's fall. Let us always keep in mind that wherever Scripture speaks of the purity of Christ, it refers to his true human nature, since it would be superfluous to say that God is pure. Moreover, the sanctification John speaks of in his seventeenth chapter cannot apply to the divine nature. This does not suggest the idea of a twofold seed in Adam, even though no contamination extended to Christ, since the generation of man is not in itself sinful or impure, but became so as an accidental consequence of the fall. Hence it is not strange that Christ, by whom our integrity was to be restored, was exempted from the common corruption. Another absurdity they thrust on us, namely, that if the Word of God became incarnate, it must have been enclosed in the narrow dwelling of an earthly body, is sheer insolence. For although the boundless essence of the Word was united with human nature into one person, we have no notion of any enclosing. The Son of God came down from heaven in a miraculous way, yet without leaving heaven; he was pleased to be conceived miraculously in the Virgin's womb, to live on the earth, and to hang upon the cross, and yet he always filled the world as from the beginning.

Calvin's burden here is plain and unflinching: the Son did not merely seem human, nor wear some borrowed celestial flesh, but truly took the seed of Abraham and David — hungry, weary, tempted, bone of our bone — that in our own nature he might destroy death and call us brethren. A faithless age recycles the old phantom in newer dress, wanting a Jesus who inspires but never quite descends, a symbol rather than a Savior who sweat and bled. Hold the harder, plainer truth: he was made like us in all things, sin excepted, so that nothing in your weakness lies outside his sympathy. Tell your children of a Christ who knew cold and hunger and a borrowed grave, not a myth that floats above the table. When you sit in the pew worn thin by trial, remember the high priest who can be touched with the feeling of your infirmities — he is not ashamed to call you brother, and that kinship is your salvation.
