How Two Natures Constitute the Person of the Mediator

This chapter contains two principal heads: I. A brief exposition of the doctrine of Christ’s two natures in one person, sec. 1–4. II. A refutation of the heresies of Servetus, which destroy the distinction of natures in Christ, and the eternity of the divine nature of the Son.

Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. Proof of two natures in Christ—a human and a divine. Illustrated by analogy, from the union of body and soul. Illustration applied.
  2. 2. Proof from passages of Scripture which distinguish between the two natures. Proof from the communication of properties.
  3. 3. Proof from passages showing the union of both natures. A rule to be observed in this discussion.
  4. 4. Utility and use of the doctrine concerning the two natures. The Nestorians. The Eutychians. Both justly condemned by the Church.
  5. 5. The heresies of Servetus refuted. General answer or sum of the orthodox doctrine concerning Christ. What meant by the hypostatic union. Objections of Servetus to the deity of Christ. Answer.
  6. 6. Another objection and answer. A twofold filiation of Christ.
  7. 7. Other objections answered.
  8. 8. Conclusion of the former objections. Other pestilential heresies of Servetus.
1¶

When it is said that the Word was made flesh, we must not understand it as if he were either changed into flesh or confusedly mixed with flesh, but that he chose the Virgin's womb as a temple in which he might dwell. He who was the Son of God became the Son of man, not by confusion of substance, but by unity of person. For we maintain that the divinity was so joined and united with the humanity that the full properties of each nature remain whole, and yet the two natures make up only one Christ. If in human affairs anything analogous to this great mystery can be found, the most fitting likeness Augustine employs the same likeness, Epistle 52.

2¶

Little reliance could be placed on these statements were it not proved by many passages throughout the sacred volume that none of them is of man's devising. What Christ said of himself, "Before Abraham was I am" (John 8:58), was far removed from his humanity. I am not unaware of the quibble by which erring spirits distort this passage, namely, that he was before all ages insofar as he was foreknown as the Redeemer, both in the counsel of the Father and in the minds of believers. But since he plainly distinguishes the time of his manifestation from his eternal existence, and expressly builds on his ancient rule to prove that he comes before Abraham, he undoubtedly claims for himself the distinctive attributes of divinity. Paul's assertion that he is "the first-born of every creature," that "he is before all things, and by him all things consist" (Colossians 1:15, 17), and his own declaration that he had glory with the Father before the world was, and that he works together with the Father, are equally inapplicable to man. These and similar properties must be assigned specifically to his divinity. Again, his being called the servant of the Father, his being said to grow in stature and wisdom and favor with God and man, not to seek his own glory, not to know the last day, not to speak of himself, not to do his own will, his being seen and handled: Isaiah 41:1, etc.; John 5:17; Luke 2:52; John 8:50; Mark 13:32; John 14:10; 6:38; Luke 24:39.

3¶

But above all, the true substance of Christ is most clearly declared in those passages that take in both natures at once. Many of these are found in the Gospel of John. What we read there of his having received power from the Father to forgive sins; of his giving life to whom he will; of his bestowing righteousness, holiness, and salvation; of his being appointed judge of both the living and the dead; of his being honored even as the Father is: John 1:29; 5:21—23; 9:5; 10:9—11; 15:1.

See Calvin, Letter to the Poles against Stancarus.

4¶

This observation, if readers apply it properly, will be of no small use in resolving a great many difficulties. For it is strange how the ignorant, and even some who are not altogether without learning, are perplexed by those modes of expression they see applied to Christ which are not properly suited either to his divinity or to his humanity, because they do not consider how they agree with the character in which he was manifested as God and man, and with his office of Mediator. It is very easy to see how beautifully they agree with one another, provided they have a sober interpreter, one who examines these great mysteries with fitting reverence. But there is nothing that furious and frantic spirits cannot throw into confusion. See Augustine, Enchiridion to Laurentius, chapter 36.

5¶

But in our own age, too, there has arisen a no less deadly monster, Michael Servetus, who in place of the Son of God has substituted a fiction made up of the essence of God, spirit, flesh, and three uncreated elements. First, indeed, he denies that Christ is the Son of God for any other reason than that he was begotten in the womb of the Virgin by the Holy Spirit. The aim of this crafty device is to make out, by destroying the distinction of the two natures, that Christ is something composed of God and man, and yet is not to be regarded as God and man. His aim throughout is to establish that before Christ was manifested in the flesh there were only shadowy figures in God, whose truth or effect first came into existence when the Word, who had been destined for that honor, truly began to be the Son of God. We indeed acknowledge that the Mediator who was born of the Virgin is properly the Son of God. And how could the man Christ be a mirror of the inestimable grace of God had not the dignity been conferred on him both of being, and of being called, the only-begotten Son of God? Meanwhile, however, the definition of the Church stands unshaken, that he is accounted the Son of God because the Word begotten by the Father before all ages took on human nature by hypostatic union. This is a term used by ancient writers to denote the union that makes one person out of two natures, and it was devised to refute the dream of Nestorius, who claimed that the Son of God dwelt in the flesh in such a way as not to be man at the same time. Servetus slanderously charges us with making the Son of God double when we say that the eternal Word was already the Son of God before he was clothed with flesh; as if we said anything more than that he was manifested in the flesh. Although he was God before he became man, he did not therefore begin to be a new God. Nor is there any greater absurdity in holding that the Son of God, who by eternal generation always had the property of being a Son, appeared in the flesh. This is indicated by the angel's word to Mary: "That holy thing which shall be born of thee shall be called the Son of God" (Luke 1:35); as if he had said that the name of Son, which was more obscure under the law, would become renowned and universally known. Corresponding to this is the passage of Paul, that being now the sons of God through Christ, we "have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father" (Romans 8:15). Were not the holy patriarchs of old also counted among the sons of God? Indeed, trusting in this privilege, they called upon God as their Father. But because, ever since the only-begotten Son of God came forth into the world, his heavenly fatherhood has been more clearly manifested, Paul assigns this to the kingdom of Christ as its distinguishing mark. We must, however, firmly hold that God was never a Father to angels and men except with respect to his only-begotten Son; and that men especially, who by their iniquity had been made hateful to God, are sons by free adoption because he is a Son by nature. Nor is there anything to Servetus's assertion that this depends on the sonship God had decreed within himself. Here we are not dealing with figures, as expiation by the blood of beasts was shown to be; but since men could not be the sons of God in reality unless their adoption was grounded in the head, it is against all reason to deprive the head of what is common to the members. I go further: since Scripture gives the name of sons of God to the angels, whose great dignity in this respect did not depend on the future redemption, Christ must take precedence of them in order, so that he may reconcile the Father to them. I will briefly repeat the same point again, and add something concerning the human race. Since angels as well as men were created at first on the condition that God should be the common Father of both, if it is true, as Paul says, that Christ was always the head, "the first-born of every creature—that in all things he might have the pre-eminence" (Colossians 1:15, 18), I think I may rightly infer that he existed as the Son of God before the creation of the world.

6¶

But if his sonship (if I may put it that way) had its beginning at the time he was manifested in the flesh, it follows that he was a Son with respect to his human nature also. Servetus, and others equally frenzied, hold that Christ who appeared in the flesh is the Son of God because, apart from his incarnation, he could not have possessed this name. Let them now answer me: is he a Son according to both natures, and with respect to both? That is indeed what they babble; but Paul's doctrine is very different. We acknowledge, indeed, that Christ in his human nature is called a Son, not merely by free adoption, as believers are, but as the true, natural, and therefore only Son, this being the mark that distinguishes him from all others. Those of us who are regenerated to a new life God honors with the name of sons; the name of true and only-begotten Son he bestows on Christ alone. But how is he an only Son among so great a multitude of brothers, except that he possesses by nature what we acquire as a gift? This honor we extend to his whole character as Mediator, so that he who was born of a Virgin, and on the cross offered himself in sacrifice to the Father, is truly and properly the Son of God; but still with respect to his Godhead. So Paul teaches when he says that he was "separated unto the gospel of God (which he had promised afore by his prophets in the Holy Scriptures), concerning his Son Jesus Christ our Lord, which was made of the seed of David according to the flesh; and declared to be the Son of God with power" (Romans 1:1–4). When he expressly calls him the Son of David according to the flesh, why should he also say that he was "declared to be the Son of God," unless he meant to indicate that this depended on something other than his incarnation? For in the same sense in which he says elsewhere that "though he was crucified through weakness, yet he liveth by the power of God" (2 Corinthians 13:4), he here draws a distinction between the two natures. They must certainly admit that, just as he is called the Son of David on account of his mother, so he is the Son of God on account of his Father, and that in a respect that differs from his human nature. Scripture gives him both names, calling him at one time the Son of God and at another the Son of Man. As to the latter, there can be no question that he is called a Son in keeping with the idiom of the Hebrew language, because he is of the offspring of Adam. On the other hand, I maintain that he is called a Son on account of his Godhead and eternal essence, because it is no less fitting to refer his being called the Son of God to his divine nature than to refer his being called the Son of Man to his human nature. In short, in the passage I have quoted, Paul does not mean that he who according to the flesh was begotten of the seed of David was declared to be the Son of God in any other sense than that in which he teaches elsewhere that Christ, who came from the Jews according to the flesh, is "over all, God blessed for ever" (Romans 9:5). But if in both passages the distinction of two natures is pointed out, how can it be denied that he who according to the flesh is the Son of Man is also, with respect to his divine nature, the Son of God?

7¶

They do indeed find a blustering defense of their heresy in the statement that "God spared not his own Son," and in the message of the angel that he who was to be born of the Virgin should be called the "Son of the Highest" (Romans 8:32; Luke 1:32). But before priding themselves on this empty objection, let them consider with us for a moment what weight there is in their argument. If it is a valid conclusion that he began to be the Son of God at his conception, because he who was conceived is called a Son, it will follow that he began to be the Word after his manifestation in the flesh, because John declares that the Word of life of which he spoke was that which "our hands have handled" (1 John 1:1). In the same way we read in the prophet, "Thou, Bethlehem Ephratah, though thou be little among the thousands of Israel, yet out of thee shall he come forth that is to be a ruler in Israel; whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting" (Micah 5:2). How will they be forced to interpret this, if they follow such a method of arguing? I have declared that we by no means agree with Nestorius, who imagined a twofold Christ, when we maintain that Christ, by means of a brotherly union, made us sons of God with himself, because in the flesh he took from us he is the only-begotten Son of God. 8¶

But although Servetus heaped together a number of horrid dogmas to which others perhaps would not subscribe, you will find that all who refuse to acknowledge the Son of God except in the flesh are obliged, when pressed more closely, to admit that he was a Son for no other reason than that he was conceived in the womb of the Virgin by the Holy Spirit. This is just like the absurdity of the ancient Manichees, who held that the soul of man was derived from God by transfusion, because it is said that he breathed into Adam's nostrils the breath of life (Genesis 2:7). For they lay such stress on the name of Son that they leave no distinction between the natures, but babble that the man Christ is the Son of God because, according to his human nature, he was begotten of God. In this way the eternal generation of Wisdom, celebrated by Solomon (Proverbs 8:22, and following), is destroyed, and no kind of Godhead exists in the Mediator; or else a phantom is substituted in place of a man. It would be useful to refute the grosser delusions of Servetus, by which he deceived himself and some others, so that godly readers might be warned by the example to keep within the bounds of sobriety and modesty. However, I consider it superfluous here, since I have already done so in a special treatise. See Calvin, Defensio Orthodoxae Fidei Sacrae Trinitatis adversus Prodigiosos Errores Michaelis Serveti Hispani.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Two whole natures, one undivided Person — true God and true man, neither confused nor torn apart, each remaining entire in the one Christ. This is no scholastic puzzle to be filed away; it is the very floor your faith stands upon, for only one who was God could bear the weight of divine wrath, and only one who was man could bear it in our place. Servetus had his day, and ours has no shortage of heirs who would shave Christ down to a great teacher or dissolve his deity into a vapor of inspiration. Against every such erosion, confess the whole Christ. Teach your household to say with conviction that the babe of Bethlehem is the eternal Word by whom all things consist, that the man crucified is the only-begotten Son who was before Abraham. A church that holds the head holds everything; a church that lets it slip keeps only a name. Guard this doctrine as you would guard the hope of your own soul, for it is that hope.
