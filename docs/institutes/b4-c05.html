Book 4 · Chapter 5 · USMC modern English

The Ancient Form of Government Utterly Corrupted by the Tyranny of the Papacy

This chapter consists of two parts,—I. Who are called to the ministry under the Papacy, their character, and the ground of their appointment, sec. l-7. II. How far they fulfil their office, sec. 8-19. 1. Who and what kind of persons are uniformly appointed bishops in the Papacy. 1. No inquiry into doctrine. 2. In regard to character, the unlearned and dissolute, boys, or men of wicked lives, chosen. 2. The right of the people taken away, though maintained by Leo, Cyprian, and Councils. It follows that there is no Canonical election in the Papacy. Two objections answered. Papal elections, what. Kind of persons elected. 3. A fuller explanation of the answer to the second objection, unfolding the errors of people, bishops, and princes. 4. No election of presbyters and deacons in the Papacy. 1. Because they are ordained for a different end. 2. Contrary to the command of Scripture and the Council of Chalcedon, no station is assigned them. 3. Both the name and thing adulterated by a thousand frauds. 5. Refutation of those corruptions. Proper end of ordination. Of trial, and other necessary things. For these, wicked and sanguinary men have substituted vain show and deplorable blindness. 6. Second corruption relating to the assignation of benefices which they call collation. Manifold abuses here exposed. Why the offices of priests are in the Papacy called benefices. 7. One individual appointed over five or six churches. This most shameful corruption severely condemned by many Councils. 8. Second part of the chapter—viz. how the office is discharged. Monks who have no place among Presbyters. Objection answered. 9. Presbyters divided into beneficiaries and mercenaries. The beneficiaries are bishops, parsons, canons, chaplains, abbots, priors. The mercenaries condemned by the word of God. 10. The name of beneficiaries given to idle priests who perform no office in the church. Objection answered. What kind of persons the canons should be. Another objection answered. The beneficiaries not true presbyters. 11. The bishops and rectors of parishes, by deserting their churches, glory only in an empty name. 12. The seeds of this evil in the age of Gregory, who inveighs against mercenaries. More sharply rebuked by Bernard. 13. The supreme Popish administration described. Ridiculous allegation of those so-called ministers of the Church. Answer. 14. Their shameful morals. Scarcely one who would not have been excommunicated or deposed by the ancient canons. 15. No true diaconate existing in the Papacy, though they have still the shadow of it. Corruption of the practice of the primitive Church in regard to deacons. 16. Ecclesiastical property, which was formerly administered by true deacons, plundered by bishops and canons, in defraud of the poor. 17. Blasphemous defence of these robbers. Answer. Kings doing homage to Christ. Theodosius. A saying of Ambrose. 18. Another defence with regard to the adorning of churches. Answer. 19. Concluding answer, showing that the diaconate is completely subverted by the Papacy.

1¶

It may now be proper to set before the reader's eyes the order of church government observed by the Roman See and all its satellites, and that whole hierarchy which is forever in their mouths, and to compare it with the description we have given of the primitive and early Church, so that the contrast may make plain what kind of church those men have who pride themselves on the mere title, as if it were enough to outweigh, or rather to overwhelm, us. It will be best to begin with the call, so that we may see who are called to the ministry, of what character they are, and on what grounds. After that we will consider how faithfully they fulfill their office. We shall give the first place to the bishops; would that they could claim the honor of holding the first rank in this discussion! But the subject does not allow me even to touch it lightly without exposing their disgrace. Still, let me remember what kind of writing I am engaged in, and not let my discourse, which ought to be framed for simple teaching, wander beyond its proper limits. But let any of them who have not laid aside all modesty tell me what kind of bishops are commonly elected in our day. Any examination of doctrine is too old-fashioned; or if any regard is had to doctrine, they choose some lawyer who knows better how to plead in the courts than to preach in the church. This much is certain: for a hundred years scarcely one in a hundred has been elected who had any acquaintance with sacred doctrine. I do not spare earlier ages because they were much better, but because the question now concerns only the present Church. If we inquire into morals, we shall find few or almost none whom the ancient canons would not have judged unworthy. If a man was not a drunkard, he was a fornicator; if he was free from this vice, he was a gambler or a sportsman, or a loose liver in some respect. For there are lighter faults which, according to the ancient canons, exclude a man from the episcopal office. But the most absurd thing of all is that even boys scarcely ten years old are made bishops by permission of the Pope. Such is the effrontery and stupidity to which they have come, that they have no dread even of that last and monstrous iniquity, which is utterly abhorrent even to natural feeling. From this it is clear what kind of elections these must have been, where such careless negligence prevailed.

2¶

Then, in election, the whole right has been taken from the people. Votes, assents, subscriptions, and everything of that sort have disappeared; the whole power has been given to the canons alone. First they confer the episcopal office on whomever they please; then they bring him out before the people, but to be adored, not examined. Yet Leo protests that no reason permits this, and declares it to be a violent imposition (Leo, Epistle 90, chapter 2). Cyprian, after declaring that it is of divine authority that election should not take place without the consent of the people, shows that a different procedure is at odds with the word of God. Numerous decrees of councils most strictly forbid it to be done otherwise, and if it is so done, order it to be void. If this is true, there is not in the whole Papacy at the present day any canonical election in accordance with either divine or ecclesiastical law. Now, even if there were no other evil in this, what excuse can they give for having robbed the Church of her right? But the corruption of the times required it (they say): since hatred and party spirit prevailed over right and sound judgment among the people and magistrates in the election of bishops, the decision had to be confined to a few. Grant that this was the last remedy in desperate circumstances. When the cure was seen to be more harmful than the disease, why was no remedy provided for this new evil? But it is said that the course the canons must follow is strictly prescribed. Yet can we doubt that even in former times the people, when they met to elect a bishop, knew that they were bound by the most sacred laws, since they saw a rule prescribed by the word of God? That one sentence in which God describes the true character of a bishop ought rightly to carry more weight than ten thousand canons. Nevertheless, carried away by the worst of passions, they had no regard for law or equity. So in our day, though most excellent laws have been made, they remain buried in writing. Meanwhile the general and approved practice (and it is carried on, as it were, by system) is that drunkards, fornicators, and gamblers are everywhere promoted to this honor. Indeed, that is a small thing: bishoprics are the rewards of adulterers and panders; for when they are given to hunters and falconers, things may be considered at their best. To excuse such unworthy proceedings in any way would be wickedness beyond measure. The people had a most excellent canon prescribed to them by the word of God, namely, that a bishop must be blameless, apt to teach, not a brawler, and so on (1 Timothy 3:2). Why, then, was the task of electing transferred from the people to these men? Simply because amid the tumults and factions of the people the word of God was not heard. And, on the other hand, why is it not transferred away from these men today, who not only violate all laws, but, having cast off shame, mix and confound things human and divine through lust, greed, and ambition?

3¶

But it is not true to say that the thing was devised as a remedy. We read that in former times tumults often arose in cities at the election of bishops; yet no one ever ventured to think of depriving the citizens of their right, for they had other methods by which they could either prevent the fault or correct it once committed. I will state the matter as it truly is. When the people began to be negligent in making their choice, and left the business to the presbyters as less suited to themselves, the presbyters abused the opportunity to usurp a domination, which they afterward established by putting forth new canons. Ordination is now nothing but a mere mockery. For the kind of examination they make a show of is so empty and trifling that it entirely lacks even the appearance of one. Therefore, when sovereigns, by agreement with the Roman Pontiffs, obtained for themselves the right of nominating bishops, the Church suffered no new injury, because the canons were merely deprived of an election which they had seized without any right, or acquired by stealth. Nothing, indeed, can be more disgraceful than that bishops should be sent from royal courts to take possession of churches, and godly princes would do well to refrain from such corruption. For there is an impious plundering of the Church whenever any people has a bishop thrust upon it whom it has not asked for, or at least freely approved. But that disorderly practice, which long existed in the churches, gave sovereigns occasion to take to themselves the presentation of bishops. They wanted the benefice to belong to themselves rather than to those who had no better right to it, and who abused it just as much.

4¶

Such is the famous call on account of which the bishops boast that they are the successors of the apostles. They say, moreover, that they alone can properly appoint presbyters. But here they most shamefully corrupt the ancient institution, in that by their ordination they appoint not presbyters to guide and feed the people, but priests to sacrifice. In the same way, when they consecrate deacons, they pay no regard to their true and proper office, but ordain them only to certain ceremonies concerning the cup and paten. But at the Council of Chalcedon it was decreed, on the contrary, that there should be no absolute ordinations, that is, ordinations without assigning to the ordained a place where they were to exercise their office. This decree is most useful for two reasons. First, so that churches may not be burdened with needless expense, nor idle men receive what ought to be distributed to the poor; and second, so that those who are ordained may consider that they are not promoted merely to an honorary office, but entrusted with a duty which they are solemnly bound to discharge. But the Roman authorities (who think that nothing in religion is to be cared for but their belly) take the first title to mean a revenue sufficient for their support, whether it comes from their own patrimony or from the priesthood. Accordingly, when they ordain presbyters or deacons, without any concern as to where they ought to minister, they confer the order, provided those ordained are rich enough to support themselves. But what man can admit that the title which the decree of the council requires is an annual revenue for sustenance? Again, when more recent canons made bishops liable for the support of those whom they had ordained without a fit title, so as to check excessive readiness to ordain, a method was devised of evading the penalty. For the man who is ordained promises that whatever title is named, he will be content with it. In this way he is barred from an action for maintenance. I say nothing of the thousand frauds committed here, as when some falsely claim the empty titles of benefices from which they cannot get a sixpence of revenue, and others by secret agreement obtain a temporary appointment which they promise to give back at once, but sometimes do not. There are still more mysteries of the same kind.

5¶

But even if these grosser abuses were removed, is it not always absurd to appoint a presbyter without assigning him a place? For when they ordain, it is only to sacrifice. But the lawful ordination of a presbyter is to the government of the Church, while deacons are called to the charge of alms. It is true that many pompous ceremonies are used to disguise the act, so that mere show may stir up veneration in the simple; but what effect can these appearances have on men of sound mind, when beneath them there is nothing solid or true? They used ceremonies either borrowed from Judaism or devised by themselves; it would be better if they abstained from these. Of the trial (for there is no need to say anything of the shadow of it which they retain), of the consent of the people, and of other necessary things, there is no mention. By shadow I mean those ridiculous gestures framed in an inept and cold imitation of antiquity. The bishops have their vicars, who inquire into doctrine before ordination. But what is the inquiry? Is it whether they can read their Missals, or whether they can decline some common noun that occurs in the lesson, or conjugate a verb, or give the meaning of some one word? For it is not necessary to give the sense of a single sentence. And yet even those who are lacking in these childish elements are not turned away, provided they bring the recommendation of money or influence. Of the same nature is the question put three times in an unintelligible voice when those who are to be ordained are brought to the altar, namely, Are they worthy of the honor? Someone (who has never seen them, but has his part in the play, so that no form may be missing) answers, They are worthy. "C'est un acte semblable, que quand ceux qu'on doit promouvoir se presentent à l'autel, on demande par trois fois en Latin, s'il ést digne; et quelcun qui ne l'a jamais vue, ou quelque valet de chambre que n'entend point Latin, repond en Latin qu'il est digne: tout ainsi qu'un personnage joueroit son rolle en une farce." That is: In the same way, when those whom they are to promote present themselves at the altar, they ask three times in Latin whether he is worthy; and someone who has never seen him, or some valet who does not understand Latin, replies in Latin that he is worthy, just as an actor would play his part in a farce.

6¶

Then in the conferring of benefices (which was formerly joined with ordination, but is now entirely separate), how much better do they conduct themselves? They have many reasons to give, for it is not the bishops alone who confer the office of priests (and even in their case, where they are called Collators, they do not always have the full right); others have the presentation, while the bishops keep only the honorary title of collation. To these are added nominations from schools, resignations, either simple or by way of exchange, commendatory rescripts, preventions, and the like. But they all conduct themselves in such a way that no one can reproach another. I maintain that in the Papacy at the present day scarcely one benefice in a hundred is conferred without simony, as the ancients defined it (Calvin on Art. 8:21). I do not say that all buy for a fixed sum; but show me one in twenty who does not reach the priesthood by some crooked method. Some owe their promotion to kinship or marriage ties, others to the influence of their parents, while others win favor by servility. In short, the end for which the offices are conferred is that provision may be made not for the churches, but for those who receive them. Accordingly, they call them benefices, and by that name they make it plain enough that they regard them in no other light than as the gifts by which princes either court the favor or reward the services of their soldiers. I say nothing of the fact that these rewards are conferred on barbers, cooks, grooms, and riffraff of that sort. At present, indeed, there are no cases in the law courts that make more noise than those concerning priestly offices, so that you may regard them as nothing else than game set before dogs to be hunted. Is it tolerable even to hear the name of pastors given to those who have forced their way into possession of a church as into an enemy's country? Who have won it by brawls in the courts? Who have bought it for a price? Who have labored for it by sordid flattery? Who, while still lisping boys, have obtained it like an inheritance from uncles and relatives? Sometimes even bastards obtain it from their fathers.

7¶

Was the license of the people, however corrupt and lawless, ever carried to such a height? But a more monstrous thing still is that one man (I do not say what kind of man, but certainly one who cannot govern himself) is appointed to the charge of five or six churches. In the courts of princes today you may see youths who are three times abbots, twice bishops, and once archbishops. Everywhere there are Canons loaded with five, six, or seven cures, of not one of which they take the least charge, except to draw the income. I will not object that the word of God cries out against this: it has long since ceased to carry the least weight with them. I will not object that many councils denounce the severest punishment against this dishonest practice; these too they boldly despise whenever it suits them. But I say that it is monstrous wickedness, utterly opposed to God, to nature, and to the government of the Church, that one thief should lie brooding over several churches, and that the name of pastor should be given to one who, even if he were willing, could not be present among his flock. And yet (such is their impudence) they cloak these abominations with the name of the church, so as to exempt them from all blame. Indeed, if you please, in these iniquities is contained that sacred succession to which, as they boast, it is owing that the Church does not perish.

8¶

Let us now see, as the second mark for judging a lawful pastor, how faithfully they discharge their office. Of the priests who are elected there, some are called monks, others seculars. The first of these herds was unknown to the early Church; indeed, even to hold such a place in the Church is so contrary to the monastic profession that in former times, when men were chosen out of monasteries for clerical offices, they ceased to be monks. And accordingly Gregory, though there were many abuses in his time, did not allow the offices to be confused in this way (Gregory, book 3, Epistle 11). For he insists that those who have been appointed abbots shall resign the clerical office, because no one can properly be a monk and a clerk at the same time, the one being an obstacle to the other. Now, if I were to ask how a man can properly fulfill his office when the canons declare him unfit for it, what answer, I ask, will they give? They will quote those abortive decrees of Innocent and Boniface, by which monks are admitted to the honor and power of the priesthood even though they remain in their monasteries. But is it at all reasonable that any unlearned ass, as soon as he has seized the Roman See, may overturn all antiquity by one little word? But of this matter later. Let it suffice for now that in the purer times of the Church it was regarded as a great absurdity for a monk to hold the office of priest. For Jerome declares that he does not perform the office of priest while he is living among monks, and ranks himself as one of the people to be governed by the priests. But granting them this, what duty do they perform? Some of the mendicants preach, while all the other monks chant or mutter masses in their cells; as if our Savior had wished, or the nature of the office permitted, presbyters to be made for such a purpose. When Scripture plainly testifies that it is the duty of a presbyter to rule his own church (Acts 20:28), is it not an impious profanation to turn it to another purpose, indeed, to change the sacred institution of God altogether? For when they are ordained, they are expressly forbidden to do what God commands of all presbyters. For this is their stock phrase: Let a monk, content with his cell, neither presume to administer the sacraments nor hold any other public office. Let them deny, if they can, that it is open mockery of God when anyone is appointed a presbyter in order to abstain from his proper and genuine office, and when he who has the name cannot have the thing.

9¶

I come to the seculars, some of whom are (as they say) beneficiaries, that is, they have offices by which they are maintained, while others hire out their services day by day to chant or say masses, and live after a fashion on the wages collected in this way. Benefices either carry a cure of souls, as bishoprics and parish charges do, or they are the stipends of pampered men who earn a living by chanting, such as prebends, canonries, parsonships, deaneries, chaplaincies, and the like; although, now that things have been turned upside down, the offices of abbot and prior are conferred not only on secular presbyters but also on boys by privilege, that is, by common and usual custom. As for the hirelings who seek their food from day to day, what else could they do than what they actually do, namely, prostitute themselves for gain in a mean and disgraceful way, especially given the vast multitude of them with which the world now teems? Hence, since they dare not beg openly, or think they would gain little in that way, they go about like hungry dogs, and by a kind of barking importunity extort from the unwilling what they may put into their hungry stomachs. If I were to try here to describe how disgraceful it is to the Church that the honor and office of a presbyter should come to this, I should never be done. My readers, therefore, must not expect from me a discourse that can fully portray this shameful indignity. I say briefly that if it is the office of a presbyter (as both the word of God prescribes (1 Corinthians 4:1) and the ancient canons require) to feed the Church and to administer the spiritual kingdom of Christ, then all those priests who have no work or wage except in the traffic of masses not only fail in their office, but have no lawful office to discharge. No place is given them to teach; they have no people to govern. In short, nothing is left them but an altar on which to sacrifice Christ; and this is to sacrifice not to God but to demons, as we shall show later (see chapter 18, sections 3, 9, 14).

10¶

I am not here touching on extraneous faults. French, "Ies vices des personnes"; the faults of individuals.

11¶

There still remain the bishops and the rectors of parishes; and I wish they would contend for the maintenance of their office. I would gladly grant that they have a godly and excellent office, if they would discharge it; but when they desert the churches committed to them, and throw the care of them on others, and still wish to be regarded as pastors, they act exactly as if the office of pastor were to do nothing. If some moneylender who never stirs from the city were to give himself out as a plowman or a vinedresser, or a soldier who has always been in the field or the camp, and has never seen books or the courts, were to pass himself off as a lawyer, who could tolerate the absurdity? Far more absurdly do those act who wish to be called and regarded as lawful pastors of the Church, and yet are unwilling to be so. How few there are who even in appearance take oversight of their church! Many spend their lives devouring the revenues of churches which they never visit even to inspect them. Some go once a year themselves, or send a steward, so that nothing may be lost in the leasing of them. When the corruption first crept in, those who wished to enjoy this kind of holiday pleaded privilege, but it is now a rare case for anyone to reside in his church. They look on them merely as farms, over which they set their vicars as bailiffs or tenant farmers. But it is contrary to common sense to regard as a shepherd one who has never seen a sheep of his flock.

12¶

It appears that some seeds of this corruption already existed in the time of Gregory, since the rulers of churches had begun to be more negligent in teaching; for he complains bitterly in these words: "The world is full of priests, and yet labourers in the harvest are rare, for we indeed undertake the office of the priesthood, but we perform not the work of the office" (Gregory, Homily 17). Again: "As they have no bowels of love, they would be thought lords, but do not at all acknowledge themselves to be fathers. They change a post of humility into the elevation of ascendancy." Again: "But we, O pastors! what are we doing, we who obtain the hire but are not labourers? We have fallen off to extraneous business; we undertake one thing, we perform another; we leave the ministry of the word, and, to our punishment, as I see, are called bishops, holding the honour of the name, not the power." Since he uses such bitter language against those who were only less diligent or zealous in their office, what, I ask, would he have said if he had seen that very few bishops, if any at all, and scarcely one in a hundred of the other clergy, went up into the pulpit even once in their whole lifetime? For men have come to such a pitch of folly that it is thought beneath the dignity of a bishop to preach a sermon to the people. In the time of Bernard things had grown still worse. Accordingly, we see how bitterly he inveighs against the whole order; and yet there is reason to believe that matters were then in a much better state than now.

13¶

Whoever will properly examine and weigh the whole form of church government as it now exists in the Papacy will find that there is no kind of plundering in which robbers act more licentiously, without law or measure. Certainly everything in it is so unlike, indeed so opposed to, the institution of Christ, has so fallen away from the ancient customs and practices of the Church, and is so contrary to nature and reason, that no greater injury can be done to Christ than to use his name in defending this disorderly rule. We (they say) are the pillars of the Church, the priests of religion, the vicegerents of Christ, the heads of the faithful, because the apostolic authority has come to us by succession. As if they were speaking to blocks of wood, they constantly pride themselves on these absurdities. Whenever they make such boasts, I in my turn will ask, What do they have in common with the apostles? We are not now dealing with some hereditary honor that can come to men while they are asleep, but with the office of preaching, which they so carefully shun. In the same way, when we maintain that their kingdom is the tyranny of Antichrist, they immediately object that their venerable hierarchy has often been extolled by great and holy men. As if the holy fathers, when they commended the hierarchy of the Church, or the spiritual government handed down to them by the apostles, ever dreamed of that shapeless and dreary chaos, where bishoprics are held for the most part by ignorant asses who do not even know the first and ordinary rudiments of the faith, or now and then by boys who have just left their nurse; or, if any are more learned (which, however, is a rare case), they regard the episcopal office as nothing else than a title of magnificence and splendor; where the rectors of churches no more think of feeding the flock than a cobbler thinks of plowing; where all things are thrown together in a confusion worse than that of Babel, so that no genuine trace of fatherly government can be seen any longer.

14¶

But if we come down to conduct, where is that light of the world which Christ requires, where the salt of the earth, where that holiness which might serve as a perpetual censorship? In our day there is no order of men more notorious for luxury, effeminacy, self-indulgence, and every kind of license; in no order are there more apt or skillful teachers of deceit, fraud, treachery, and perfidy; nowhere is there more skill or boldness in mischief, to say nothing of ostentation, pride, greed, and cruelty. The world is so sick of bearing these things that there is no fear that I may seem to exaggerate. One thing I say, which even they themselves will not be able to deny: among the bishops there is scarcely an individual, and among the parish clergy not one in a hundred, who, if sentence were passed on his conduct according to the ancient canons, would not deserve to be excommunicated, or at least deposed from his office. I seem to be saying something almost incredible, so completely has that ancient discipline, which required strict censure of the morals of the clergy, fallen into disuse; but such is really the fact. Let those who serve under the banner and auspices of the Roman See now go and boast of their priestly order. It is certain that what they have is not from Christ, nor from his apostles, nor from the fathers, nor from the early Church.

15¶

Let the deacons now come forward and show their most sacred distribution of the goods of the Church (see chapter 19, section 32). And yet their deacons are not elected for that purpose at all, for the only charge they lay on them is to minister at the altar, to read the Gospel, or to chant and perform I know not what frivolous acts. Nothing is said of alms, nothing of the care of the poor, nothing at all of the function they formerly performed. I am speaking of the institution itself; for if we look at what they do, theirs is in fact no office, but only a step toward the priesthood. In one thing those who hold the place of deacons in the mass display an empty image of antiquity, for they receive the offerings before the consecration. Now, the ancient practice was that before the communion of the Supper the faithful kissed one another and offered alms at the altar, thus declaring their love first by a symbol and afterward by an act of kindness. The deacon, who was the steward of the poor, received what was given so that he might distribute it. Now, no more of these alms reaches the poor than if they were thrown into the sea. They therefore deceive the Church by that lying deaconship. Certainly in this they have nothing resembling the apostolic institution or the ancient practice. The distribution of goods itself they have moved elsewhere, and have arranged it so that nothing more disorderly can be imagined. For as robbers, after murdering their victims, divide the plunder, so these men, after putting out the light of God's word, as if they had murdered the Church, have imagined that whatever had been dedicated to godly uses was marked out for prey and plunder. Accordingly, they have made a division, each seizing as much for himself as he could.

16¶

All those ancient methods we have explained are not only disturbed but entirely disguised and wiped out. The chief part of the plunder has gone to the bishops and the city presbyters, who, having enriched themselves in this way, have been turned into canons. That the division was a mere scramble is clear from this, that even to this day they are going to law over the shares. Be that as it may, the settlement has provided that out of all the goods of the Church not one penny shall go to the poor, to whom at least half belonged. The canons expressly assign a fourth part to them, while the other fourth they set apart for the bishops, to spend on hospitality and other offices of kindness. I say nothing of what the clergy ought to do with their portion, or the use to which they ought to put it, for it has been clearly shown that what is set apart for churches, buildings, and other expenses ought to be given to the poor in time of necessity. If they had one spark of the fear of God in their hearts, could they, I ask, bear the knowledge that all their food and clothing is the fruit of theft, indeed, of sacrilege? But since they are little moved by the judgment of God, they should at least reflect that those whom they would persuade that the orders of their Church are as beautiful and well arranged as they are accustomed to boast are men endowed with sense and reason. Let them answer briefly whether the diaconate is a license to rob and steal. If they deny it, they will be forced to confess that no diaconate remains among them, since the whole administration of their church's resources has been openly turned into sacrilegious plunder.

17¶

But here they use a very plausible gloss, for they say that the dignity of the Church is fittingly maintained by this magnificence. And some of their sect are so impudent as to dare to boast openly that only in this way are the prophecies fulfilled in which the ancient prophets describe the splendor of Christ's kingdom, where the priestly order is displayed in royal attire; and that it was not without cause that God made these promises to his Church: "All kings shall fall down before him: all nations shall serve him" (Psalm 72:11). "Awake, awake; put on thy strength, O Sion; put on thy beautiful garments, O Jerusalem, the holy city" (Isaiah 52:1). "All they from Sheba shall come; they shall bring gold and incense, and they shall show forth the praises of the Lord. All the flocks of Kedar shall be gathered together unto thee" (Isaiah 60:6, 7). I fear I should seem childish if I dwelt long on refuting this dishonesty. I do not wish, therefore, to waste words; I ask, however, if any Jew were to misapply these passages, what answer would they give? They would rebuke his stupidity in giving a carnal and worldly application to things said spiritually of Christ's spiritual kingdom. For we know that under the image of earthly objects the prophets have portrayed for us the heavenly glory which ought to shine in the Church. For in those blessings which these words literally express, the Church was never poorer than under the apostles; and yet all admit that the power of Christ's kingdom was then at its most flourishing. What, then, is the meaning of the passages above? That everything precious, lofty, and illustrious ought to be made subject to the Lord. As to its being said expressly of kings that they will submit to Christ, that they will cast their crowns at his feet, that they will dedicate their resources to the Church, when was this more truly and fully shown than when Theodosius, having thrown aside the purple and laid down the insignia of empire, humbled himself like one of the people before God and the Church in solemn repentance? Than when he and other godly princes like him made it their study and their care to preserve pure doctrine in the Church, and to cherish and protect sound teachers? But that priests did not then wallow in superfluous wealth is sufficiently shown by this one sentence of the Council of Aquileia, over which Ambrose presided: "Poverty in the priests of the Lord is glorious." It is certain that the bishops of that day had some means by which they might have made the glory of the Church conspicuous, if they had thought these the true ornaments of the Church. But knowing that nothing was more contrary to the duty of pastors than to pride themselves on the delicacies of the table, on splendid clothes, numerous attendants, and magnificent palaces, they cultivated and followed the humility and modesty, indeed the very poverty, which Christ has consecrated among his servants.

18¶

But not to be tedious, let us again sum up briefly and show how far that distribution, or rather squandering, of the goods of the Church which now exists differs from the true diaconate, which both the word of God commends and the ancient Church observed (Book 1, chapter 11, sections 7, 13; Book 3, chapter 20, section 30; above, chapter 4, section 8). I say that what is spent on adorning churches is wrongly laid out unless it is accompanied by that moderation which the very nature of sacred things prescribes, and which the apostles and other holy fathers prescribed by both precept and example. But is anything like this seen in the churches today? Whatever fits, I do not say that ancient frugality, but a decent moderation, is rejected. Nothing pleases but what smacks of luxury and the corruption of the times. Meanwhile, so far are they from taking proper care of the living temples, that they would let thousands of the poor perish sooner than break up the smallest cup or platter to relieve their need. So that I may not judge too severely on my own authority, I would only ask the godly reader to consider what Exuperius, the Bishop of Toulouse whom we have mentioned, or Acacius, or Ambrose, or anyone of like mind, would say if they were to rise from the dead. Certainly, while the needs of the poor are so great, they would not approve of their funds being carried off from them as superfluous; not to mention that, even if there were no poor, the uses to which these funds are put are harmful in many respects and useful in none. But I do not appeal to men. These goods have been dedicated to Christ, and ought to be distributed at his pleasure. In vain, however, will they make out that to be expenditure for Christ which they have squandered against his commands; though, to confess the truth, the ordinary revenue of the Church is not much reduced by these expenses. No bishoprics are so wealthy, no abbacies so productive, in short, no benefices so numerous and ample, as to satisfy the gluttony of the priests. But while they spare themselves, they induce the people through superstition to spend what ought to have been distributed to the poor on building temples, erecting statues, buying plate, and providing costly vestments. In this way the daily alms are swallowed up in this abyss.

19¶

Of the revenue they draw from lands and property, what else can I say than what I have already said, and what is plain before the eyes of all? We see with what kind of faithfulness the greatest portion is administered by those who are called bishops and abbots. What madness it is to look for church order here! Is it fitting that those whose life ought to have been a singular example of frugality, modesty, self-control, and humility should rival princes in the number of their attendants, the splendor of their houses, and the delicacies of dress and feasting? Can anything be more contrary to the duty of those whom the eternal and inviolable edict of God forbids to long for filthy lucre, and commands to be content with simple food, than not only to lay hands on villages and castles, but also to invade the largest provinces, and even to seize on empire itself? If they despise the word of God, what answer will they give to the ancient canons of councils, which decree that the bishop shall have a small dwelling not far from the church, and a frugal table and furniture (Council of Carthage, chapters 14, 15)? What answer will they give to the declaration of the Council of Aquileia, in which poverty in the priests of the Lord is pronounced glorious? For the charge which Jerome gives to Nepotian, to make the poor and strangers acquainted with his table, and to have Christ with them as a guest, they would perhaps reject as too austere. What he adds immediately afterward they would be ashamed to acknowledge, namely, that the glory of a bishop is to provide for the support of the poor, and that the disgrace of all priests is to make a study of their own riches. This they cannot admit without covering themselves with disgrace. But there is no need to press them so hard here, since all we wished was to show that the lawful order of deacons was abolished long ago, and that they can no longer pride themselves on this order in commendation of their Church. This, I think, has been fully established.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Here Calvin lays bare how the noble order of the ancient Church was hollowed out — offices handed to the unlearned and the dissolute, men made bishops who never taught, a structure preserved in name while its substance was devoured by tyranny. The lesson is sobering: corruption rarely arrives by open denial; it creeps in when the form is kept but the calling is gutted. A word for 2026: watchfulness is not cynicism but love, and the believer who guards what he has been given must learn to ask not merely whether a thing bears the right name, but whether Christ is truly served beneath it. Examine the ministries you support and the men you follow — do they feed the flock or merely occupy the chair? Train your children to discern, not to drift, lest a generation inherit empty titles and forfeit the gospel. Steadfast attachment to the truth, and a willingness to grieve and resist its counterfeits, is the inheritance we owe those who come after us.
