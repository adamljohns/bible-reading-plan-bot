Of Christian Liberty

The three divisions of this chapter are,—I. Necessity of the doctrine of Christian Liberty, sec. 1. The principal parts of this liberty explained, sec. 2-8. II. The nature and efficacy of this liberty against the Epicureans and others who take no account whatever of the weak, sec. 9 and 10. III. Of offense given and received. A lengthened and not unnecessary discussion of this subject, sec. 11-16. 1. Connection of this chapter with the previous one on Justification. A true knowledge of Christian liberty useful and necessary. 1. It purifies the conscience. 2. It checks licentiousness. 3. It maintains the merits of Christ, the truth of the Gospel, and the peace of the soul. 2. This liberty consists of three parts. First, Believers renouncing the righteousness of the law, look only to Christ. Objection. Answer, distinguishing between Legal and Evangelical righteousness. 3. This first part clearly established by the whole Epistle to the Galatians. 4. The second part of Christian liberty—viz. that the conscience, freed from the yoke of the law, voluntarily obeys the will of God. This cannot be done so long as we are under the law. Reason. 5. When freed from the rigorous exactions of the law, we can cheerfully and with much alacrity answer the call of God. 6. Proof of this second part from an Apostle. The end of this liberty. 7. Third part of liberty—viz. the free rise of things indifferent. The knowledge of this part necessary to remove despair and superstition. Superstition described. 8. Proof of this third part from the Epistle to the Romans. Those who observe it not only use evasion. 1. Despisers of God. 2. The desperate. 3. The ungrateful. The end and scope of this third part. 9. Second part of the chapter, showing the nature and efficacy of Christian liberty, in opposition to the Epicureans. Their character described. Pretext and allegation. Use of things indifferent. Abuse detected. Mode of correcting it. 10. This liberty maintained in opposition to those who pay no regard to the weak. Error of this class of men refuted. A most pernicious error. Objection. Reply. 11. Application of the doctrine of Christian liberty to the subject of offenses. These of two kinds. Offense given. Offense received. Of offense given, a subject comprehended by few. Of Pharisaical offense, or offense received. 12. Who are to be regarded as weak and Pharisaical. Proved by examples and the doctrine of Paul. The just moderation of Christian liberty. necessity of vindicating it. No regard to be paid to hypocrites. Duty of edifying our weak neighbors. 13. Application of the doctrine to things indifferent. Things necessary not to be omitted from any fear of offense. 14. Refutation of errors in regard to Christian liberty. The consciences of the godly not to be fettered by human traditions in matters of indifference. 15. Distinction to be made between Spiritual and Civil government. These must not be confounded. How far conscience can be bound by human constitutions. Definition of conscience. Definition explained by passages from the Apostolic writings. 16. The relation which conscience bears to external obedience; first, in things good and evil; secondly, in things indifferent.

1¶

We must now treat of Christian liberty, the explanation of which certainly ought not to be omitted by anyone who proposes to give a concise summary of gospel doctrine. For it is a matter of the first necessity, and without the knowledge of it the conscience can scarcely attempt anything without hesitation; in many things it must hold back and waver, and in all things it proceeds with fickleness and fear. In particular, it forms a fitting appendix to justification, and is of no small help in understanding its force. Indeed, those who seriously fear God will see from this the incomparable benefits of a doctrine which wicked scoffers are constantly attacking with their jibes, since the drunkenness of mind under which they labor leaves their insolence without restraint. This, therefore, seems the proper place to consider the subject. Moreover, though it has already been touched on now and then, there was an advantage in putting off the fuller treatment of it until now; for the moment any mention is made of Christian liberty, lust begins to boil, or mad disturbances arise, unless a swift restraint is laid on those licentious spirits by whom the best things are perverted into the worst. For either, under the pretext of this liberty, they shake off all obedience to God and break out into unbridled license, or they are indignant, thinking that all choice, order, and restraint are abolished. What can we do when we are hemmed in by such straits? Are we to say farewell to Christian liberty, in order to cut off every opportunity for such dangerous consequences? But, as we have said, unless the subject is understood, neither Christ, nor the truth of the gospel, nor the inward peace of the soul is rightly known. Our effort must rather be, while not suppressing this very necessary part of doctrine, to meet the absurd objections to which it usually gives rise.

2¶

Christian liberty, in my view, consists of three parts. First, the consciences of believers, when they seek assurance of their justification before God, must rise above the law and think no more of obtaining justification by it. For since the law, as has already been shown (above, chapter 17, section 1), leaves not one man righteous, either we are shut out from all hope of justification, or we must be set free from the law, and so set free that no account at all is taken of works. For whoever imagines that, in order to obtain justification, he must bring any amount of works whatever, cannot fix any measure or limit, but makes himself a debtor to the whole law. Therefore, setting aside all mention of the law and every thought of works, we must, in the matter of justification, take refuge in the mercy of God alone; turning our gaze away from ourselves, we must look to Christ alone. For the question is not how we may be righteous, but how, though unworthy and unrighteous, we may be regarded as righteous. If consciences would gain any assurance of this, they must give no place to the law. Still, it cannot rightly be inferred from this that believers have no need of the law. It does not cease to teach, exhort, and urge them to what is good, although it has no place in their consciences before the judgment seat of God. The two things are very different and must be well and carefully distinguished. The whole life of Christians ought to be a kind of striving after godliness, since they are called to holiness (Ephesians 1:4; 1 Thessalonians 4:5). The office of the law is to stir them up to the pursuit of purity and holiness by reminding them of their duty. For when the conscience is anxious about how it may have the favor of God, what answer it could give, and what confidence it would have if brought before his judgment seat, in such a case the requirements of the law are not to be brought forward; Christ alone, who surpasses all the perfection of the law, is to be held forth for righteousness.

3¶

On this almost the whole argument of the Epistle to the Galatians turns; for it can be proved from express passages that those interpreters are absurd who teach that Paul is contending there only for freedom from ceremonies. Among such passages are these: "Christ has redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us." "Stand fast, therefore, in the liberty wherewith Christ has made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage. Behold, I Paul say unto you, that if ye be circumcised, Christ shall profit you nothing. For I testify again to every man that is circumcised, that he is a debtor to do the whole law. Christ is become of no effect unto you, whosoever of you are justified by the law; ye are fallen from grace" (Galatians 3:13; 5:1-4). These words certainly refer to something of a higher order than freedom from ceremonies. I admit, indeed, that Paul treats of ceremonies there, because he was contending with false apostles who were scheming to bring back into the Christian Church those ancient shadows of the law which were abolished by the coming of Christ. But in discussing this question it was necessary to bring in higher matters, on which the whole controversy turns. First, because the brightness of the gospel was obscured by those Jewish shadows, he shows that in Christ we have a full manifestation of all the things that were foreshadowed by the Mosaic ceremonies. Secondly, since those impostors were instilling into the people the most harmful opinion that this obedience was enough to merit the grace of God, he insists very strongly that believers must not imagine that they can obtain justification before God by any works, much less by those paltry observances. At the same time he shows that by the cross of Christ they are free from the condemnation of the law, to which otherwise all men are exposed, so that in Christ alone they may rest in full security. This argument bears on the present subject (Galatians 4:5, 21, and so on). Lastly, he asserts the right of believers to liberty of conscience, a liberty which may not be restrained without necessity.

4¶

Another point, which depends on the former, is that consciences obey the law not as if compelled by legal necessity, but, being free from the yoke of the law itself, they obey the will of God of their own accord. Since they are in constant terror as long as they are under the dominion of the law, they are never inclined to obey God promptly unless they have first obtained this liberty. Our meaning will be explained more briefly and clearly by an example. The command of the law is, "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your might" (Deuteronomy 6:5). To accomplish this, the soul must first be stripped of every other thought and feeling, the heart cleansed of all its desires, and all its powers gathered and united on this one object. Those who, compared with others, have made much progress in the way of the Lord are still very far from this goal. For although they love God in their mind and with a sincere affection of heart, yet both are still in great measure taken up with the lusts of the flesh, by which they are held back and kept from moving toward God at a quickened pace. They do indeed make many efforts, but the flesh partly weakens their strength and partly binds them to itself. What can they do when they feel in this way that there is nothing they are less capable of than fulfilling the law? They wish, they aspire, they strive; but they do nothing with the required perfection. If they look to the law, they see that every work they attempt or plan is accursed. Nor can anyone deceive himself by concluding that the work is not altogether bad merely because it is imperfect, and that therefore whatever good is in it is still accepted by God. For the law, demanding perfect love, condemns all imperfection unless its rigor is softened. Let any man, then, consider the work which he wants to be thought partly good, and he will find that it is a transgression of the law for the very reason that it is imperfect.

5¶

See how our works lie under the curse of the law if they are tested by the standard of the law. But how can unhappy souls set themselves eagerly to a work from which they can hope to gain nothing in return but a curse? On the other hand, if, freed from this severe demand, or rather from the whole rigor of the law, they hear themselves invited by God with fatherly gentleness, they will cheerfully and readily obey the call and follow his guidance. In a word, those who are bound by the yoke of the law are like servants who have certain tasks assigned to them daily by their masters. Such servants think that nothing has been done, and they do not dare to come into their masters' presence until the exact amount of work has been completed. But sons, who are treated by their parents in a more open and generous way, do not hesitate to offer them works that are only begun or half finished, or even with something faulty in them, trusting that their obedience and willingness of mind will be accepted, even though the performance is less exact than was wished. Such should be our feelings, since we surely trust that our most indulgent Father will approve our services, however small they may be, and however rough and imperfect. So he declares to us by the prophet, "I will spare them as a man spareth his own son that serveth him" (Malachi 3:17), where the word spare plainly means indulgence, or the overlooking of faults, while at the same time the service is remembered. This confidence is necessary in no small degree, since without it everything would be attempted in vain; for God does not regard any work of ours as done for himself unless it is truly done from a desire to serve him. But how can this be amid these terrors, while we doubt whether God is offended or served by our work?

6¶

This is why the author of the Epistle to the Hebrews ascribes to faith all the good works which the holy patriarchs are said to have performed, and judges them by faith alone (Hebrews 11:2). On this liberty there is a remarkable passage in the Epistle to the Romans, where Paul argues, "Sin shall not have dominion over you; for ye are not under the law, but under grace" (Romans 6:14). For after he had exhorted believers, "Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body, that ye should obey it in the lusts thereof: Neither yield ye your members as instruments of unrighteousness unto sin; but yield yourselves unto God, as those that are alive from the dead, and your members as instruments of righteousness unto God," they might have objected that they still carried about with them a body full of lust, and that sin still dwelt in them. He therefore comforts them by adding that they are freed from the law; as if he had said, Although you feel that sin is not yet put out, and that righteousness does not yet fully live in you, you have no reason for fear and dejection, as if God were always offended because of the remains of sin, since by grace you are freed from the law, and your works are not tried by its standard. Let those, however, who infer that they may sin because they are not under the law understand that they have no right to this liberty, the purpose of which is to encourage us in doing good.

7¶

The third part of this liberty is that we are not bound before God to any observance of external things which are in themselves indifferent (ἀδιάφορα), but that we are now fully free either to use them or to leave them alone. The knowledge of this liberty is very necessary for us; where it is lacking, our consciences will have no rest and there will be no end of superstition. In our day many think us absurd for raising a question about the free eating of meat, the free use of dress and holidays, and similar frivolous trifles, as they consider them; but they are of more importance than is commonly supposed. For once the conscience is caught in the net, it enters a long and tangled labyrinth from which it is afterward very hard to escape. When a man begins to doubt whether it is lawful for him to use linen for sheets, shirts, napkins, and handkerchiefs, he will not long be secure about hemp, and at last he will have doubts about tow; for he will turn over in his mind whether he cannot eat supper without napkins, or do without handkerchiefs. If he thinks a daintier food unlawful, he will afterward feel uneasy about eating loaf bread and common fare, because he will think that his body might perhaps be sustained on still meaner food. If he hesitates over a more pleasant wine, he will hardly drink the worst with a good conscience; and at last he will not dare to touch water if it is sweeter and purer than usual. In short, he will come to the point of thinking it a crime to step on a straw lying in his path. For it is no trivial dispute that is begun here: the point in question is whether the use of this thing or that agrees with the divine will, which ought to govern all our acts and plans. Here some must be hurried by despair into an abyss, while others, despising God and casting off his fear, will not be able to make a way for themselves without ruin. When men are entangled in such doubts, whichever way they turn, everything they see must offend their conscience.

8¶

"I know," says Paul, "that there is nothing unclean of itself" (by unclean meaning unholy); "but to him that esteemeth any thing to be unclean, to him it is unclean" (Romans 14:14). By these words he makes all external things subject to our liberty, provided that the nature of that liberty commends itself to our minds as before God. But if some superstitious notion raises scruples, things which were pure in their own nature become defiled to us. Therefore the apostle adds, "Happy is he that condemneth not himself in that which he alloweth. And he that doubteth is damned if he eat, because he eateth not of faith: for whatsoever is not of faith is sin" (Romans 14:22, 23). When men in such difficulties go ahead with greater boldness, doing securely whatever pleases them, do they not to that extent revolt from God? Those who are deeply moved by some fear of God, if they are forced to do many things against their consciences, are discouraged and filled with dread. None of these receive any of the gifts of God with thanksgiving, by which alone, Paul declares, all things are sanctified for our use (1 Timothy 4:5). By thanksgiving I mean that which comes from a mind that recognizes the kindness and goodness of God in his gifts. For many indeed understand that the blessings they enjoy are the gifts of God, and they praise God with their words; but since they are not persuaded that these have been given to them, how can they thank God as the giver? In a word, we see where this liberty tends, namely, that we are to use the gifts of God without any scruple of conscience and without any disturbance of mind, for the purpose for which he gave them; in this way our souls may both have peace with him and recognize his generosity toward us. For here are included all ceremonies whose observance is free, so that while our consciences are not to be placed under the necessity of observing them, we are also to remember that, by the kindness of God, the use of them is made to serve edification.

9¶

It must, however, be carefully observed that Christian liberty is, in all its parts, a spiritual thing, whose whole force consists in giving peace to trembling consciences, whether they are anxious and troubled about the forgiveness of sins, or about whether their imperfect works, stained by the weaknesses of the flesh, are pleasing to God, or are perplexed about the use of things indifferent. It is therefore perversely interpreted by those who use it as a cloak for their lusts, so that they may abuse the good gifts of God without restraint, or who think there is no liberty unless it is exercised in front of men, and who therefore, in using it, pay no regard to their weak brothers. Under this head the sins of the present age are more numerous. For there is scarcely anyone whose means allow him to live lavishly who does not delight in feasting, and dress, and the luxurious grandeur of his house, who does not wish to outdo his neighbor in every kind of refinement, and who does not pride himself immensely on his splendor. And all these things are defended under the pretext of Christian liberty. They say they are things indifferent. I grant it, provided they are used indifferently. But when they are too eagerly coveted, when they are proudly boasted of, when they are indulged in with luxurious extravagance, things which were otherwise lawful in themselves are certainly defiled by these vices. Paul makes an admirable distinction regarding things indifferent: "Unto the pure all things are pure: but unto them that are defiled and unbelieving is nothing pure; but even their mind and conscience is defiled" (Titus 1:15). For why is a woe pronounced on the rich who have received their consolation (Luke 6:24), who are full, who laugh now, who "lie upon beds of ivory and stretch themselves upon their couches," who "join house to house" and "lay field to field," "and the harp and the viol, the tablet and pipe, and wine, are in their feasts" (Amos 6:6; Isaiah 5:8, 10)? Certainly ivory and gold and riches are good creatures of God, permitted, indeed appointed, by divine providence for the use of man; nor was it ever forbidden to laugh, or to be full, or to add new possessions to old and inherited ones, or to delight in music, or to drink wine. This is true; but when the means are at hand, to roll and wallow in luxury, to intoxicate the mind and soul with present pleasures and to be always hunting after new ones, is very far from a lawful use of the gifts of God. Let them, therefore, put away immoderate desire, immoderate extravagance, vanity, and arrogance, so that they may use the gifts of God purely, with a pure conscience. When their mind is brought to this soberness, they will be able to keep to the lawful use. On the other hand, where this moderation is lacking, even plain and ordinary delicacies are excessive. For it is a true saying that a haughty mind often dwells in coarse and homely clothing, while true humility hides under fine linen and purple. Let everyone, then, live in his own station, whether poorly, or moderately, or in splendor; but let all remember that God gives nourishment for life, not for luxury, and let them regard it as the law of Christian liberty to learn with Paul, in whatever state they are, "therewith to be content," to know "both how to be abased" and "how to abound," "to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need" (Philippians 4:11).

10¶

Very many also err in this: as if their liberty were not safe and whole without men to witness it, they use it indiscriminately and imprudently, and in this way they often give offense to weak brothers. You may see some today who cannot think they possess their liberty unless they take possession of it by eating meat on Friday. Their eating I do not blame, but this false notion must be driven from their minds; for they ought to understand that their liberty gains nothing new from being seen by men, but is to be enjoyed before God, and consists as much in abstaining as in using. If they understand that it makes no difference in the sight of God whether they eat meat or eggs, whether they are dressed in red or in black, that is quite enough. The conscience to which the benefit of this liberty was owed is set free. Therefore, even if for the rest of their whole life they should abstain from meat and always wear one color, they are no less free. Indeed, precisely because they are free, they abstain with a free conscience. But they err most grievously in paying no regard to the weakness of their brothers, which we ought to bear with, so as not to give them offense rashly. But French, "Mais quelcun dira"; but someone will say.

11¶

I will here make some observations on offenses: what distinctions are to be made among them, and which kind are to be avoided and which disregarded. This will afterward enable us to determine what scope there is for our liberty among men. We are pleased with the common division into offense given and offense taken, since it has the plain sanction of Scripture and expresses the meaning quite properly. If, from untimely levity, or wantonness, or rashness, you do anything out of order or out of its proper place, by which the weak or the inexperienced are offended, it may be said that offense has been given by you, since the occasion of the offense comes from your fault. And in general, offense is said to be given in any matter where the person from whom it came is at fault. Offense is said to be taken when a thing which was otherwise done, not wickedly or untimely, is made an occasion of offense out of ill will or some malicious feeling. For here offense was not given, but malicious interpreters constantly take offense. By the first kind only the weak are offended; by the second, the ill-tempered and Pharisaical. Therefore we shall call the one the offense of the weak and the other the offense of Pharisees, and we will so temper the use of our liberty as to make it yield to the ignorance of weak brothers, but not to the severity of Pharisees. What is due to weakness Paul shows fully in many passages. "Him that is weak in the faith receive ye." Again, "Let us not judge one another any more: but judge this rather, that no man put a stumbling-block, or an occasion to fall, in his brother's way"; and many others to the same effect in the same place, to which we refer the reader rather than quoting them here. The sum is, "We then that are strong ought to bear the infirmities of the weak, and not to please ourselves. Let every one of us please his neighbor for his good to edification." Elsewhere he says, "Take heed lest by any means this liberty of yours become a stumbling-block to them that are weak." Again, "Whatsoever is sold in the shambles, that eat, asking no question for conscience sake." "Conscience, I say, not your own, but of the other." Finally, "Give none offense, neither to the Jews nor to the Gentiles nor to the Church of God." Also in another passage, "Brethren, ye have been called into liberty, only use not liberty for an occasion to the flesh, but by love serve one another." Romans 14:1, 13; 16:1; 1 Corinthians 8:9; 10:25, 29, 32; Galatians 5:13.

12¶

The matter still remains uncertain unless we understand who are the weak and who the Pharisees; for if this distinction is removed, I do not see how, where offenses are concerned, any liberty at all would remain that was not constantly in the greatest danger. But Paul seems to me to have marked out most clearly, by example as well as by teaching, how far our liberty, where offense is concerned, is to be moderated or maintained. When he takes Timothy as his companion, he circumcises him; nothing can persuade him to circumcise Titus (Acts 16:3; Galatians 2:3). The acts are different, but there is no difference in purpose or intention. In circumcising Timothy, though he was free from all men, he made himself the servant of all: "Unto the Jews I became as a Jew, that I might gain the Jews; to them that are under the law, as under the law, that I might gain them that are under the law; to them that are without law, as without law (being not without law to God, but under the law to Christ), that I might gain them that are without law. To the weak became I as weak that I might gain the weak: I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some" (1 Corinthians 9:20-22). Here we have the proper moderation of liberty, when in things indifferent it can be restrained to some good purpose. What he had in view in firmly resisting the circumcision of Titus, he himself testifies when he writes: "But neither Titus, who was with me, being a Greek, was compelled to be circumcised: and that because of false brethren unawares brought in, who came in privily to spy out our liberty which we have in Christ Jesus, that they might bring us into bondage: to whom we gave place by subjection, no, not for an hour, that the truth of the gospel might continue with you" (Galatians 2:3-5). Here we see the necessity of defending our liberty when, through the unjust demands of false apostles, it is put in danger in weak consciences. In all cases we must pursue love and look to the edification of our neighbor. "All things are lawful for me," he says, "but all things are not expedient; all things are lawful for me, but all things edify not. Let no man seek his own, but every man another's wealth" (1 Corinthians 10:23, 24). Nothing is plainer than this rule: we are to use our liberty if it tends to the edification of our neighbor, but if it is not helpful to our neighbor, we are to abstain from it. There are some who pretend to imitate this prudence of Paul by abstaining from liberty, while there is nothing for which they use it less than for the purposes of love. Consulting their own ease, they would have all mention of liberty buried, though it is no less in the interest of our neighbors to use liberty for their good and edification than to moderate it at times for their benefit. It is the part of a godly man to think that the free power granted to him in external things is meant to make him the readier for all the duties of love.

14¶

Since, by means of this privilege of liberty which we have described, believers have received authority from Christ not to entangle themselves in the observance of things in which he wished them to be free, we conclude that their consciences are exempt from all human authority. For it would be unfitting that the gratitude owed to Christ for his generous gift should perish, or that the consciences of believers should derive no benefit from it. We must not regard it as a trivial matter when we see how much it cost our Savior, since it was purchased not with silver or gold but with his own blood (1 Peter 1:18, 19); so that Paul does not hesitate to say that Christ has died in vain if we place our souls in subjection to men (Galatians 5:1, 4; 1 Corinthians 7:23). Several chapters of the Epistle to the Galatians are wholly taken up with showing that Christ is obscured, or rather extinguished, for us unless our consciences maintain their liberty; and they have certainly fallen from it if they can be bound with the chains of laws and constitutions at the pleasure of men. But as the knowledge of this subject is of the greatest importance, so it calls for a longer and clearer exposition. For the moment the abolition of human constitutions is mentioned, the greatest disturbances are stirred up, partly by the seditious and partly by slanderers, as if obedience of every kind were at the same time abolished and overthrown.

15¶

Therefore, so that this may not become a stumbling block to anyone, let us observe that in man there is a twofold government: the one spiritual, by which the conscience is trained to godliness and the worship of God; the other civil, by which the individual is instructed in the duties which, as men and citizens, we are bound to perform (see Book 4, chapter 10, sections 3-6). To these two forms are commonly given the not inappropriate names of spiritual and temporal jurisdiction, indicating that the former kind has to do with the life of the soul, while the latter concerns matters of the present life, not only food and clothing, but the enacting of laws which require a man to live among his fellows purely, honorably, and modestly. The former has its seat within the soul; the latter only regulates outward conduct. We may call the one the spiritual kingdom and the other the civil kingdom. Now these two, as we have divided them, must always be viewed apart from each other. When the one is being considered, we should call our minds away and not allow them to think of the other. For there are in man, as it were, two worlds, over which different kings and different laws can preside. By attending to this distinction we will not mistakenly transfer the doctrine of the gospel concerning spiritual liberty to civil order, as if, in regard to outward government, Christians were less subject to human laws because their consciences are free before God, or as if they were exempt from all service of the flesh because they are free in regard to the Spirit. Again, because even in those constitutions which seem to relate to the spiritual kingdom there may be some deception, it is necessary to distinguish between those which are to be held lawful as agreeing with the Word of God, and those, on the other hand, which ought to have no place among the godly. We shall have another opportunity of speaking of civil government (see Book 4, chapter 20). For the present I also put off speaking of ecclesiastical laws, because that subject will be discussed more fully in the Fourth Book, when we come to treat of the power of the Church. We would therefore conclude the present discussion in this way. The question, as I have said, though not very obscure or perplexing in itself, causes difficulty for many because they do not distinguish with enough precision between what is called the external forum and the forum of conscience. What increases the difficulty is that Paul commands us to obey the magistrate "not only for wrath, but also for conscience sake" (Romans 13:1, 5). From this it follows that civil laws also bind the conscience. If this were so, then what we said a little earlier, and have still to say, about spiritual government would fall. To resolve this difficulty, the first important thing is to understand what is meant by conscience. The definition must be sought from the derivation of the word. For just as men, when they grasp the knowledge of things by the mind and understanding, are said to know, and from this comes the word knowledge or science, so when they have a sense of the divine justice added as a witness, which does not allow them to hide their sins but drags them forward as culprits to the bar of God, that sense is called conscience. For it stands, as it were, between God and man, not allowing man to suppress what he knows in himself, but pursuing him until he is convicted. This is what Paul means when he says, "Their conscience also bearing witness, and their thoughts the meanwhile accusing, or else excusing one another" (Romans 2:15). Simple knowledge might remain in man shut up, as it were; therefore this sense, which summons man before the bar of God, is set over him as a kind of sentinel to watch and search out all his secrets, so that nothing may remain buried in darkness. Hence the ancient proverb, Conscience is a thousand witnesses. For the same reason Peter also uses the expression "the answer of a good conscience" (1 Peter 3:21) for peace of mind, when, persuaded of the grace of Christ, we present ourselves boldly before God. And the author of the Epistle to the Hebrews says that we have "no more conscience of sins" (Hebrews 10:2), meaning that we are held to be freed or acquitted, so that sin no longer accuses us.

16¶

Therefore, just as works have respect to men, so conscience has reference to God, a good conscience being nothing else than inward integrity of heart. In this sense Paul says that "the end of the commandment is charity, out of a pure heart, and of a good conscience, and of faith unfeigned" (1 Timothy 1:5). Later in the same chapter he shows how much it differs from intellect when he speaks of "holding faith, and a good conscience; which some having put away, have made shipwreck" (1 Timothy 1:19). For by these words he indicates that it is a living inclination to serve God, a sincere desire to live in godliness and holiness. Sometimes, indeed, it is extended even to men, as when Paul testifies, "Herein do I exercise myself, to have always a conscience void of offense toward God, and toward men" (Acts 24:16). He speaks this way because the fruits of a good conscience go out and reach even to men. But, as I have said, properly speaking it refers to God alone. Hence a law is said to bind the conscience because it binds the individual simply, without looking at men or taking any account of them. For example, God not only commands us to keep our mind chaste and pure from lust, but forbids all outward lewdness or obscenity of speech. My conscience is subject to the observance of this law even if there were not another man in the world; and whoever violates it not only sins by setting a bad example to his brothers, but stands convicted in his conscience before God. The same rule does not hold in things indifferent. We ought to abstain from everything that causes offense, but with a free conscience. So Paul, speaking of meat consecrated to idols, says, "If any man say unto you, This is offered in sacrifice unto idols, eat not for his sake that showed it, and for conscience sake": "Conscience, I say, not your own, but of the other" (1 Corinthians 10:28, 29). A believer, after being warned beforehand, would sin if he still ate meat so offered. But though abstinence on his part is necessary with respect to his brother, as it is commanded by God, he still does not cease to retain liberty of conscience. We see how the law, while binding the outward act, leaves the conscience free.

A Word for 2026 — U.S.M.C. Ministries Calvin's account of Christian liberty is no charter for indulgence but the believer's threefold freedom: the conscience released from seeking justification by the law, the heart freed to obey God cheerfully as a son rather than a cowering servant, and the liberty to use or abstain from things indifferent without superstition or scruple. Yet this freedom is spiritual, ordered to the peace of trembling consciences, and always tempered by love for the weaker brother. In 2026, when liberty is loudly claimed as a cloak for appetite — feasting, finery, and self-display all defended as harmless — the watchful home must recover Calvin's balance: free indeed, but never using freedom as an occasion for the flesh. Distinguish the spiritual kingdom from the civil; let no human tradition bind the conscience Christ purchased with his blood, yet remember that the same liberty which permits also restrains, for the sake of a brother's edification. Live in your station, content, holding your freedom with a free conscience and a tender care for those who watch you.
