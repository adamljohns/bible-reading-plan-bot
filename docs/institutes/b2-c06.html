Book 2 · Chapter 6 · USMC modern English

Redemption for Man Lost to Be Sought in Christ

The parts of this chapter are, I. The excellence of the doctrine of Christ the Redeemer—a doctrine always entertained by the Church, sec. 1. II. Christ, the Mediator in both dispensations, was offered to the faith of the pious Israelites and people of old, as is plain from the institution of sacrifice, the calling of Abraham’s family, and the elevation of David and his posterity, sec. 2. III. Hence the consolation, strength, hope, and confidence of the godly under the Law, Christ being offered to them in various ways by their heavenly Father.

Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. The knowledge of God the Creator of no avail without faith in Christ the Redeemer. First reason. Second reason strengthened by the testimony of an Apostle. Conclusion. This doctrine entertained by the children of God in all ages from the beginning of the world. Error of throwing open heaven to the heathen, who know nothing of Christ. The pretexts for this refuted by passages of Scripture.
  2. 2. God never was propitious to the ancient Israelites without Christ the Mediator. First reason founded on the institution of sacrifice. Second reason founded on the calling of Abraham. Third reason founded on the elevation of David’s family to regal dignity, and confirmed by striking passages of Scripture.
  3. 3. Christ the solace ever promised to the afflicted; the banner of faith and hope always erected. This confirmed by various passages of Scripture.
  4. 4. The Jews taught to have respect to Christ. This teaching sanctioned by our Saviour himself. The common saying, that God is the object of faith, requires to be explained and modified. Conclusion of this discussion concerning Christ. No saving knowledge of God in the heathen.
1¶

Since the whole human race was undone in the person of Adam, the excellence and dignity of our origin, as already described, is so far from benefiting us that it rather turns to our greater disgrace, until God — who does not acknowledge man, defiled and corrupted by sin, as his own work — appears as a Redeemer in the person of his only begotten Son. Since we have fallen from life into death, all that knowledge of God the Creator which we have discussed would be useless unless it were followed by faith, which holds God out to us as a Father in Christ. The natural order undoubtedly was that the structure of the world should be a school in which we might learn godliness, and from it pass on to eternal life and perfect happiness. But after looking at the perfection we see wherever we turn our eyes, above and below, we are met by the divine curse, which, while it involves innocent creatures in our guilt, necessarily fills our own souls with despair. For although God is still pleased to show his fatherly favor toward us in many ways, we cannot conclude that he is a Father from a mere survey of the world. Conscience presses on us within and shows that sin is a just reason for our being forsaken, and it will not allow us to think that God regards or treats us as sons. Added to this are our sloth and ingratitude. Our minds are so blinded that they cannot perceive the truth, and all our senses are so corrupt that we wickedly rob God of his glory. We must therefore conclude with Paul: "After that in the wisdom of God the world by wisdom knew not God, it pleased God by the foolishness of preaching to save them that believe" (1 Corinthians 1:21). By the "wisdom of God" he means this magnificent theater of heaven and earth, filled with countless wonders, the wise contemplation of which should have enabled us to know God. But we do this with little profit; and therefore he invites us to faith in Christ — a faith which, by its appearance of foolishness, repels the unbeliever. Therefore, although the preaching of the cross does not agree with human wisdom, we must nevertheless humbly embrace it if we would return to God our Maker, from whom we are estranged, so that he may again become our Father. It is certain that after the fall of our first parent no knowledge of God apart from a Mediator was effectual for salvation. Christ is not speaking of his own age only, but embraces all ages, when he says, "This is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent" (John 17:3). All the more shameful, therefore, is the presumption of those who throw heaven open to the unbelieving and profane without that grace which Scripture consistently describes as the only door by which we enter into life. If anyone should restrict our Savior's words to the period after the proclamation of the Gospel, the refutation is ready at hand; for on a ground common to all ages and nations it is declared that those who are estranged from God, and as such are under the curse and are children of wrath, cannot be pleasing to God until they are reconciled. To this we may add the answer our Savior gave to the Samaritan woman: "Ye worship ye know not what; we know what we worship: for salvation is of the Jews" (John 4:22). By these words he both charges every Gentile religion with falsehood and gives the reason — namely, that under the Law the Redeemer was promised to the chosen people only, and that consequently no worship was ever pleasing to God which did not have regard to Christ. Hence also Paul affirms that all the Gentiles were "without God" and deprived of the hope of life. Now, since John teaches that there was life in Christ from the beginning, and that the whole world had lost it (John 1:4), it is necessary to return to that fountain. And accordingly Christ declares that inasmuch as he is the one who makes propitiation, he is life. And indeed, the inheritance of heaven belongs to none but the sons of God (John 15:6). Now, it would be most incongruous to give the place and rank of sons to any who have not been engrafted into the body of the only begotten Son. And John plainly testifies that those who believe in his name become the sons of God. But since it is not my intention at present to discuss the subject of faith in Christ formally, it is enough to have touched on it in passing in this way.

2¶

This is why God never showed himself favorable to his ancient people, nor gave them any hope of grace, apart from a Mediator. I say nothing of the sacrifices of the Law, by which believers were plainly and openly taught that salvation was not to be found anywhere but in the atonement that Christ alone accomplished. All I maintain is that the prosperous and happy state of the Church was always founded on the person of Christ. For although God embraced the whole posterity of Abraham in his covenant, Paul rightly argues (Galatians 3:16) that Christ was truly the seed in whom all the nations of the earth were to be blessed, since we know that not all who were born of Abraham according to the flesh were counted as the seed. To pass over Ishmael and others, how did it come about that of the two sons of Isaac, the twin brothers Esau and Jacob, while they were still in the womb, the one was chosen and the other rejected? Indeed, how did it come about that the firstborn was rejected and the younger alone admitted? Moreover, how does it happen that the majority are rejected? It is plain, therefore, that the seed of Abraham is considered chiefly in one head, and that the promised salvation is not attained without coming to Christ, whose office it is to gather together those who were scattered abroad. Thus the original adoption of the chosen people depended on the grace of the Mediator. Although it is not expressed in very distinct terms in Moses, it nevertheless appears to have been commonly known to all the godly. For before a king was appointed over the Israelites, Hannah, the mother of Samuel, describing the happiness of the righteous, speaks this way in her song: "He shall give strength unto his king, and exalt the horn of his anointed," meaning by these words that God would bless his Church. To this corresponds the prediction added afterward: "I will raise me up a faithful priest, and he shall walk before mine anointed for ever" (1 Samuel 2:10, 35). And there can be no doubt that our heavenly Father intended that a living image of Christ should be seen in David and his posterity. Accordingly, exhorting the righteous to fear Him, he bids them "Kiss the Son" (Psalm 2:12). Corresponding to this is the passage in the Gospel: "He that honoureth not the Son, honoureth not the Father" (John 5:23). Therefore, although the kingdom was broken up by the revolt of the ten tribes, the covenant God had made in David and his successors had to stand, as is also declared by his Prophets: "Howbeit I will not take the whole kingdom out of his hand: but I will make him prince all the days of his life for David my servant's sake" (1 Kings 11:34). The same thing is repeated a second and a third time. It is also expressly said, "I will for this afflict the seed of David, but not for ever" (1 Kings 11:39). Some time later it was said, "Nevertheless, for David's sake did the Lord his God give him a lamp in Jerusalem, to set up his son after him, and to establish Jerusalem" (1 Kings 15:4). And when matters were on the brink of destruction, it was said again, "Yet the Lord would not destroy Judah for David his servant's sake, as he had promised to give him alway a light, and to his children" (2 Kings 8:19).

The sum of the whole comes to this: David, all others being excluded, was chosen to be the person in whom the good pleasure of the Lord should rest; as it is said elsewhere, "He forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh"; "Moreover, he refused the tabernacle of Joseph, and chose not the tribe of Ephraim"; "But chose the tribe of Judah, the mount Zion which he loved"; "He chose David also his servant, and took him from the sheepfolds: from following the ewes great with young he brought him to feed Jacob his people, and Israel his inheritance" (Psalm 78:60, 67, 70, 71). In short, God, in preserving his Church in this way, intended that its security and salvation should depend on Christ as its head. Accordingly, David exclaims, "The Lord is their strength, and he is the saving strength of his anointed"; and then prays, "Save thy people, and bless thine inheritance," indicating that the safety of the Church was inseparably bound up with the government of Christ. In the same sense he says elsewhere, "Save, Lord: let the king hear us when we call" (Psalm 20:9). These words plainly teach that believers, in seeking the help of God, placed their sole confidence in this — that they were under the unseen government of the King. This may be inferred from another psalm: "Save now, I beseech thee, O Lord: Blessed be he that cometh in the name of the Lord" (Psalm 118:25, 26). Here it is obvious that believers are invited to Christ, in the assurance that they will be safe when they are entirely in his hand. To the same effect is another prayer, in which the whole Church implores the divine mercy: "Let thy hand be upon the Man of thy right hand, upon the Son of man, whom thou madest strong (or best fitted) for thyself" (Psalm 80:17). For although the author of the psalm laments the scattering of the whole nation, he prays for its revival in him who is its sole Head. After the people had been led away into captivity, the land laid waste, and matters to all appearance hopeless, Jeremiah, lamenting the calamity of the Church, complains especially that by the destruction of the kingdom the hope of believers was cut off: "The breath of our nostrils, the anointed of the Lord, was taken in their pits, of whom we said, Under his shadow we shall live among the heathen" (Lamentations 4:20). From all this it is abundantly plain that, since the Lord cannot be favorable to the human race without a Mediator, Christ was always held out to the holy Fathers under the Law as the object of their faith.

3¶

Moreover, when comfort is promised in affliction, and especially when the deliverance of the Church is described, the banner of faith and hope in Christ is unfurled. "Thou wentest forth for the salvation of thy people, even for salvation with thine anointed," says Habakkuk (Habakkuk 3:13). And whenever the Prophets mention the renewal of the Church, the people are directed to the promise made to David that his kingdom would endure for ever. And there is nothing strange in this, since otherwise there would have been no stability in the covenant. To this purpose is the remarkable prophecy in Isaiah 7:14. After seeing that the unbelieving king Ahab rejected what he had testified concerning the deliverance of Jerusalem from siege and its immediate safety, he passes, as it were abruptly, to the Messiah: "Behold, a virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and shall call his name Emmanuel," indicating indirectly that although the king and his people wickedly rejected the promise offered to them, as if they were determined to make the faithfulness of God fail, the covenant would not be defeated — the Redeemer would come in his own time. In short, all the prophets, to show that God could be appeased, were always careful to bring forward that kingdom of David on which redemption and eternal salvation depended. Thus it is said in Isaiah, "I will make an everlasting covenant with you, even the sure mercies of David. Behold, I have given him for a witness to the people" (Isaiah 55:3, 4), indicating that believers in times of calamity could have no hope if they did not have this testimony that God would be ready to hear them. In the same way, to revive their drooping spirits, Jeremiah says, "Behold, the days come, saith the Lord, that I will raise unto David a righteous Branch, and a King shall reign and prosper, and shall execute judgment and justice in the earth. In his days Judah shall be saved, and Israel shall dwell safely" (Jeremiah 23:5, 6). In Ezekiel also it is said, "I will set up one Shepherd over them, and he shall feed them, even my servant David; he shall feed them, and he shall be their shepherd. And I the Lord will be their God, and my servant David a prince among them: I the Lord have spoken it. And I will make with them a covenant of peace" (Ezekiel 34:23, 24, 25). And again, after speaking of this wonderful renewal, he says, "David my servant shall be king over them: and they all shall have one shepherd." "Moreover, I will make a covenant of peace with them; it shall be an everlasting covenant with them" (Ezekiel 37:24–26). I select a few passages out of many, because I merely wish to impress on my readers the fact that the hope of believers was always stored up in Christ alone. All the other prophets agree in this. Thus Hosea: "Then shall the children of Judah and the children of Israel be gathered together, and appoint themselves one head" (Hosea 1:11). He afterward explains this in clearer terms: "Afterward shall the children of Israel return, and seek the Lord their God, and David their king" (Hosea 3:5). Micah also, speaking of the return of the people, says expressly, "Their king shall pass before them, and the Lord on the head of them" (Micah 2:13). So Amos, in predicting the renewal of the people, says, "In that day will I raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen, and close up the breaches thereof; and I will raise up the ruins, and I will build it as in the days of old" (Amos 9:11); in other words, the only banner of salvation was the exaltation of the family of David to royal splendor, as fulfilled in Christ. Hence, too, Zechariah, being nearer in time to the manifestation of Christ, speaks more plainly: "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion; shout, O daughter of Jerusalem: behold, thy King cometh unto thee: he is just, and having salvation" (Zechariah 9:9). This corresponds to the passage already quoted from the Psalms: "The Lord is their strength, and he is the saving health of their anointed." Here salvation is extended from the head to the whole body.

4¶

By making the Jews familiar with these prophecies, God intended to teach them that in seeking deliverance they should turn their eyes directly toward Christ. And although they had sadly degenerated, they never entirely lost the knowledge of this general principle: that God, by the hand of Christ, would be the deliverer of the Church, as he had promised to David, and that only in this way would the free covenant by which God had adopted his chosen people be fulfilled. This is why, when our Savior entered Jerusalem shortly before his death, the children shouted, "Hosannah to the son of David" (Matthew 21:9). For there seems to have been a hymn known to all and in general use, in which they sang that the only remaining pledge they had of the divine mercy was the promised coming of a Redeemer. For this reason Christ tells his disciples to believe in him, so that they might have a distinct and complete belief in God: "Ye believe in God, believe also in me" (John 14:1). For although, strictly speaking, faith rises from Christ to the Father, he indicates that even when it rests on God it gradually fades away unless he himself steps in to give it solid strength. The majesty of God is too high for mortals to climb up to, who creep like worms on the earth. Therefore the common saying that God is the object of faith (Lactantius, book 4, chapter 16) needs to be received with some qualification. When Christ is called the image of the invisible God (Colossians 1:15), the expression is not used without reason, but is meant to remind us that we can have no knowledge of our salvation until we see God in Christ. For although the Jewish scribes had darkened by their false interpretations what the Prophets had taught concerning the Redeemer, Christ nevertheless took it as a fact received, as it were, by public consent, that there was no other remedy in desperate circumstances, no other way of delivering the Church, than the manifestation of the Mediator. It is true that the fact Paul points to — namely, that Christ is the end of the Law (Romans 10:4) — was not as generally known as it ought to have been, though it is both true and plainly evident from the Law and the Prophets. I am not now, however, treating of faith, since we shall have a more fitting place for that elsewhere (Book 3, chapter 2); but what I wish to impress on my readers in this way is that the first step in godliness is to acknowledge that God is a Father, to defend, govern, and cherish us until he brings us to the eternal inheritance of his kingdom; that hence it is plain, as we observed a little earlier, that there is no knowledge of God without Christ; and that consequently, from the beginning of the world, Christ was held out to all the elect as the object of their faith and confidence. In this sense Irenæus says that the Father, who is boundless in himself, is bounded in the Son, because he has adapted himself to our capacity, lest our minds should be swallowed up by the immensity of his glory (Irenaeus, book 4, chapter 8). Fanatics, not paying attention to this, twist a useful thought into an impious dream (French, "reverie infernale").

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Having shown how thoroughly we are lost, Calvin turns to the only place redemption is found: Christ, the one Mediator held forth to the faithful in every age. The pious of old were never accepted apart from him; the sacrifices, the calling of Abraham, the throne of David all pointed forward to the same Redeemer the gospel now reveals. Knowledge of God as Creator, Calvin warns, avails nothing without faith in God as Redeemer — and this is the word our age most needs, for 2026 offers a thousand vague spiritualities that admire a distant Maker while refusing the crucified Son. There is no other name, no second door, no general reverence that saves apart from Christ. In the home, let every reading of Scripture, Old Testament and New alike, bend the children's eyes toward him. We do not seek salvation in our striving, our heritage, or our sincerity; we seek it where it has always been hidden and now shines openly — in Christ alone.
