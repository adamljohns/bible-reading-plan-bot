Book 3 · Chapter 22 · USMC modern English

This Doctrine Confirmed by Proofs From Scripture

The divisions of this chapter are,—I. A confirmation of the orthodox doctrine in opposition to two classes of individuals. This confirmation founded on a careful exposition of our Savior’s words, and passages in the writings of Paul, sec. 1-7. II. A refutation of some objections taken from ancient writers, Thomas Aquinas, and more modern writers, sec. 8-10. III. Of reprobation, which is founded entirely on the righteous will of God, sec. 11. 1. Some imagine that God elects or reprobates according to a foreknowledge of merit. Others make it a charge against God that he elects some and passes by others. Both refuted, 1. By invincible arguments; 2. By the testimony of Augustine. 2. Who are elected, when, in whom, to what, for what reason. 3. The reason is the good pleasure of God, which so reigns in election that no works, either past or future, are taken into consideration. This proved by notable declarations of one Savior and passages of Paul. 4. Proved by a striking discussion in the Epistle to the Romans. Its scope and method explained. The advocates of foreknowledge refuted by the Apostle, when he maintains that election is special and wholly of grace. 5. Evasion refuted. A summary and analysis of the Apostle’s discussion. 6. An exception, with three answers to it. The efficacy of gratuitous election extends only to believers, who are said to be elected according to foreknowledge. This foreknowledge or prescience is not speculative but active. 7. This proved from the words of Christ. Conclusion of the answer, and solution of the objection with regard to Judas. 8. An objection taken from the ancient fathers. Answer from Augustine, from Ambrose, as quoted by Augustine, and an invincible argument by an Apostle. Summary of this argument. 9. Objection from Thomas Aquinas. Answer. 10. Objection of more modern writers. Answers. Passages in which there is a semblance of contradiction reconciled. Why many called and few chosen. An objection founded on mutual consent between the word and faith. Solution confirmed by the words of Paul, Augustine, and Bernard. A clear declaration by our Savior. 11. The view to be taken of reprobation. It is founded on the righteous will of God.

1¶

Many people dispute all the positions we have laid down, and especially the free election of believers; yet this teaching cannot be overthrown. They commonly imagine that God distinguishes between people according to the merits he foresees each one will have. On this view, he gives the adoption of sons to those he foreknows will not be unworthy of his grace, and he dooms to destruction those whose dispositions he perceives will be prone to mischief and wickedness. In this way they insert foreknowledge as a kind of veil. By doing so they not only obscure election, but pretend to give it a different origin altogether. This is not merely the common opinion of ordinary people, for in every age it has had powerful supporters (see section 8). I freely admit this, so that no one will expect to do great damage to our cause simply by opposing it with famous names. The truth of God here is too certain to be shaken, and too clear to be overborne by human authority. There are others, however, who are neither skilled in Scripture nor entitled to any weight, who assault sound teaching with an insolence and dishonesty that cannot be tolerated. As the French edition puts it: there are some who, since they are not practiced in Scripture, deserve no credit or reputation, and yet are all the more bold and reckless in slandering the teaching they do not understand; and so their arrogance simply cannot be supported.

Holiness of life springs from election and is its aim. So that the proof may be complete, it is important to attend to each separate clause of that passage; for when they are joined together they leave no doubt. By giving them the name of elect, Paul makes it clear that he is addressing believers, as he indeed declares shortly after. It is therefore a complete distortion of the word to limit it to the age in which the gospel was first published. By saying that they were chosen before the foundation of the world, he takes away every reference to merit. For what basis of distinction could there be between persons who did not yet exist, and persons who were later to come into existence in Adam in the same way? But if they were chosen in Christ, it follows not only that each one was chosen on some ground outside himself, but also that some were placed on a different footing from others, since we see that not all are members of Christ. When he adds that they were chosen in order that they might be holy, the apostle openly refutes the error of those who derive election from foreknowledge, since he declares that whatever virtue appears in people is the result of election. Then, if a higher cause is sought, Paul answers that God so predestined, and predestined according to the good pleasure of his will. With these words he overturns all the grounds of election that people imagine to exist within themselves. For he shows that whatever favors God grants in regard to the spiritual life flow from this one fountain: God chose whomever he willed, and before they were born he had set apart the grace he intended to give them, reserved for their use.

3¶

Wherever this good pleasure of God reigns, no good works are taken into account. The apostle does not, in fact, spell out the contrast here, but it is to be understood, just as he himself explains it in another place: God “has called us with a holy calling, not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace, which was given us in Christ Jesus before the world began” (2 Tim. 1:9). We have already shown that the added words, “that we might be holy,” remove every doubt. If you say that he foresaw they would be holy, and therefore chose them, you reverse Paul’s order. You may therefore safely conclude: if he chose us so that we might be holy, then he did not choose us because he foresaw that we would be holy. The two ideas are plainly inconsistent—namely, that the godly owe their holiness to election, and yet that they reach election by means of their works. There is no force in the trick they keep returning to, that the Lord does not grant election as a reward for past merits, but grants it in view of future ones. For when it is said that believers were chosen so that they might be holy, it is at the same time implied that the holiness that was to be in them takes its origin from election. And how can it be consistently said that things derived from election are the cause of election? Paul seems afterward to confirm the very thing he had said by adding, “according to his good pleasure which he has purposed in himself” (Eph. 1:9). For to say that God “purposed in himself” is the same as saying that in forming his decree he considered nothing outside himself. And so it is immediately added that the whole aim contemplated in our election is that “we should be to the praise of his glory.” Surely the grace of God would not deserve all the praise for election if election were not free; and it would not be free if, in choosing any individual, God paid regard to his future works. So what Christ said to his disciples proves to be universally true of all believers: “You have not chosen me, but I have chosen you” (John 15:16). Here he not only excludes past merits, but declares that they had nothing in themselves for which they could be chosen, except insofar as his mercy went before them. And how are we to understand Paul’s words, “Who has first given to him, and it shall be repaid to him again?” (Rom. 11:35). His meaning is clearly this: people are wholly indebted to the goodness of God that comes before them, since there is nothing in them, past or future, to win his favor.

4¶

In the Epistle to the Romans (Rom. 9:6), where he again takes up this subject in greater depth and at greater length, Paul declares that “they are not all Israel who are of Israel.” For though all were blessed in respect of their hereditary rights, not all equally obtained the inheritance. The whole discussion was prompted by the pride and boasting of the Jews, who, by claiming the name of the church for themselves, would have made the faith of the gospel depend on their own pleasure—just as today the Roman party would gladly use this same pretext to put themselves in the place of God. Paul, while granting that as far as the covenant was concerned they were the holy offspring of Abraham, nevertheless argues that the greater part of them were strangers to it. He argues this not only because they were degenerate and so had become illegitimate children instead of true sons, but because the main point to be considered was the special election of God, by which alone his adoption was ratified. If the godliness of some had established them in the hope of salvation, and the rebellion of others had been the sole cause of their rejection, it would have been foolish and pointless for Paul to lead his readers back to a secret election. But if the will of God—for which no cause outside himself either appears or is to be sought—distinguishes some from others, so that not all the sons of Israel are true Israelites, then it is useless for anyone to seek the origin of his own condition within himself. Paul then pursues the subject further by contrasting the cases of Jacob and Esau. Both were sons of Abraham; both were in their mother’s womb at the same time. There was something very strange in the change by which the honor of the birthright was transferred to Jacob, and yet Paul declares that this change was a testimony to the election of the one and the rejection of the other.

The question under consideration is the origin and cause of election. Those who defend foreknowledge insist that it is to be found in people’s virtues and vices. They take the short and easy route of asserting that God showed, in the case of Jacob, that he chooses those who are worthy of his grace, and, in the case of Esau, that he rejects those he foresees to be unworthy. Such is their confident claim; but what does Paul say? “For the children being not yet born, neither having done any good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him who calls; it was said to her [Rebecca], The elder shall serve the younger. As it is written, Jacob have I loved, but Esau have I hated” (Rom. 9:11–13). If foreknowledge had anything to do with this distinction between the brothers, the mention of timing would have been pointless. Even granting that Jacob was chosen for a worth he would attain by future virtues, why did Paul say that he was not yet born? Nor would there have been any reason to add that he had as yet done no good, because the answer would always have been ready: that nothing is hidden from God, and that therefore Jacob’s godliness was already present before him. If works win favor, a value should have been placed on them before Jacob was born, just as if he had been fully grown. But in resolving the difficulty, the apostle goes on to show that Jacob’s adoption rested not on works but on the calling of God. As for works, he makes no mention of past or future, but distinctly sets them in opposition to the calling of God, indicating that when place is given to the one, the other is overthrown. It is as if he had said: the only thing to be considered is what pleased God, not what people supplied from themselves. Finally, it is certain that all the causes which people are accustomed to invent, outside the secret counsel of God, are excluded by the use of the terms purpose and election.

5¶

Why should people try to darken these statements by assigning some place in election to past or future works? This is entirely to evade what the apostle is contending for—namely, that the distinction between the brothers is not founded on any ground of works, but on the bare calling of God, since it was settled before the children were born. If there had been any soundness in this subtlety, it would not have escaped the apostle’s notice. But, being fully aware that God foresaw no good in a person except what he had already determined beforehand to give by means of his election, Paul does not adopt a backward arrangement that would make good works come before their cause. We learn from the apostle’s words that the salvation of believers rests entirely on the decree of divine election, and that this privilege is gained not by works but by free calling. We also have an example of the thing itself set before us. Esau and Jacob are brothers, born of the same parents, within the same womb, not yet born. In them all things are equal, and yet God’s judgment regarding them is different. He adopts the one and rejects the other. The only right of precedence was that of the firstborn; but that is set aside, and the younger is preferred to the elder. Indeed, in the case of others, God seems to have disregarded the right of the firstborn for the express purpose of cutting off all human ground for boasting. Rejecting Ishmael, he gives his favor to Isaac; setting Manasseh aside, he honors Ephraim.

6¶

Someone may object that these small and lesser favors do not let us decide anything about the future life—that we should not suppose that the one who received the honor of being firstborn was for that reason adopted into the inheritance of heaven. (Many objectors do not even spare Paul, but accuse him of twisting Scripture from its proper meaning in quoting these passages.) I answer, as before, that the apostle did not blunder through carelessness, nor deliberately misapply the passages of Scripture. Rather, he saw—what these people cannot be brought to consider—that God meant, under an earthly sign, to declare the spiritual election of Jacob, which otherwise lay hidden at his unapproachable judgment seat. For unless we refer the birthright given to Jacob to the world to come, the form of blessing would be utterly empty and absurd, since he gained nothing by it but a host of toils and troubles, exile, sharp sorrows, and bitter cares. Therefore, when Paul knew beyond doubt that through the outward sign God was declaring the spiritual and unfading blessings he had prepared for his servant in his kingdom, he did not hesitate to draw an argument for the latter from the former. For we must remember that the land of Canaan was given as a pledge of the heavenly inheritance; and therefore there can be no doubt that Jacob, like the angels, was grafted into the body of Christ, so that he might share the same life. Jacob, then, is chosen, while Esau is rejected; the predestination of God makes a distinction where none existed in respect of merit. If you ask the reason, the apostle gives it: “For he says to Moses, I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I will have compassion” (Rom. 9:15). And what, pray, does this mean? It is simply a clear declaration by the Lord that he finds nothing in people themselves to move him to show kindness; that it is owing entirely to his own mercy; and, accordingly, that their salvation is his own work. Since God places your salvation in himself alone, why should you turn down to yourself? Since he assigns it to his own mercy alone, why will you fall back on your own merits? Since he confines your thoughts to his own mercy, why do you turn partly to the view of your own works?

We must therefore come to that smaller number whom Paul elsewhere describes as foreknown by God (Rom. 11:2)—not foreknown, as these people imagine, by idle, inactive contemplation, but in the sense the word often carries. For surely when Peter says that Christ was “delivered by the determined counsel and foreknowledge of God” (Acts 2:23), he does not present God as merely contemplating, but as actually accomplishing our salvation. So too Peter, in saying that the believers to whom he writes are chosen “according to the foreknowledge of God” (1 Pet. 1:2), rightly expresses that secret predestination by which God has sealed those whom he has been pleased to adopt as sons. By using the term “purpose” as equivalent to a word that always means a fixed determination, he clearly shows that God, in being the author of our salvation, does not go beyond himself. In this sense he says in the same chapter that Christ, “as a lamb,” “was foreordained before the creation of the world” (1 Pet. 1:19–20). What could have been more lifeless or absurd than to picture God looking down from the height of heaven to see where the salvation of the human race was to come from? By “a people foreknown,” Peter means the same thing as Paul does by a remnant chosen out of a multitude that falsely takes the name of God. In another place, to crush the empty boasting of those who, while only wearing a mask, claim before the world a first place among the godly, Paul says, “The Lord knows those who are his” (2 Tim. 2:19). In short, by that term he marks out two classes of people: the one made up of the whole line of Abraham, the other a people set apart from that line, who, though hidden from human sight, are open to the eye of God. And there is no doubt that he took the passage from Moses, who declares that God would be merciful to whomever he pleased (although Moses was speaking of an elect people whose standing seemed equal). It is as if Paul had said that within a common adoption there was included a special grace which God grants to some as a holier treasure, and that there is nothing in the common covenant to keep this number from being set apart from the common order. God, being pleased in this matter to act as a free giver and disposer, plainly declares that the only ground on which he will show mercy to one rather than to another is his own sovereign pleasure. For when mercy is granted to the one who asks for it, even though God does not refuse him, the man still either anticipates the favor or partly earns it—whereas God claims the entire merit of it for himself.

7¶

Now let the supreme Judge and Master decide the whole case. Seeing such stubbornness in his hearers that his words fell upon the crowd almost without fruit, he removes this stumbling block by exclaiming, “All that the Father gives me shall come to me.” “And this is the Father’s will who has sent me, that of all which he has given me I should lose nothing” (John 6:37, 39). Notice that the Father’s gift is the first step in our being handed over to the keeping and protection of Christ. Someone here, perhaps, will turn the point around and object that only those belong in this special way to the Father who make a willing surrender by faith. But the one thing Christ maintains is this: that even if the desertion of vast crowds should shake the world, still the counsel of God would stand firm, more stable than heaven itself, and his election would never fail. The elect are said to have belonged to the Father before he handed them over to his only begotten Son. It is asked: were they his by nature? No—they were strangers, but he makes them his by handing them over. The words of Christ are too clear to be made obscure by any of the fog of quibbling: “No one can come to me unless the Father who has sent me draws him.” “Everyone, therefore, who has heard and learned from the Father comes to me” (John 6:44–45). If all without distinction bowed the knee to Christ, election would be common to everyone; but as it is, in the small number of believers a plain difference appears. Accordingly our Savior, shortly after declaring that the disciples given to him were the shared possession of the Father, adds, “I pray not for the world, but for those whom you have given me, for they are yours” (John 17:9). This is why the whole world no longer belongs to its Creator, except insofar as grace rescues from the curse, from divine wrath, and from eternal death some—not many—who would otherwise perish, while he leaves the world to the destruction to which it is doomed. Meanwhile, although Christ steps in as Mediator, he still claims the right of choosing together with the Father: “I speak not of you all: I know whom I have chosen” (John 13:18). And if it is asked from where he has chosen them, he answers in another place, “Out of the world”—which he shuts out of his prayers when he commits his disciples to the Father (John 15:19). We must indeed hold, when he affirms that he knows whom he has chosen, first, that certain individuals of the human race are meant, and second, that they are marked out not by the quality of their virtues but by a heavenly decree. From this it follows that, since Christ makes himself the author of election, no one excels by his own strength or effort. When elsewhere he numbers Judas among the elect, though he was a devil (John 6:70), he is referring only to the apostolic office—which, though a bright display of divine favor (as Paul so often acknowledges it to be in his own case), does not in itself contain the hope of eternal salvation. Judas, therefore, when he carried out the office of apostle treacherously, may have been worse than a devil; but Christ will never let even one of those whom he has once grafted into his body perish. For in securing their salvation he will do what he has promised, exerting a divine power greater than all (John 10:28). When he says, “Those whom you gave me I have kept, and none of them is lost but the son of perdition” (John 17:12), the expression—though it stretches the word—is not at all unclear. The sum of it is this: God, by free adoption, forms into sons those whom he wishes to have as sons; and the inner cause lies in himself, because he is satisfied with his own secret pleasure.

8¶

But Ambrose, Origen, and Jerome held the opinion that God dispenses his grace among people according to the use he foresees each will make of it. It may be added that Augustine also held this opinion for a time; but after he had advanced further in the knowledge of Scripture, he not only retracted it as plainly false, but powerfully refuted it (Augustine, Retractations, Book 1, ch. 13). Indeed, even after the retraction, glancing at the Pelagians who still clung to that error, he says: “Who does not wonder that the apostle failed to make this most acute observation? For after stating a most startling proposition about those not yet born, and then raising the question to himself as an objection—‘What then? Is there unrighteousness with God?’—he had an opening to answer that God foresaw the merits of both. Yet he does not say so, but takes refuge in the justice and mercy of God” (Augustine, Letter 106, to Sixtus). And in another place, after ruling out all merit before election, he says: “Here, surely, there is no room for the empty argument of those who defend the foreknowledge of God against the grace of God, and who therefore maintain that we were chosen before the foundation of the world because God foreknew that we would be good, not that he himself would make us good. This is not the language of the one who says, ‘You have not chosen me, but I have chosen you’ (John 15:16). For if he had chosen us because he foreknew that we would be good, he would at the same time also have foreknown that we would choose him” (Augustine, on John, Tractate 8; see also what follows to the same effect). Let the testimony of Augustine carry weight with those who gladly rest in the authority of the Fathers—though Augustine himself does not allow that he differs from the rest, that is, from the other ancient teachers.

9¶

Nor let us be held back by the subtlety of Thomas Aquinas, that the foreknowledge of merit is the cause of predestination—not, indeed, in respect of the act of predestinating, but in such a way that on our side it may in some sense be called a cause, namely, in respect of a particular way of assessing predestination. So, on his view, it is said that God predestines a person to glory according to his merit, in that he decreed to give him the grace by which he merits glory. But while the Lord would have us see nothing more in election than his sheer goodness, for anyone to want to see more is a misguided affectation. Yet if we were to make a test of subtlety, it would not be hard to refute the sophistry of Thomas. He maintains that the elect are in a manner predestined to glory on account of their merits, because God predestines to give them the grace by which they merit glory. What if I were to object, on the contrary, that predestination to grace serves election to life and follows it as its handmaid—that grace is predestined for those to whom the possession of glory was previously assigned, since the Lord is pleased to bring his sons by election to justification? For it will then follow that predestination to glory is the cause of predestination to grace, and not the other way around. But let us be done with these disputes as needless among those who think there is enough wisdom for them in the word of God. For it was truly said by an old church writer that those who attribute the election of God to merits are wiser than they ought to be (Ambrose, On the Calling of the Gentiles, Book 1, ch. 2).

10¶

Some object that God would be inconsistent with himself, in inviting all without distinction while choosing only a few. On their view, then, the universal scope of the promise destroys the distinction of special grace. Some moderate people speak this way, not so much to suppress the truth as to escape puzzling questions and rein in excessive curiosity. The intention is praiseworthy, but the approach is by no means to be approved, since pretense is never excusable. In others, however, who put their insolence on display, we see only a worthless quibble or a shameful error. The way Scripture reconciles the two things—namely, that by outward preaching all are called to faith and repentance, and yet that the Spirit of faith and repentance is not given to all—I have already explained, and will repeat again shortly. But the very point they assume I deny as false in two respects. For the One who warns that when it rains on one city there will be drought in another (Amos 4:7), and who declares in another place that there will be a famine of his word (Amos 8:11), does not lay himself under a fixed obligation to call all equally. And the One who, forbidding Paul to preach in Asia and leading him away from Bithynia, carries him over into Macedonia (Acts 16:6), shows that it belongs to him to distribute the treasure however he pleases. But it is through Isaiah that he more clearly shows how he sets apart the promises of salvation especially for the elect (Isa. 8:16); for he declares that his disciples would consist of them alone, and not of the whole human race indiscriminately. From this it is plain that the teaching of salvation, which is said to be set apart for the sons of the church alone, is abused when it is represented as effective for all. For now let it be enough to observe that, although the word of the gospel is addressed generally to all, the gift of faith is rare. Isaiah assigns the cause when he says that the arm of the Lord is not revealed to all (Isa. 53:1). Had he said that the gospel is maliciously and perversely rejected because many stubbornly refuse to hear, there might perhaps be some pretext for this universal call. But it is not the prophet’s aim to lessen the guilt of people when he states that the source of their blindness is that God does not deign to reveal his arm to them; he only reminds us that, since faith is a special gift, it is useless for outward teaching to sound in the ear. But I would gladly learn from these teachers whether it is mere preaching, or faith, that makes people sons of God. Surely, when it is said, “As many as received him, to them he gave power to become the sons of God, even to those who believe on his name” (John 1:12), no jumbled mass is set before us, but a special order is assigned to believers, who are “born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God.”

But it is objected that there is a mutual agreement between faith and the word. That must hold wherever there is faith. But it is nothing new for the seed to fall among thorns or on stony ground—not only because the majority in fact appear to be rebels against God, but because not all are given eyes and ears. How, then, can it consistently be said that God calls while knowing that those called will not come? Let Augustine answer for me: “Would you dispute with me? Then wonder with me, and cry out, O the depth! Let us both agree in awe, lest we perish in error” (Augustine, On the Words of the Apostle, Sermon 11). Moreover, if election is, as Paul declares, the parent of faith, I turn the argument around and maintain that faith is not universal, since election is special. For it is easily inferred from the chain of causes and effects, when Paul says that the Father “has blessed us with all spiritual blessings in heavenly places in Christ, just as he has chosen us in him before the foundation of the world” (Eph. 1:3–4), that these riches are not common to all, because God has chosen only whom he willed. And the reason he commends, in another place, “the faith of the elect” is to keep anyone from supposing that he acquires faith by his own nature; for to God alone belongs the glory of freely enlightening those whom he had previously chosen (Titus 1:1). For it is well said by Bernard: “His friends he hears apart when he says to them, Fear not, little flock: to you it is given to know the mysteries of the kingdom. Who are these? Those whom he foreknew and predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son. He has made known his great and secret counsel. The Lord knows those who are his, but what was known to God has been made manifest to people; nor, indeed, does he deign to give a share in this great mystery to any except those whom he foreknew and predestined to be his own” (Bernard, Letter 107). Shortly after, he concludes: “The mercy of the Lord is from everlasting to everlasting upon those who fear him; from everlasting through predestination, to everlasting through glorification: the one knows no beginning, the other no end.” But why cite Bernard as a witness, when we hear from the lips of our Master, “Not that anyone has seen the Father, except he who is from God” (John 6:46)? By these words he indicates that all who are not born anew by God are dazzled by the brightness of his face. And indeed faith is fittingly joined with election, provided it holds the second place. This order is clearly expressed by our Savior in these words: “This is the will of the Father who has sent me, that of all which he has given me I should lose nothing”; “And this is the will of him who sent me, that everyone who sees the Son and believes on him may have everlasting life” (John 6:39–40). If he would have all to be saved, he would appoint his Son their guardian, and would graft them all into his body by the sacred bond of faith. So it is now clear that faith is a singular pledge of fatherly love, stored up for the sons whom he has adopted. This is why Christ elsewhere says that the sheep follow the shepherd because they know his voice, but that they will not follow a stranger, because they do not know the voice of strangers (John 10:4). But where does that difference come from, unless their ears have been opened by God? For no one makes himself a sheep; he is shaped by heavenly grace. And why does the Lord declare that our salvation will always be sure and certain? Simply because it is guarded by the unconquerable power of God (John 10:29). Accordingly, he concludes that unbelievers are not of his sheep (John 10:16). The reason is that they are not among the number of those who, as the Lord promised through Isaiah, were to be his disciples. Moreover, since the passages I have quoted imply perseverance, they are also witnesses to the unbending constancy of election.

11¶

We come now to the reprobate, to whom the apostle refers at the same point (Rom. 9:13). For just as Jacob, who as yet had earned nothing by good works, is received into favor, so Esau, while as yet stained by no crime, is hated. If we turn our gaze to works, we do an injustice to the apostle, as though he had failed to see the very thing that is clear to us. And there is full proof that he did not fail to see it, since he expressly insists that when they had as yet done neither good nor evil, the one was chosen and the other rejected, in order to prove that the foundation of divine predestination is not in works. Then, after raising the objection, “Is God unjust?”—instead of using what would have been the surest and plainest defense of God’s justice, namely, that God had repaid Esau according to his wickedness—he is content with a different solution: that the reprobate are expressly raised up so that the glory of God may be displayed through them. At last he concludes that God has mercy on whom he will have mercy, and whom he will, he hardens (Rom. 9:18). You see how he traces both back to the mere pleasure of God. Therefore, if we can assign no reason for his granting mercy to his people except that it so pleases him, neither can we have any reason for his rejecting others except his will. When God is said to visit in mercy, or to harden whom he will, people are reminded that they are not to look for any cause beyond his will.

Here Calvin marshals Scripture itself to silence the ancient evasion — that God elects those he foresees will be worthy. Paul leaves no room: Jacob and Esau, not yet born, neither having done good nor evil, the one loved and the other passed by, that the purpose of God might stand not of works but of him who calls. Election is the parent of faith, not its child; we are chosen that we might be holy, never chosen because we would be. In 2026 this is no dry abstraction for the lecture hall — it is the steel beneath a believer's assurance. When the accuser whispers that your standing rests on the unevenness of your obedience, return to the fountain: God purposed your salvation in himself, considering nothing external to his own good pleasure. Let no household trade this granite confidence for the sand of self-reckoning. Teach your sons and daughters that grace which depended on foreseen merit would be no grace at all — and that the Lord, blessedly, owes us nothing and gives us himself.
