Book 1 · Chapter 16 · USMC modern English

The World, Created by God, Still Cherished and Protected by Him — Each and All of Its Parts Governed by His Providence

The divisions of this chapter are, I. The doctrine of the special providence of God over all the creatures, singly and collectively, as opposed to the dreams of the Epicureans about fortune and fortuitous causes. II. The fiction of the Sophists concerning the omnipotence of God, and the error of philosophers, as to a confused and equivocal government of the world, sec. 1-5. All animals, but especially mankind, from the peculiar superintendence exercised over them, are proofs, evidences, and examples of the providence of God, sec. 6, 7. III. A consideration of fate, fortune, chance, contingence, and uncertain events (on which the matter here under discussion turns).

  1. 1. Even the wicked, under the guidance of carnal sense, acknowledge that God is the Creator. The godly acknowledge not this only, but that he is a most wise and powerful governor and preserver of all created objects.   2. 2. Refutation of the Epicureans, who oppose fortune and fortuitous causes to Divine Providence, as taught in Scripture. The sun, a bright manifestation of Divine Providence.
  3. 3. Figment of the Sophists as to an indolent Providence refuted. Consideration of the Omnipotence as combined with the Providence of God. Double benefit resulting from a proper acknowledgement of the Divine Omnipotence. Cavils of Infidelity.
  4. 4. A definition of Providence refuting the erroneous dogmas of Philosophers. Dreams of the Epicureans and Peripatetics.
  5. 5. Special Providence of God asserted and proved by arguments founded on a consideration of the Divine Justice and Mercy. Proved also by passages of Scripture, relating to the sky, the earth, and animals.
  6. 6. Special Providence proved by passages relating to the human race, and the more especially that for its sake the world was created.
  7. 7. Special Providence proved, lastly, from examples taken from the history of the Israelites, of Jonah, Jacob, and from daily experience.
  8. 8. Erroneous views as to Providence refuted:—I. The sect of the Stoics. II. The fortune and chance of the Heathen.
  9. 9. How things are said to be fortuitous to us, though done by the determinate counsel of God. Example. Error of separating contingency and event from the secret, but just, and most wise counsel of God. Two examples.
1¶

It would be cold and lifeless to represent God as a momentary Creator, who finished his work once for all and then left it. Here especially we must part company with the ungodly, and maintain that the presence of the divine power is conspicuous no less in the continuing state of the world than in its first creation. For although even wicked men are compelled, by the mere sight of the earth and sky, to rise to the Creator, yet faith has a way of its own of assigning the whole praise of creation to God. To this effect is the passage of the Apostle already quoted, that by faith we understand that the worlds were framed by the Word of God (Hebrews 11:3); for unless we go on to his Providence, we cannot understand the full force of what is meant by God being the Creator, however much we may seem to grasp it with our mind and confess it with our tongue. The carnal mind, once it has perceived the power of God in the creation, stops there; and at the very most it thinks and ponders on nothing else than the wisdom, power, and goodness displayed by the Author of such a work (things which arise of their own accord and force themselves on the notice even of the unwilling), or on some general activity, exercised in preserving and governing it, on which the power of motion depends. In short, it imagines that all things are sufficiently sustained by the energy divinely infused into them at the beginning. But faith must go deeper. After learning that there is a Creator, it must at once infer that he is also a Governor and Preserver — and that not by producing a kind of general motion in the machinery of the globe and in each of its parts, but by a special providence sustaining, cherishing, and overseeing all the things he has made, down to the very least, even to a sparrow. So David, after briefly stating that the world was created by God, immediately comes down to the continual course of Providence: "By the word of the Lord were the heavens framed, and all the host of them by the breath of his mouth"; and he immediately adds, "The Lord looketh from heaven, he beholdeth the children of men" (Psalm 33:6, 13, etc.). He adds other things to the same effect. For although not all reason so precisely, yet because it would not be credible that human affairs were overseen by God unless he were the maker of the world, and no one could seriously believe that he is its Creator without being convinced that he takes care of his works, David with good reason and in admirable order leads us from the one to the other. In general, indeed, philosophers teach, and the human mind conceives, that all the parts of the world are quickened by the secret inspiration of God. They do not, however, reach the height to which David rises, carrying all the godly with him, when he says: "These wait all upon you, that you may give them their meat in due season. That you give them they gather: you open your hand, they are filled with good. You hide your face, they are troubled: you take away their breath, they die, and return to their dust. You send forth your Spirit, they are created, and you renew the face of the earth" (Psalm 104:27-30). Indeed, though they subscribe to the saying of Paul, that in God "we live, and move, and have our being" (Acts 17:28), yet they are far from a serious grasp of the grace which he commends, because they have not the least taste for that special care in which alone the fatherly favor of God is discerned.

2¶

That this distinction may be the more evident, we must consider that the Providence of God, as taught in Scripture, is opposed to fortune and chance causes. Through a mistaken opinion prevailing in all ages, and almost universally prevailing in our own day — namely, that all things happen by chance — the true doctrine of Providence has been not only obscured but almost buried. If someone falls among robbers or ravenous beasts; if a sudden gust of wind at sea causes a shipwreck; if someone is struck down by the fall of a house or a tree; if another, wandering through desert paths, meets with deliverance, or, after being tossed by the waves, reaches port and makes some wondrous hair's-breadth escape from death — all these occurrences, prosperous as well as adverse, carnal sense will attribute to fortune. But whoever has learned from the mouth of Christ that all the hairs of his head are numbered (Matthew 10:30) will look further for the cause, and will hold that all events whatever are governed by the secret counsel of God. With regard to inanimate objects, again, we must hold that though each has its own particular properties, yet all of them exert their force only insofar as they are directed by the immediate hand of God. They are therefore merely instruments, into which God continually infuses whatever energy he sees fit, and which he turns and directs to any purpose at his pleasure. No created object makes a more wonderful or glorious display than the sun. For, besides lighting the whole world with its brightness, how admirably it nourishes and invigorates all living creatures by its heat, and makes the earth fruitful by its rays, warming the seeds of grain in its lap and so calling forth the green blade! This it supports, increases, and strengthens with further nourishment until it rises into the stalk; and it still feeds it with constant moisture until it comes into flower, and from flower to fruit, which it continues to ripen until it reaches maturity. In the same way, by its warmth trees and vines bud and put forth first their leaves, then their blossoms, then their fruit. And the Lord, so that he might claim the entire glory of these things as his own, was pleased that light should exist, and that the earth should be stocked with all kinds of plants and fruits, before he made the sun. No godly man, therefore, will make the sun either the necessary or the principal cause of those things which existed before the sun was created, but only the instrument which God uses because he so pleases, though he can lay it aside and act just as well by himself. Again, when we read that at the prayer of Joshua the sun was stopped in its course (Joshua 10:13), and that as a favor to Hezekiah its shadow went back ten degrees (2 Kings 20:11), by these miracles God declared that the sun does not rise and set daily by a blind instinct of nature, but is governed by him in its course, so that he may renew the remembrance of his fatherly favor toward us. Nothing is more natural than for spring to follow winter in its turn, summer spring, and autumn summer; yet in this sequence the variations are so great and so unequal as to make it very plain that every single year, month, and day is regulated by a new and special providence of God.

3¶

And truly God claims omnipotence for himself, and would have us acknowledge it — not the empty, idle, slumbering omnipotence which the sophists imagine, but a watchful, effective, energetic, and ever-active omnipotence; not an omnipotence which acts only as a general principle of confused motion, as in ordering a stream to keep within the channel once prescribed for it, but one which is directed to individual and particular movements. God is considered omnipotent not because he is able to act, yet may cease or be idle, nor because by a general instinct he continues the order of nature previously appointed, but because, governing heaven and earth by his providence, he so overrules all things that nothing happens without his counsel. For when it is said in the Psalms, "He has done whatsoever he has pleased" (Psalm 115:3), what is meant is his sure and deliberate purpose. It would be tasteless to interpret the Psalmist's words in the manner of the philosophers, as meaning that God is the first agent because he is the beginning and cause of all motion. Rather, this is the comfort of the faithful in their adversity: that everything they endure comes by the ordinance and command of God, and that they are under his hand. But if the government of God extends in this way to all his works, it is a childish quibble to confine it to natural influx. See Hyperius in Methodo Theologiæ.

4¶

First, then, let the reader remember that the providence we mean is not one by which the Deity sits idly in heaven and looks on at what is taking place in the world, but one by which he, as it were, holds the helm and overrules all events. Hence his providence extends no less to the hand than to the eye. The French adds, "Cest à dire, que non seulement il voit, mais aussi ordonne ce qu'il veut estra fait;" — "that is to say, he not only sees, but ordains what he wills to be done."

Pliny, book 2, chapter 7. "Irridendum vero, agere curam rerum humanarum, illud, quicquid est sumum. Anne tam tristi atque multiplici ministerio non pollui credamus dubitemusve?"

My intention now is to refute an opinion that has gained very wide acceptance — an opinion which, while it grants God some blind and ambiguous movement, withholds what is of chief importance, namely, the disposing and directing of everything to its proper end by incomprehensible wisdom. By withholding government, it makes God the ruler of the world in name only, not in reality. For what, I ask, is meant by government, if it is not to preside in such a way as to determine the destiny of what you preside over? I do not, however, entirely reject what is said of a universal providence, provided, on the other hand, that it is conceded to me that the world is governed by God, not only because he maintains the order of nature he appointed, but because he takes special charge of every one of his works. It is true, indeed, that each kind of created thing is moved by a secret instinct of nature, as if they obeyed the eternal command of God and of their own accord followed the course which God at first appointed. And to this we may refer our Savior's words, that he and his Father have always been at work from the beginning (John 5:17); also the words of Paul, that "in him we live, and move, and have our being" (Acts 17:28); and also the words of the author of the Epistle to the Hebrews, who, wishing to prove the divinity of Christ, says that he upholds "all things by the word of his power" (Hebrews 1:3). But some, under the pretext of the general providence, hide and obscure the special providence, which is taught in Scripture so surely and clearly that it is strange how anyone can bring himself to doubt it. And indeed, those who put forward that disguise are themselves forced to qualify their doctrine by adding that many things are done by the special care of God. This, however, they mistakenly confine to particular acts. What must be proved, therefore, is that single events are so regulated by God, and all events so proceed from his determinate counsel, that nothing happens by chance.

5¶

Suppose that the beginning of motion belongs to God, but that all things move of themselves or by chance, according to the impulse which nature gives: then the alternations of day and night, summer and winter, will be the work of God, inasmuch as he, in assigning each its office, appointed a fixed law, namely, that they should always keep the same course with uniform regularity, day following night, month following month, and year following year. But when at one time excessive heat combined with drought burns up the fields, and at another excessive rains rot the crops, while sudden devastation is caused by tempests and hailstorms, these will not be the works of God, except insofar as rainy or fair weather, heat or cold, are produced by the conjunction of the stars and other natural causes. On this view there is no place left either for the fatherly favor or for the judgments of God. If it is said that God fully shows his kindness to the human race by furnishing heaven and earth with the ordinary power of producing food, the explanation is meager and heathenish — as if the fruitfulness of one year were not a special blessing, and the scarcity and dearth of another a special punishment and curse from God. But since it would take too much time to list all the arguments, let the authority of God himself suffice. In the Law and the Prophets he repeatedly declares that as often as he waters the earth with dew and rain he shows his favor; that by his command the heaven becomes hard as iron, and the crops are destroyed by mildew and other plagues; and that storms and hail, in laying waste the fields, are signs of sure and special vengeance. Once this is admitted, it is certain that not a drop of rain falls without the express command of God. David, indeed (Psalm 146:9), extols the general providence of God in supplying food to the young ravens that cry to him; but when God himself threatens living creatures with famine, does he not plainly declare that they are all fed by him, at one time with scanty measure, at another with more ample? It is childish, as I have already said, to confine this to particular acts, when Christ says without reservation that not a sparrow falls to the ground without the will of his Father (Matthew 10:29). Surely, if the flight of birds is regulated by the counsel of God, we must acknowledge with the prophet that while he "dwelleth on high," he "humbleth himself to behold the things that are in heaven and in the earth" (Psalm 113:5, 6).

6¶

But since we know that it was chiefly for the sake of mankind that the world was made, we must look to this as the end God has in view in governing it. The prophet Jeremiah exclaims, "O Lord, I know that the way of man is not in himself: it is not in man that walketh to direct his steps" (Jeremiah 10:23). Solomon again says, "Man's goings are of the Lord: how can a man then understand his own way?" (Proverbs 20:24). Will it now be said that man is moved by God according to the bent of his nature, but that man himself gives the movement whatever direction he pleases? If that were truly so, man would have full control of his own ways. To this it will perhaps be answered that man can do nothing without the power of God. But the answer will not do, since both Jeremiah and Solomon attribute to God not power only, but also election and decree. And Solomon, in another place, elegantly rebukes the rashness of men who fix their plans without reference to God, as if they were not led by his hand: "The preparations of the heart in man, and the answer of the tongue, is from the Lord" (Proverbs 16:1). It is surely a strange madness for miserable men, who cannot even speak except insofar as God pleases, to set about acting without him! Scripture, moreover, the better to show that everything done in the world is according to his decree, declares that the things which seem most a matter of chance are subject to him. For what seems more attributable to chance than a branch that falls from a tree and kills a passing traveler? But the Lord sees it very differently, and declares that he delivered him into the hand of the slayer (Exodus 21:13). In the same way, who does not attribute the lot to the blindness of Fortune? Not so the Lord, who claims the decision for himself (Proverbs 16:33). He does not say that by his power the lot is thrown into the lap and drawn out, but declares that the one thing which could be attributed to chance comes from him. To the same effect are the words of Solomon, "The poor and the deceitful man meet together; the Lord lighteneth both their eyes" (Proverbs 29:13). For although rich and poor are mingled together in the world, in saying that the condition of each is divinely appointed, he reminds us that God, who enlightens all, always has his own eye open; and so he exhorts the poor to patient endurance, since those who are discontented with their lot are trying to throw off a burden which God has laid on them. So too another prophet rebukes the ungodly, who ascribe it to human effort or to fortune that some grovel in the mire while others rise to honor: "Promotion cometh neither from the east, nor from the west, nor from the south. But God is the judge: he putteth down one, and setteth up another" (Psalm 75:6, 7). Because God cannot lay aside the office of judge, he infers that it is owing to God's secret counsel that some are raised up while others remain without honor.

7¶

Indeed, I affirm in general that particular events are evidences of the special providence of God. In the wilderness God caused a south wind to blow and brought the people a plentiful supply of birds (Exodus 19:13). When he willed that Jonah should be thrown into the sea, he sent out a whirlwind. Those who deny that God holds the reins of government will say that this was contrary to his ordinary practice; but I infer from it that no wind ever rises or rages without his special command. In no way could it be true that "he maketh the winds his messengers, and the flames of fire his ministers," and that "he maketh the clouds his chariot, and walketh upon the wings of the wind" (Psalm 104:3, 4), if he did not drive the clouds and winds at his pleasure and in them display the special presence of his power. In the same way we are taught elsewhere that whenever the sea is stirred into a storm, its waves bear witness to the special presence of God: "He commandeth and raiseth the stormy wind, which lifteth up the waves." "He maketh the storm a calm, so that the waves thereof are still" (Psalm 107:25, 29). He also declares elsewhere that he had struck the people with blasting and mildew (Amos 4:9). Again, while man naturally possesses the power of continuing his kind, God describes it as a mark of his special favor that some remain childless while others are blessed with offspring; for the fruit of the womb is his gift. Hence the words of Jacob to Rachel: "Am I in God's stead, who has withheld from you the fruit of the womb?" (Genesis 30:2). To conclude in a word: nothing in nature is more ordinary than that we should be nourished by bread. Yet the Spirit declares not only that the produce of the earth is God's special gift, but "that man does not live by bread only" (Deuteronomy 8:3), because it is not mere fullness that nourishes him, but the secret blessing of God. And so, on the other hand, he threatens to take away "the stay and the staff, the whole stay of bread, and the whole stay of water" (Isaiah 3:1). Indeed, there could be no serious meaning in our prayer for daily bread if God did not supply us with food by a fatherly hand. Accordingly, to convince the faithful that God, in feeding them, fulfills the office of the best of parents, the prophet reminds them that he "giveth food to all flesh" (Psalm 136:25). In short, when we hear on the one hand that "the eyes of the Lord are upon the righteous, and his ears are open unto their cry," and on the other hand that "the face of the Lord is against them that do evil, to cut off the remembrance of them from the earth" (Psalm 34:15, 16), let us be assured that all creatures above and below stand ready at his service, so that he may use them in whatever way he pleases. From this we infer not only that the general providence of God, continuing the order of nature, extends over the creatures, but that by his wonderful counsel they are adapted to a definite and special purpose.

8¶

Those who would bring this doctrine into disrepute slander it as the Stoic dogma of fate. The same charge was formerly brought against Augustine (Ad Bonifacium, book 2, chapter 6, and elsewhere). We do not wish to quarrel about words; but we do not accept the term Fate, both because it belongs to the class of profane novelties which Paul teaches us to shun (1 Timothy 6:20), and because there is an attempt, by means of a hateful term, to fix a stigma on the truth of God. But the dogma itself is falsely and maliciously charged against us. For we do not, with the Stoics, imagine a necessity consisting of a perpetual chain of causes and a kind of tangled series contained in nature; rather, we hold that God is the disposer and ruler of all things — that from the remotest eternity, according to his own wisdom, he decreed what he was to do, and now by his power carries out what he decreed. Hence we maintain that by his providence not only heaven and earth and inanimate creatures, but also the counsels and wills of men, are so governed as to move exactly in the course which he has appointed. What, then, you will say — does nothing happen by chance, nothing contingently? I answer that it was a true saying of Basil the Great, that Fortune and Chance are heathen terms, the meaning of which ought not to occupy godly minds. For if all success is a blessing from God, and calamity and adversity are his curse, there is no place left in human affairs for fortune and chance. We ought also to be moved by the words of Augustine (Retractations, book 1, chapter 1): "In my writings against the Academics," he says, "I regret having so often used the term Fortune, although I meant by it not some goddess, but the chance outcome of events in external matters, whether good or evil. Hence, too, those words, Perhaps, Perchance, Fortuitously, Forte. Forsan. Forsitan, Fortuito.

9¶

But since our sluggish minds lie far beneath the height of Divine Providence, we must make use of a distinction that may help them to rise. I say, then, that though all things are ordered by the counsel and fixed arrangement of God, to us they are nevertheless fortuitous. This is not because we imagine that Fortune rules the world and mankind and turns all things upside down at random (far be such a heartless thought from every Christian breast). It is because the order, method, end, and necessity of events are for the most part hidden in the counsel of God; and so, though it is certain that they are produced by the will of God, they have the appearance of being fortuitous, since this is the form in which they present themselves to us, whether they are considered in their own nature or weighed according to our knowledge and judgment. Suppose, for example, that a merchant, after entering a forest in the company of trustworthy men, unwisely strays from his companions and wanders bewildered until he falls into a den of robbers and is murdered. His death was not only foreseen by the eye of God, but had been fixed by his decree. For it is said, not that he foresaw how far the life of each individual would extend, but that he determined and fixed the bounds which could not be passed (Job 14:5). Still, in relation to our capacity of discernment, all these things appear fortuitous. How will the Christian regard it? Though he will consider that every circumstance in that man's death was indeed fortuitous in its nature, he will have no doubt that the Providence of God overruled it and directed fortune to his own end. The same holds in the case of future contingencies. Since all future events are uncertain to us, they seem to hang in suspense, as if ready to go either way. Still, the conviction remains settled in our hearts that nothing will happen which the Lord has not provided for. In this sense the term event is repeatedly used in Ecclesiastes, because at first glance men do not penetrate to the primary cause, which lies hidden. And yet what Scripture teaches of the secret providence of God was never so completely erased from the human heart that some sparks did not always shine in the darkness. So the soothsayers of the Philistines, though they waver in uncertainty, attribute the adverse event partly to God and partly to chance. If the ark, they say, "goes up by the way of his own coast to Bethshemish, then he has done us this great evil; but if not, then we shall know that it is not his hand that smote us, it was a chance that happened to us" (1 Samuel 6:9). Foolishly, indeed, when divination fails them, they flee to fortune. Still we see them held back, so that they do not dare to regard their disaster as fortuitous. But the way in which God, by the curb of his Providence, turns events in whatever direction he pleases will appear from a remarkable example. At the very moment when David was discovered in the wilderness of Maon, the Philistines make an invasion of the country, and Saul is forced to withdraw (1 Samuel 23:26, 27). If God, in order to provide for the safety of his servant, threw this obstacle in Saul's way, we surely cannot say that, though the Philistines took up arms contrary to human expectation, they did it by chance. What seems to us contingency, faith will recognize as the secret impulse of God. The reason is not always equally plain, but we ought undoubtedly to hold that all the changes which take place in the world are produced by the secret working of the hand of God. At the same time, what God has determined, though it must come to pass, is not, however, strictly or in its own nature necessary. We have a familiar example in the case of our Savior's bones. Since he took a body like ours, no sane man will deny that his bones were capable of being broken; and yet it was impossible that they should be broken (John 19:33, 36). Hence again we see that there was good ground for the distinction the Schoolmen made between necessity secundum quid and absolute necessity, and also between the necessity of the consequent and the necessity of the consequence. God made the bones of his Son breakable, though he exempted them from actual breaking; and so, with reference to the necessity of his counsel, he made impossible what might naturally have taken place.

Calvin will not let us divide the world between a God who made it and a fortune that runs it; the same hand that flung the stars holds the sparrow, and not one of its parts escapes his governing care. For a believer in 2026 — a year that, like every year, will bring its layoffs, its diagnoses, its headlines engineered to make us afraid — this is no abstraction but the ground we stand on. The economy that feels like blind chance, the illness that arrives unannounced, the door that closes without explanation: none of it is fortune, and none of it is loose from his decree. Teach this to your children at the table, that the God who numbers their hairs also numbers their days, so that when the world tells them everything is random they will know better. Watchfulness is not anxiety; it is the settled confidence of those who know who reigns. We do not drift on a sea of accidents — we are kept.
