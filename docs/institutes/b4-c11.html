Of the Jurisdiction of the Church, and the Abuses of It, as Exemplified in the Papacy

This chapter may be conveniently comprehended under two heads,—I. Ecclesiastical jurisdiction, its necessity, origin, description, and essential parts—viz. the sacred ministry of the word, and discipline of excommunication, of which the aim, use, and abuse are explained, sec. 1-8. II. Refutation of the arguments advanced by Papists in defence of the tyranny of Pontiffs, the right of both swords, imperial pomp and dignity, foreign jurisdiction, and immunity from civil jurisdiction, sec. 9-16. 1. The power of the Church in regard to jurisdiction. The necessity, origin, and nature of this jurisdiction. The power of the keys to be considered in two points of view. The first view expounded. 2. Second view expounded. How the Church binds and looses in the way of discipline. Abuse of the keys in the Papacy. 3. The discipline of excommunication of perpetual endurance. Distinction between civil and ecclesiastical power. 4. The perpetual endurance of the discipline of excommunication confirmed. Duly ordered under the Emperors and Christian magistrates. 5. The aim and use of ecclesiastical jurisdiction in the primitive Church. Spiritual power was kept entirely distinct from the power of the sword. 6. Spiritual power was not administered by one individual, but by a lawful consistory. Gradual change. First, the clergy alone interfered in the judicial proceedings of the Church. The bishop afterwards appropriated them to himself. 7. The bishops afterwards transferred the rights thus appropriated to their officials, and converted spiritual jurisdiction into a profane tribunal. 8. Recapitulation. The Papal power confuted. Christ wished to debar the ministers of the word from civil rule and worldly power. 9. Objections of the Papists. 1. By this external splendour the glory of Christ is displayed. 2. It does not interfere with the duties of their calling. Both objections answered. 10. The commencement and gradual progress of the Papistical tyranny. Causes, 1. Curiosity; 2. Ambition; 3. Violence; 4. Hypocrisy; 5. Impiety. 11. Last cause, the mystery of iniquity, and the Satanic fury of Antichrist usurping worldly dominion. The Pope claims both swords. 12. The pretended donation of Constantine. Its futility exposed. 13. When, and by what means, the Roman Pontiffs attained to imperial dignity. Hildebrand its founder. 14. By what acts they seized on Rome and other territories. Disgraceful rapacity. 15. Claim of immunity from civil jurisdiction. Contrast between this pretended immunity and the moderation of the early bishops. 16. What end the early bishops aimed at in steadfastly resisting civil encroachment.

1¶

It remains to consider the third part of ecclesiastical power, which, when things are rightly ordered, is in fact the chief part, and which, as we have said, consists in jurisdiction. Now the whole jurisdiction of the Church relates to discipline, of which we are shortly to treat. For as no city or village can exist without a magistrate and government, so the Church of God, as I have already taught but am obliged to repeat, needs a kind of spiritual government. This is altogether distinct from civil government, and is so far from hindering or weakening it that it rather does much to aid and advance it. This power of jurisdiction, then, is in a word nothing but the order provided for preserving the spiritual polity. To this end there were established in the Church from the beginning tribunals which might take cognizance of morals, rebuke vices, and exercise the office of the keys. Paul mentions this order in the First Epistle to the Corinthians under the name of governments (1 Corinthians 12:28); and in the same way in the Epistle to the Romans, when he says, "He that ruleth with diligence" (Romans 12:8). For he is not addressing magistrates, none of whom were Christians at that time, but those who were joined with the pastors in the spiritual government of the Church. In the Epistle to Timothy also he mentions two kinds of presbyters: some who labor in the word, and others who do not perform the office of preaching but rule well (1 Timothy 5:17). By this latter class he undoubtedly means those who were appointed to oversee morals and to the whole use of the keys. For the power of which we speak depends wholly on the keys which Christ bestowed on the Church in the eighteenth chapter of Matthew, where he commands that those who despise private admonition be sharply rebuked in public, and, if they persist in their stubbornness, be expelled from the fellowship of believers. Moreover, such admonitions and corrections cannot be made without inquiry, and hence the need for some judicial procedure and order. Therefore, if we would not make the promise of the keys void and abolish altogether excommunication, solemn admonitions, and everything of that kind, we must of necessity give some jurisdiction to the Church. Let the reader note that we are not here treating of the general authority of doctrine, as in Matthew 21 and John 20, but maintaining that the right of the Sanhedrin has been transferred to the fold of Christ. Until that time the power of government had belonged to the Jews. This Christ establishes in his Church, so far as it was a pure institution, and with a heavy sanction. So it had to be, since otherwise the judgment of a poor and despised Church might be spurned by rash and haughty men. And lest it cause the reader any difficulty that Christ, in the same words, makes a considerable difference between the two things, it will be proper to explain it here. There are two passages which speak of binding and loosing. The one is Matthew 16, where Christ, after promising that he will give Peter the keys of the kingdom of heaven, immediately adds, "Whatsoever thou shalt bind on earth shall be bound in heaven; and whatsoever thou shalt loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven" (Matthew 16:19). These words have the very same meaning as those in the Gospel of John, where, as he is about to send the disciples out to preach, he breathes on them and says, "Whose soever sins ye remit, they are remitted unto them; and whose soever sins ye retain, they are retained" (John 20:23). I will give an interpretation that is not subtle, not forced, not twisted, but genuine, natural, and plain. This command concerning remitting and retaining sins, and the promise made to Peter concerning binding and loosing, ought to be referred to nothing but the ministry of the word. When the Lord committed that ministry to the apostles, he at the same time furnished them with this power of binding and loosing. For what is the sum of the gospel but this: that all, being slaves of sin and death, are loosed and set free by the redemption which is in Christ Jesus, while those who do not receive and acknowledge Christ as deliverer and redeemer are condemned and sentenced to eternal chains? When the Lord delivered this message to his apostles, to be carried by them into all nations, he honored it with this remarkable testimony in order to prove that it was his own message and came from him; and this served as an admirable confirmation both to the apostles themselves and to all to whom the message was to come. It was important that the apostles should have a constant and complete assurance of their preaching, which they were not only to carry on with endless labor, anxiety, harassment, and danger, but in the end to seal with their blood. It was important, I say, that they should know it was not vain or empty but full of power and efficacy; that amid all their anxieties, dangers, and difficulties they might be persuaded that they were doing the work of God; that though the whole world withstood and opposed them, they might know that God was for them; and that, not having Christ, the author of their doctrine, bodily present on earth, they might understand that he was in heaven to confirm the truth of the doctrine he had delivered to them. On the other hand, it was necessary that their hearers should be most certainly assured that the doctrine of the gospel was not the word of the apostles but of God himself; not a voice rising from the earth, but one descending from heaven. For such things as the forgiveness of sins, the promise of eternal life, and the message of salvation cannot lie in the power of man. Christ therefore testified that in the preaching of the gospel the apostles acted only as ministers; that it was he who, using their mouths as instruments, spoke and promised all; and that therefore the forgiveness of sins which they announced was the true promise of God, and the condemnation which they pronounced the certain judgment of God. This attestation was given for all ages, and it remains firm, making all men certain and secure that the word of the gospel, by whomever it may be preached, is the very word of God, published at the supreme tribunal, written in the book of life, ratified, firm, and fixed in heaven. We now understand that the power of the keys in those places is simply the preaching of the gospel, and that, so far as men are concerned, it is not so much a power as a ministry. Properly speaking, Christ did not give this power to men but to his word, of which he made men the ministers.

2¶

The other passage in which binding and loosing are mentioned is in the eighteenth chapter of Matthew, where Christ says, "If he shall neglect to hear them, tell it unto the Church: but if he neglect to hear the Church, let him be unto thee as an heathen man and a publican. Verily I say unto you, Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth shall be bound in heaven; and whatsoever ye shall loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven" (Matthew 18:17, 18). This passage is not entirely like the former, but is to be understood somewhat differently. Yet in saying that they are different, I do not mean that there is not a close affinity between them. First, they are alike in this: both are general statements; in both there is always the same power of binding and loosing (namely, by the word of God), the same command, and the same promise. They differ in this: the former passage relates specially to the preaching which the ministers of the word perform, while the latter relates to the discipline of excommunication which has been committed to the Church. Now the Church binds the one whom she excommunicates, not by plunging him into eternal ruin and despair, but by condemning his life and conduct and warning him that unless he repents he is condemned. She looses the one whom she receives into communion, because she makes him, as it were, a partaker of the unity which she has in Christ Jesus. Let no one, therefore, stubbornly despise the judgment of the Church, or count it a small matter to be condemned by the votes of the faithful. The Lord testifies that such a judgment of the faithful is nothing else than the publishing of his own sentence, and that what they do on earth is ratified in heaven. For they have the word of God by which they condemn the perverse; they have the word by which they receive the penitent back into favor. Now they cannot err or disagree with the judgment of God, because they judge only according to the law of God, which is not an uncertain or worldly opinion but the holy will of God, an oracle of heaven. On these two passages, which I think I have expounded briefly as well as plainly and truly, these madmen, carried along without any discernment by their spirit of giddiness, try to found now confession, now excommunication, now jurisdiction, now the right of making laws, now indulgences. The former passage they bring forward in order to build up the primacy of the Roman See. So familiar are the keys to those who have thought fit to furnish them with locks and doors, that you would say their whole life had been spent in the locksmith's trade.

3¶

Some, imagining that all these things were temporary because magistrates were still strangers to our profession of religion, are led astray by failing to observe the distinction and difference between ecclesiastical and civil power. For the Church does not have the right of the sword to punish or restrain; she has no power to coerce, no prison, nor any of the other punishments which the magistrate customarily inflicts. Then too, the aim is not to punish the sinner against his will, but to obtain a profession of repentance by a voluntary chastisement. The two things, therefore, are widely different, because the Church does not take to herself anything that is proper to the magistrate, nor is the magistrate competent to do what the Church does. An example will make this clearer. Does someone get drunk? In a well-ordered city his punishment will be imprisonment. Has he committed fornication? The punishment will be similar, or rather more severe. In this way the laws, the magistrates, and the outward tribunal will be satisfied. But it will turn out that the offender gives no sign of repentance, but rather frets and grumbles. Will the Church not step in here? Such persons cannot be admitted to the Lord's Supper without injury to Christ and his sacred institution. Reason requires that he who gives offense to the Church by a bad example should remove the offense he has caused by a formal declaration of repentance. The reason offered by those who take the opposite view is a cold one. Christ, they say, gave this office to the Church when there were no magistrates to carry it out. But it often happens that the magistrate is negligent, and sometimes even needs to be chastised himself, as was the case with the Emperor Theodosius. Moreover, the same thing might be said of the whole ministry of the word. On that view, then, let pastors now stop censuring open wickedness; let them stop chiding, accusing, and rebuking. For there are Christian magistrates who ought to correct these things by the laws and the sword. But as the magistrate ought to cleanse the Church of offenses by bodily punishment and coercion, so the minister in his turn ought to help the magistrate reduce the number of offenders. So they ought to join their efforts, the one being not a hindrance but a help to the other.

4¶

And indeed, if we attend more closely to the words of Christ, it will readily appear that the state and order of the Church described there is perpetual, not temporary. For it would be incongruous that those who refuse to obey our admonitions should be handed over to the magistrate, which would nevertheless be necessary if he were to take the place of the Church. Why should the promise, "Verily I say unto you, What thing soever ye shall bind on earth," be limited to one year or to a few? Moreover, Christ made no new enactment here, but followed the custom always observed in the Church of his ancient people, thereby indicating that the Church cannot do without the spiritual jurisdiction which existed from the beginning. This has been confirmed by the consent of all ages. For when emperors and magistrates began to take the Christian name, spiritual jurisdiction was not at once abolished, but was only so arranged as not to weaken civil jurisdiction in any respect, or be confused with it. And rightly so. For the magistrate, if he is godly, will have no wish to exempt himself from the common subjection of the children of God, of which not the least part is to submit himself to the Church when she judges according to the word of God; so far is it from being his duty to abolish that judgment. For, as Ambrose says, "What more honorable title can an emperor have than to be called a son of the Church? A good emperor is within the Church, not above the Church" (Ambrose, To Valentinian, Epistle 32). Those, therefore, who strip the Church of this power in order to adorn the magistrate, not only corrupt the meaning of Christ by a false interpretation, but pass no light condemnation on the many holy bishops who have lived since the days of the apostles, as though they had usurped the honor and office of the civil magistrate on a false pretext.

5¶

But on the other hand, it will be proper to see what the true use of ecclesiastical discipline was in ancient times, and how great were the abuses that crept in, so that we may know what of ancient practice is to be abolished and what restored, if, after overthrowing the kingdom of Antichrist, we would set up the true kingdom of Christ again. First, the aim is to prevent scandals from arising, and, when they arise, to remove them. In its use two things are to be considered: first, that this spiritual power be kept altogether distinct from the power of the sword; secondly, that it be administered not at the will of one individual but by a lawful consistory (1 Corinthians 5:4). Both were observed in the purer times of the Church. For holy bishops did not exercise their power by fines, imprisonment, or other civil penalties, but, as became them, used the word of God alone. For the severest punishment of the Church, her last thunderbolt as it were, is excommunication, which is not used except in necessity. There is nothing contrary to this in Augustine's statement (Epistle 119) that, like the teachers of the liberal arts and studies, bishops too were often accustomed in their judicial proceedings to chastise with the rod.

6¶

This power, as we have already said, did not belong to one individual who could exercise it as he pleased, but to the consistory of elders, which was in the Church what a council is in a city. Cyprian, when he mentions those by whom it was exercised in his time, usually joins the whole clergy with the bishop (Cyprian, Book 3, Epistles 14, 19). In another place he shows that, although the clergy presided, the people were not at the same time shut out from cognizance; for he writes: "From the beginning of my bishopric I resolved to do nothing without the advice of the clergy, nothing without the consent of the people." But the common and usual way of exercising this jurisdiction was through the council of presbyters, of whom, as I have said, there were two classes. Some were for teaching; others were only overseers of morals. This institution gradually declined from its original form, so that in the time of Ambrose the clergy alone took cognizance of ecclesiastical cases. He complains of this in the following terms: "The ancient synagogue, and afterwards the Church, had elders, without whose advice nothing was done. This has fallen into disuse, by whose fault I do not know, unless it be through the sloth, or rather the pride, of teachers, who want it to seem that they alone are something" (Ambrose, on 1 Timothy 5). We see how indignant this holy man was that the better state had been impaired in some degree, and yet the order which then existed was at least tolerable. What, then, if he had seen those shapeless ruins which show no trace of the ancient building? How he would have lamented! First, contrary to what was right and lawful, the bishop took to himself what had been given to the whole Church. For this is just as if the consul had driven out the senate and seized the whole government. For although he is superior in rank to the others, the authority of the consistory is greater than that of one individual. It was therefore a gross wickedness when one man, transferring the common power to himself, paved the way for tyrannical license, robbed the Church of what was her own, and suppressed and cast aside the consistory ordained by the Spirit of Christ.

7¶

But as evil always breeds evil, the bishops, disdaining this jurisdiction as a thing unworthy of their care, handed it over to others. Hence the appointment of officials to take their place. I am not now speaking of the character of this class of men; all I say is that they differ in no respect from civil judges. And yet they call it spiritual jurisdiction, though all the litigation concerns worldly affairs. Even if there were no other evil in this, how can they dare to call a court of lawsuits a church court? But, they say, there are admonitions; there is excommunication. This is how God is mocked. Does some poor man owe a sum of money? He is summoned. If he appears, he is found liable; when he is found liable, if he does not pay, he is admonished. After the second admonition, the next step is excommunication. If he does not appear, he is admonished to appear; if he delays, he is admonished, and before long excommunicated. I ask, is there any resemblance whatever between this and the institution of Christ, or ancient custom, or ecclesiastical procedure? But, they say, vices too are censured there. Fornication, lewdness, drunkenness, and similar wickedness they not only tolerate, but by a kind of silent approval encourage and confirm, and that not among the people only but also among the clergy. Out of many they summon a few, either so as not to seem to wink too openly, or so as to fine them in money. I say nothing of the plunder, robbery, embezzlement, and sacrilege committed there. I say nothing of the kind of men who are for the most part appointed to the office. It is enough, and more than enough, that when the Romanists boast of their spiritual jurisdiction we are ready to show that nothing is more contrary to the procedure instituted by Christ, and that it bears no more resemblance to ancient practice than darkness does to light.

8¶

Although we have not said all that might be brought forward here, and even what has been said is only briefly touched on, enough, I trust, has been said to leave no one in doubt that the spiritual power on which the Pope prides himself, together with all his adherents, is an impious contradiction of the word of God and an unjust tyranny over his people. Under the name of spiritual power I include their boldness in framing new doctrines, by which they led the wretched people away from the genuine purity of the word of God; the wicked traditions by which they ensnared them; and the pseudo-ecclesiastical jurisdiction which they exercise through suffragans and officials. For if we allow Christ to reign among us, the whole of that domination cannot help but tumble and fall at once. The right of the sword, which they also claim for themselves, is not exercised over consciences, and so does not fall to be considered here. Yet it is worth observing here that they are always like themselves, there being nothing they less resemble than what they would be thought to be, namely, pastors of the Church. I do not speak of the vices of particular men, but of the common wickedness, and therefore the pestilent nature, of the whole order, which is thought to be maimed unless it is distinguished by wealth and haughty titles. If in this matter we seek the authority of Christ, there can be no doubt that he meant to bar the ministers of his word from civil rule and worldly power when he said, "The princes of the Gentiles exercise dominion over them, and they that are great exercise authority upon them. But it shall not be so among you" (Matthew 20:25, 26). For he indicates not only that the office of pastor is distinct from the office of prince, but that the two things differ so widely that they cannot be joined in the same person. Moses indeed held both (Exodus 18:16); but, first, this was the effect of a rare miracle, and, secondly, it was temporary, until matters could be better arranged. For when a definite form is prescribed by the Lord, the civil government is left to Moses, and he is ordered to resign the priesthood to his brother. And rightly so; for it is more than nature can do for one man to carry both burdens. This has been carefully observed in the Church in every age. Never did any bishop, so long as any true appearance of a church remained, think of usurping the right of the sword; so that in the age of Ambrose it was a common proverb that emperors longed more for the priesthood than priests for imperial power. This is stated by Ambrose, Sermon on the Surrender of the Basilicas. See also Augustine, On Faith and Works, chapter 4.

9¶

But after a method was devised by which bishops might hold the title, honor, and wealth of their office without its burden and care, so that they might be left wholly idle, the right of the sword was given to them, or rather, they seized it for themselves. On what pretext will they defend this effrontery? Was it the part of bishops to entangle themselves in hearing lawsuits and in governing states and provinces, and to take up occupations so utterly foreign to them? Bishops, I say, whose own office requires so much time and labor that, even if they gave themselves to it diligently and wholly, without being drawn away by other pursuits, they would scarcely be equal to it? But such is their perversity that they do not hesitate to boast that in this way the dignity of Christ's kingdom is duly upheld, while they themselves are not withdrawn from their own calling. As to the first claim: if it is a fitting ornament of the sacred office that those who hold it be raised so high as to become formidable to the greatest monarchs, they have reason to remonstrate with Christ, who in this respect has seriously curtailed their honor. For what, on their view, could be more insulting than these words: "The kings of the Gentiles exercise authority over them"? "But ye shall not be so" (Luke 22:25, 26). And yet he lays no harder law on his servants than he had first laid on himself. "Who," he says, "made me a judge or divider over you?" (Luke 12:14). We see that he flatly refuses the office of judging; and he would not have done this if the thing had been in keeping with his office. Will his servants not submit to the subordination to which the Lord thus lowered himself? As to the other point, I wish they could prove it by experience as easily as they assert it. But since the apostles did not think it right to leave the word of God and serve tables, these men are thereby compelled to admit, though they refuse to be taught, that it is impossible for the same person to be a good bishop and a good prince. For if those who, by the greatness of the gifts with which they were endowed, were equal to far more numerous and weighty cares than any who came after them, confessed that they could not serve the ministry of the word and of tables without sinking under the burden, how can these men, who are no men at all compared with the apostles, possibly surpass them a hundred times in diligence? The very attempt is most impudent and audacious presumption. Still, we see the thing done; with what success is plain. The result could only be that they have deserted their own functions and gone over to another camp.

10¶

There can be no doubt that this great advance was made from slender beginnings. They could not get so far in one step; but at one time, by craft and cunning, they quietly raised themselves before anyone foresaw what would happen; at another, when occasion offered, they extorted some increase of power from princes by threats and terror; at another, when they saw princes disposed to give generously, they abused their foolish and thoughtless readiness. In ancient times the godly, when any dispute arose, left the decision to the bishop in order to avoid the necessity of a lawsuit, because they had no doubt of his integrity. The ancient bishops were often much displeased at being entangled in such matters, as Augustine declares somewhere; but lest the parties should rush off to some contentious tribunal, they reluctantly submitted to the annoyance. These voluntary arbitrations, which differed entirely from the strife of the courts, these men have turned into ordinary jurisdiction. When cities and districts, hard pressed for a time by various difficulties, turned to the patronage of the bishops and threw themselves on their protection, these men by a strange artifice made themselves masters instead of patrons. That they have seized a good part by the violence of faction cannot be denied. The princes, again, who freely conferred jurisdiction on bishops were led to do so by various causes. Though their indulgence had some appearance of piety, by this misguided generosity they did not best serve the interests of the Church, whose ancient and true discipline they thereby corrupted, or rather, to tell the truth, abolished altogether. Those bishops who turn the goodness of princes to their own advantage have given more than enough proof by this one example of their conduct that they were not true bishops at all. Had they possessed one spark of the apostolic spirit, they would doubtless have answered in the words of Paul, "The weapons of our warfare are not carnal," but spiritual (2 Corinthians 10:4). But carried away by blind greed, they ruined themselves, their posterity, and the Church.

11¶

At length the Roman Pontiff, not content with modest districts, laid hands first on kingdoms and afterward on the empire. And in order to keep, on some pretext or other, a possession secured by sheer robbery, he boasts at one time that he holds it by divine right; at another he pretends a donation from Constantine; at another, some different title. First, I answer with Bernard: "Grant that on some ground or other he can claim it, it is not by apostolic right. For Peter could not give what he did not have, but what he had he gave to his successors, namely, the care of the churches. But when our Lord and Master says that he was not appointed a judge between two, the servant and disciple ought not to think it beneath him not to be judge of all" (Bernard, On Consideration, Book 2). Bernard is speaking of civil judgments, for he adds, "Your power, then, is over sins, not over rights of property, since it was for the former and not the latter that you received the keys of the kingdom of heaven. Which of the two seems to you the higher dignity, forgiving sins or dividing lands? There is no comparison. These low earthly things have for their judges the kings and princes of the earth. Why do you invade the territories of others?" and so on. Again: "You have been made superior" (he is addressing Pope Eugenius), "for what? Not to domineer, I presume. Let us therefore remember, however highly we think of ourselves, that a ministry is laid upon us, not a dominion given to us. Learn that you need a slender rod, not a scepter, to do the work of a prophet." Again: "It is plain that the apostles are forbidden to exercise dominion. Go, then, and dare to usurp for yourself either apostleship with dominion or dominion with apostleship." Immediately afterward he says, "The apostolic pattern is this: dominion is forbidden, ministry is enjoined." Though Bernard speaks this way, and so speaks as to make it plain to all that he speaks the truth, and though the thing itself is plain without a word, the Roman Pontiff was not ashamed at the Council of Arles to decree that the supreme right of both swords belonged to him by divine right.

12¶

As for the donation of Constantine, those who are moderately acquainted with the history of the period need not be told that the claim is not only fabulous but also absurd. But to say nothing of history, Gregory alone is a fit and entirely sufficient witness to this. For wherever he speaks of the emperor he calls him His Most Serene Lord, and himself his unworthy servant. Gregory, Book 2, Epistle 5; Book 3, Epistle 20; Book 2, Epistle 61; Book 4, Epistles 31, 34.

13¶

Should anyone ask when this fictitious empire began to emerge, not yet five hundred years have passed since the Roman Pontiffs were in subjection to the emperors, and no pontiff was elected without the emperor's authority. The occasion for overturning this order was given to Gregory VII by Henry IV, a giddy and rash man, without prudence, of great audacity, and of dissolute life. When he had all the bishoprics of Germany at his court, partly for sale and partly exposed to plunder, Hildebrand, who had been provoked by him, seized on this plausible pretext for asserting his claim. Because his cause seemed good and godly, it was viewed with great favor, while Henry, on account of the insolence of his rule, was generally hated by the princes. At length Hildebrand, who took the name of Gregory VII, an impure and wicked man, betrayed his sinister intentions. At this many who had joined him in his conspiracy deserted him. He gained this much, however: his successors were able not only to shake off the yoke with impunity but also to bring the emperors into subjection to themselves. Moreover, many of the later emperors were more like Henry than Julius Caesar. It was not difficult to overcome these while they sat at home, sluggish and careless, instead of exerting themselves vigorously, as was most necessary, by every lawful means to curb the greed of the pontiffs. We see what color there is for the grand donation of Constantine, by which the Pope pretends that the western empire was given to him.

14¶

Meanwhile the pontiff never ceased, whether by fraud, by treachery, or by arms, to invade the dominions of others. Rome itself, which was then free, they brought under their power about a hundred and thirty years ago. At length they obtained the dominion they now possess; and to keep or enlarge it they have now for two hundred years (for they had begun before they usurped dominion over the city) so troubled the Christian world that they have almost destroyed it. Formerly, in the time of Gregory, when the guardians of church property seized lands which they considered to belong to the Church and, in the manner of the treasury, affixed their seals to attest their claim, Gregory assembled a council of bishops, inveighed bitterly against that profane custom, and asked whether they would not anathematize the churchman who of his own accord tried to seize some possession by inscribing a title on it, and likewise the bishop who ordered it to be done, or did not punish it when it was done without his order. All pronounced the anathema. If it is a crime deserving anathema for a churchman to claim a property by inscribing a title, then, now that for two hundred years the pontiffs have been planning nothing but war and bloodshed, the destruction of armies, the plunder of cities, the ruin or overthrow of nations, and the devastation of kingdoms, only so that they may seize the property of others, what anathemas could sufficiently punish such conduct? Surely it is perfectly obvious that the very last thing they aim at is the glory of Christ. For if they were of their own accord to give up every portion of secular power they possess, no danger to the glory of God, no danger to sound doctrine, no danger to the safety of the Church would follow; but they are carried along blind and headlong by a lust for power, thinking that nothing can be safe unless they rule, as the prophet says, "with force and with cruelty" (Ezekiel 34:4).

15¶

Attached to jurisdiction is the immunity claimed by the Romish clergy. They think it beneath them to answer before a civil judge in personal cases, and they consider both the liberty and the dignity of the Church to consist in exemption from the ordinary courts and laws. But the ancient bishops, who in other respects were most resolute in asserting the rights of the Church, did not think it any injury to themselves or their order to act as subjects. Godly emperors also, as often as there was occasion, summoned clergy before their tribunals and met with no opposition. For Constantine, in a letter to the Nicomedians, speaks thus: "Should any of the bishops rashly stir up tumult, his audacity shall be restrained by the minister of God, that is, by my executive" (Theodoret, Book 1, chapter 20). Valentinian says, "Good bishops cast no reproach on the power of the emperor, but sincerely keep the commandments of God, the great King, and obey our laws" (Theodoret, Book 4, chapter 8). This was unquestionably the view then held by all. Ecclesiastical cases, indeed, were brought before the bishop's court: when a clergyman had offended, but not against the laws, he was charged only under the Canons, and instead of being cited before the civil court he had the bishop as his judge in that particular case. Likewise, when a question of faith was being debated, or one which properly belonged to the Church, cognizance of it was left to the Church. It is in this sense that the words of Ambrose are to be understood: "Your father, of august memory, not only replied by word of mouth but enacted by law that in a question of faith the judge should be one who was neither unequal in office nor unqualified by the nature of his jurisdiction" (Ambrose, Epistle 32). Again: "If we attend to the Scriptures, or to ancient examples, who can deny that in a question of faith, a question of faith, I say, bishops are accustomed to judge Christian emperors, not emperors to judge bishops?" Again: "I would have come before your consistory, O emperor, if either the bishops or the people had allowed me to come; they say that a question of faith should be discussed in the Church before the people." He maintains, indeed, that a spiritual case, that is, one pertaining to religion, is not to be brought before the civil court, where worldly disputes are argued. His firmness in this respect is rightly praised by all. And yet, though he has a good cause, he goes so far as to say that if it comes to force and violence, he will yield. "I will not desert the post committed to me, but if I am forced, I will not resist; prayers and tears are our weapons" (Ambrose, Sermon on the Surrender of the Basilicas). Let us note the singular moderation of this holy man, his combination of prudence, greatness of spirit, and boldness. Justina, the emperor's mother, unable to win him over to the Arian party, sought to drive him from the government of the Church. And this would have been the result had he gone to the palace to plead his cause when summoned. He maintains, therefore, that the emperor is not fit to decide such a controversy. Both the necessity of the times and the very nature of the matter demanded this. He thought it better for him to die than to consent to hand down such an example to posterity; and yet if violence is offered, he does not think of resisting. For he says it is not the part of a bishop to defend the faith and rights of the Church by arms. But in all other cases he declares himself ready to do whatever the emperor commands. "If he asks tribute, we do not refuse it; the lands of the Church pay tribute. If he asks for lands, he has the power to take them; none of us stands in the way." Gregory speaks in the same way: "I am not ignorant of the mind of my most serene lord; he is not accustomed to meddle in priestly cases, lest he should in some degree burden himself with our sins." He does not exclude the emperor in general from judging priests, but says that there are certain cases which he ought to leave to the ecclesiastical tribunal.

16¶

And so all that these holy men sought by this exception was to prevent irreligious princes from hindering the Church in the discharge of her duty by their tyrannical whim and violence. They did not object when princes interposed their authority in ecclesiastical affairs, provided this was done to preserve and not to disturb the order of the Church, to establish and not to destroy discipline. For since the Church does not have, and ought not to wish to have, the power of compulsion (I mean civil coercion), it is the part of godly kings and princes to uphold religion by laws, edicts, and judgments. Thus, when the emperor Maurice had commanded certain bishops to receive their neighboring colleagues who had been driven out by the Barbarians, Gregory confirms the order and urges them to obey. Book 1, Epistle 43; Book 4, Epistles 32, 34; Book 7, Epistle 39.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

Calvin's burden here is that Christ entrusted his church with a real but strictly spiritual jurisdiction—the keys, exercised through the ministry of the Word and the discipline of excommunication—and that this power is corrupted the moment it grasps for the sword, for wealth, or for dominion over princes. The two governments God ordained, civil and spiritual, aid one another precisely by staying distinct. In 2026 the temptation runs the other way: a church tempted to chase political leverage and cultural muscle as though the kingdom advanced by influence rather than by the Word faithfully preached and faithfully obeyed. Let the household and the congregation recover what is theirs—patient admonition, plain accountability, the courage to name sin among brothers—and leave to the magistrate what belongs to the magistrate. A church that knows the limits of its sword is the church most able to wield its true authority. Spiritual power kept spiritual is power that endures when empires do not.
