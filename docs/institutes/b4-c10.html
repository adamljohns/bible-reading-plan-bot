Of the Power of Making Laws — The Cruelty of the Pope and His Adherents, in This Respect, in Tyrannically Oppressing and Destroying Souls

1¶

We come now to the second part of power, which, according to them, consists in the making of laws; and from this source countless traditions have arisen, to be so many deadly snares to miserable souls. For they have been no more scrupulous than the scribes and Pharisees in laying burdens on the shoulders of others which they would not touch with a finger (Matthew 23:4; Luke 11:16). I have shown elsewhere (Book 3, chapter 4, sections 4-7) what cruel murder they commit through their doctrine of auricular confession. The same violence is not apparent in their other laws, but even those that seem most tolerable press tyrannically on the conscience. I say nothing of the way in which they corrupt the worship of God and rob God himself, who is the only Lawgiver, of his right. The power we now have to consider is this: whether it is lawful for the Church to bind its laws upon the conscience. In this discussion civil order is not touched; the only point under consideration is how God may be rightly worshiped according to the rule he has prescribed, and how our spiritual liberty, with reference to God, may remain unimpaired. In ordinary speech the name of human traditions is given to all decrees concerning the worship of God which men have issued without the authority of his word. It is against these that we contend, not against the sacred and useful constitutions of the Church which serve to preserve discipline, or decency, or peace. Our aim is to curb the unlimited and barbarous dominion over souls usurped by those who wish to be thought pastors of the Church, but who are in fact its most cruel murderers. They say that the laws they enact are spiritual, pertaining to the soul, and they affirm that these laws are necessary to eternal life. But in this way the kingdom of Christ, as I observed a little earlier, is invaded; in this way the liberty which he has given to the consciences of believers is utterly oppressed and overthrown. I say nothing of the great impiety with which, to enforce the observance of their laws, they declare that from it they seek forgiveness of sins, righteousness, and salvation, while they make the whole sum of religion and piety consist in it. What I contend for is this: that necessity ought not to be laid on consciences in matters in which Christ has made them free; and unless they are freed, they cannot, as we have shown before (Book 3, chapter 19), have peace with God. If they would keep the grace which they have once received in Christ, they must acknowledge Christ their deliverer as their only king and be ruled by the only law of liberty, namely, the sacred word of the gospel; they must be subject to no bondage and bound by no chains.

2¶

These Solons, indeed, imagine that their constitutions are laws of liberty, a pleasant yoke, a light burden; but who does not see that this is sheer falsehood? They themselves, indeed, do not feel the burden of their laws. Having cast off the fear of God, they carelessly and steadily disregard their own laws as well as those that are divine. But those who have any concern for their salvation are far from thinking themselves free so long as they are entangled in these snares. We see how great caution Paul used in this matter, not venturing to impose a fetter in any one thing, and with good reason: he certainly foresaw how great a wound would be inflicted on the conscience if those things should be made necessary which the Lord had left free. On the contrary, it is scarcely possible to count the constitutions which these men have most harshly enforced under the penalty of eternal death, and which they exact with the greatest rigor as necessary to salvation. And while very many of them are most difficult to observe, all of them taken together are impossible, so great is the mass. How, then, can those on whom this mountain of difficulty lies possibly avoid being perplexed with extreme anxiety and filled with terror? My intention here, then, is to attack constitutions of this kind: constitutions enacted for the purpose of binding the conscience inwardly before God and imposing religious duties, as if they commanded things necessary to salvation.

3¶

Many are greatly puzzled by this question because they do not distinguish with sufficient care between what is called the external forum and the forum of conscience French, "entre le siege judicial de Dieu, qui est spirituel, et la justice terrestre des hommes;" — between the judgment seat of God and the earthly justice of men.

4¶

Therefore, as works have respect to men, so conscience has reference to God; and so a good conscience is nothing other than inward integrity of heart. In this sense Paul says that "the end of the commandment is charity out of a pure heart, and of a good conscience, and of faith unfeigned" (1 Timothy 1:5). Afterward, in the same chapter, he shows how widely it differs from the intellect, saying that "some having put away" a good conscience, "concerning faith have made shipwreck." For by these words he indicates that it is a living inclination to worship God, a sincere desire to live piously and holily. Sometimes, indeed, it is extended to men as well, as when Paul declares, "Herein do I exercise myself, to have always a conscience void of offence toward God, and toward men" (Acts 24:16). But this is said because the benefits of a good conscience flow outward and reach even to men. Properly speaking, however, it concerns God alone, as I have already said. Hence a law may be said to bind the conscience when it binds a man simply, without reference to other men and without taking them into account. For example, God commands us not only to keep our mind chaste and pure from all lust, but also forbids every kind of obscenity in speech and all outward lewdness. My conscience is bound to observe this law even if there were not another man in the world. So he who behaves intemperately not only sins by setting a bad example to his brothers, but stands convicted in his conscience before God. Another rule holds in the case of things which are in themselves indifferent. For we ought to abstain from them when they give offense, but the conscience is free. Thus Paul says of meat consecrated to idols, "If any man say unto you, This is offered in sacrifice unto idols, eat not for his sake that showed it, and for conscience sake;" "conscience, I say, not your own, but of the other" (1 Corinthians 10:28, 29). A believer would sin if, after being warned, he still ate meat of that kind. But however necessary abstinence may be with respect to a brother, as the Lord prescribes, the conscience does not cease to retain its liberty. We see how the law, while it binds the outward work, leaves the conscience free.

5¶

Let us now return to human laws. If they are imposed for the purpose of creating a religious obligation, as if observing them were in itself necessary, we say that the restraint thus laid on the conscience is unlawful. Our consciences have to do not with men but with God alone. Hence the common distinction between the earthly forum and the forum of conscience. French, "Et de fait, tel a eté le sens de cette distinction vulgaire qu'on a tenue par toutes les ecoles; que c'est autre choses des jurisdictions humaines et politiques, que de celles qui touchent à la conscience;" — And in fact, such is the import of the common distincti has been held by all the schools, that human and civil jurisdictions are quite different from those which touch the conscience.

6¶

Such, however, are the things which at present are called ecclesiastical constitutions by the Papacy, and which are put forward as part of the true and necessary worship of God. But as they are without number, so they form countless fetters to bind and ensnare the soul. Although we touched on this subject in expounding the law (Book 3, chapter 4, section 6), yet since this is more properly the place for a full discussion of it, I will now try to give a summary of it as carefully as I can. I shall, however, leave aside the branch relating to the tyranny with which false bishops claim for themselves the right of teaching whatever they please, having already considered it as far as seemed necessary; but I shall treat at length the power they claim of making laws. The pretext, then, on which our false bishops burden the conscience with new laws is that the Lord has appointed them spiritual legislators and given them the government of the Church. Hence they maintain that everything they order and prescribe must of necessity be observed by the Christian people, and that whoever violates their commands is guilty of a twofold disobedience, being a rebel against both God and the Church. Certainly, if they were true bishops, I would grant them some authority in this matter, not as much as they demand, but as much as is required for rightly ordering the polity of the Church; but since they are anything but what they wish to be thought, they cannot possibly take anything to themselves, however little, without going to excess. But since this too has been shown elsewhere, let us grant for the present that whatever power true bishops possess belongs to them rightly; still I deny that they have been set over believers as legislators, to prescribe a rule of life on their own authority or to bind the people committed to them to their decrees. When I say this, I mean that they have no right at all to insist that whatever they devise without the authority of the word of God must be observed by the Church as a matter of necessity. Since such power was unknown to the apostles, and was so often denied to the ministers of the Church by our Lord himself, I wonder how any have dared to usurp it, and dare at present to defend it, without any precedent from the apostles and against the plain prohibition of God.

7¶

Everything that pertains to a perfect rule of life the Lord has so comprehended in his law that he has left nothing for men to add to the summary given there. His purpose in doing this was, first, that since all uprightness of conduct consists in regulating all our actions by his will as the standard, he alone should be regarded as the master and guide of our life; and, second, that he might show that there is nothing he requires of us more than obedience. For this reason James says, "He that speaketh evil of his brother, and judgeth his brother, speaketh evil of the law, and judgeth the law:" "There is one lawgiver, who is able to save and to destroy" (James 4:11, 12). We hear how God claims it as his own special privilege to rule us by his laws. Isaiah had said this before, though somewhat obscurely: "The Lord is our judge, the Lord is our lawgiver, the Lord is our king; he will save us" (Isaiah 33:22). Both passages show that the power of life and death belongs to him who has power over the soul. Indeed, James expresses this clearly. This power no man may assume for himself. God, therefore, to whom the power of saving and destroying belongs, must be acknowledged as the only King of souls; or, as the words of Isaiah express it, he is our king and judge, our lawgiver and savior. So Peter, when he reminds pastors of their duty, exhorts them to feed the flock without lording it over the heritage (1 Peter 5:2), meaning by the heritage the body of believers. If we rightly consider that it is unlawful to transfer to man what God declares to belong to himself alone, we shall see that this cuts off completely all the power claimed by those who would take it upon themselves to order anything in the Church without authority from the word of God.

8¶

Moreover, since the whole question turns on this point, that because God is the only lawgiver it is unlawful for men to assume that honor for themselves, it will be proper to keep in mind the two reasons for which God claims it for himself alone. The first reason is that his will is for us the perfect rule of all righteousness and holiness, and that in the knowledge of it we therefore have a perfect rule of life. The other reason is that when the right and proper way of worshiping him is in question, he alone whom we ought to obey, and on whose will we ought to depend, has authority over our souls. When these two reasons are kept in view, it will be easy to decide which human constitutions are contrary to the word of the Lord. Of this kind are all those which are devised as part of the true worship of God and whose observance is bound upon the conscience as a necessary obligation. Let us remember, then, to weigh all human laws in this balance if we would have a sure test that will not allow us to go astray. The first reason Paul presses in the Epistle to the Colossians against the false apostles who tried to lay new burdens on the churches. The second he uses more often in the Epistle to the Galatians, in a similar case. In the Epistle to the Colossians, then, he maintains that the doctrine of the true worship of God is not to be sought from men, because the Lord has faithfully and fully taught us how he is to be worshiped. To prove this, he says in the first chapter that the gospel contains all wisdom, so that the man of God may be made perfect in Christ. At the beginning of the second chapter he says that all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden in Christ, and from this he concludes that believers should beware of being led away from the flock of Christ by vain philosophy, according to the constitutions of men (Colossians 2:10). At the end of the chapter he condemns still more decisively all ἐθελοθρησκειας, that is, invented modes of worship which men either devise themselves or receive from others, and all precepts whatever which they presume to hand down on their own authority concerning the worship of God. We hold, therefore, that all constitutions are impious in whose observance the worship of God is claimed to be placed. The passages in Galatians in which he insists that fetters are not to be bound on the conscience (which ought to be ruled by God alone) are plain enough, especially chapter 5. Let it suffice, therefore, to refer to them.

9¶

But so that the whole matter may be made plainer by examples, it will be proper, before we go further, to apply the doctrine to our own times. The constitutions which they call ecclesiastical, and with which the Pope and his adherents burden the Church, we hold to be harmful and impious, while our opponents defend them as sacred and wholesome. Now there are two kinds of them: some relate to ceremonies and rites, and others more especially to discipline. Have we, then, any just cause for attacking both? Certainly a more just cause than we could wish. First, do not their authors themselves plainly declare that the very essence of the worship of God (so to speak) is contained in them? For what purpose do they put forward their ceremonies, except that God may be worshiped by them? Nor is this done merely through the error of the ignorant multitude, but with the approval of those who hold the place of teachers. I am not now speaking of the gross abominations by which they have schemed to corrupt all godliness; but they would not consider it so atrocious a crime to err in some minute tradition if they did not make the worship of God subordinate to their inventions. Since Paul, then, declares it intolerable that the lawful worship of God should be subjected to the will of men, in what do we err when we cannot tolerate this at present? Especially when we are commanded to worship God according to the elements of this world, a thing which Paul declares to be opposed to Christ (Colossians 2:20). On the other hand, the way in which they lay consciences under a strict necessity of observing whatever they command is well known. When we protest against this, we make common cause with Paul, who will on no account allow the consciences of believers to be brought under human bondage.

10¶

Moreover, the worst of all is that once religion begins to be made up of such vain inventions, this perversion is immediately followed by the abominable depravity for which our Lord rebuked the Pharisees, of making the commandment of God void through their traditions (Matthew 15:3). I do not wish to dispute with our present legislators in my own words; let them win the victory if they can clear themselves of this accusation of Christ. But how can they do so, seeing they regard it as immeasurably more wicked to let the year pass without auricular confession than to have spent it in the greatest wickedness; to have defiled the tongue with a slight taste of meat on Friday than to have polluted the whole body daily with fornication; to have put one's hand to honest labor on a day consecrated to one or another of their petty saints than to have used all one's members constantly in the greatest crimes; for a priest to be joined to a wife in lawful wedlock than to be engaged in a thousand adulteries; to have failed to perform a vowed pilgrimage than to have broken faith in every promise; not to have spent lavishly on the monstrous, superfluous, and useless luxury of churches than to have refused the poor in their greatest need; to have passed an idol without honoring it than to have treated the whole human race with contempt; not to have muttered long meaningless sentences at certain hours than never to have framed one proper prayer? What is meant by making the word of God void through tradition, if it is not done when, commending the ordinances of God only coldly and perfunctorily, they nevertheless eagerly and anxiously urge strict obedience to their own ordinances, as if the whole power of piety were contained in them? When, excusing the transgression of the divine law with trivial satisfactions, they punish the slightest violation of one of their decrees with nothing less than imprisonment, exile, fire, or sword? When, though neither severe nor unrelenting against the despisers of God, they persecute to the uttermost, with implacable hatred, those who despise themselves, and so train all those whose simplicity they hold in bondage that they would sooner see the whole law of God overthrown than one iota of what they call the precepts of the Church broken? First, there is a grievous offense in this, that one man despises, judges, and casts off his neighbor over trivial matters, matters which are free if the judgment of God is to decide. But now, as if this were a small evil, those frivolous elements of this world (as Paul calls them in his Epistle to the Galatians, Galatians 4:9) are counted of more value than the heavenly oracles of God. He who is all but acquitted for adultery is judged over meat; and he to whom fornication is permitted is forbidden to marry. This, indeed, is all that is gained by that double-dealing obedience, which turns away from God exactly as far as it leans toward men.

11¶

There are two other grave faults which we condemn in these constitutions. First, they prescribe observances which are in great part useless and sometimes absurd; second, by their vast number pious consciences are oppressed, and, being carried back to a kind of Judaism, they so cling to shadows that they cannot come to Christ. My charge that they are useless and absurd will, I know, scarcely be believed by carnal wisdom, which finds them so pleasing that the Church seems to be altogether disfigured when they are taken away. But this is just what Paul says, that they "have indeed a show of wisdom in will-worship, and humility, and neglecting of the body" (Colossians 2:23), a most wholesome warning which we ought never to lose sight of. Human traditions, he says, deceive by an appearance of wisdom. Where does this show come from? Simply from this: being framed by men, they are recognized by the human mind as its own, and once recognized it embraces them more willingly than anything, however good, that is less suited to its vanity. Second, they seem to be a fit training in humility, since they keep the minds of men groveling on the ground under their yoke; and so they have another recommendation. Last, because they seem to tend to curb the desires of the flesh and to subdue it by the rigor of abstinence, they appear to be wisely devised. But what does Paul say to all this? Does he pull off these masks so that the simple may not be deluded by a false pretext? Considering it enough for their refutation to say that they were inventions of men, he passes over all these things without refuting them, as things of no value. Indeed, because he knew that all invented worship is condemned in the Church, and is the more suspect to believers the more pleasing it is to the human mind; because he knew that this false show of outward humility differs so widely from true humility that it can easily be detected; and finally, because he knew that this tutelage is valued no more highly than bodily exercise, he wished the very things that commended human traditions to the ignorant to be regarded by believers as their refutation.

12¶

So at present not only the unlearned common people, but everyone in proportion as he is puffed up with worldly wisdom, is wonderfully captivated by the glare of ceremonies, while hypocrites and silly women think nothing better or more beautiful can be imagined. But those who examine them thoroughly and weigh them more truly by the rule of godliness know, concerning the value of all such ceremonies, first, that they are trifles of no use; and second, that they are impostures which delude the eyes of the onlookers with empty show. I am speaking of those ceremonies which the Roman masters insist are great mysteries, while we know by experience that they are mere mockery. Nor is it strange that their authors have gone so far as to delude themselves and others with sheer frivolities, since they have taken their model partly from the dreams of the Gentiles, and partly, like apes, have rashly imitated the ancient rites of the Mosaic law, with which we have no more to do than with the sacrifices of animals and other similar things. Certainly, even if there were no other proof, no sane man would expect any good from such an ill-assorted jumble. And the case itself plainly shows that very many ceremonies have no other use than to stupefy the people rather than to teach them. In the same way, hypocrites attach great importance to those new canons which pervert discipline rather than preserve it; but closer examination will show that they are nothing but the shadowy and fleeting phantom of discipline.

13¶

To come to the second fault: who does not see that ceremonies, by being heaped one upon another, have grown to such a multitude that it is impossible to tolerate them in the Christian Church? This is why a strange mixture of Judaism is apparent in ceremonies, while other observances prove a deadly snare to pious minds. Augustine complained that in his time, while the precepts of God were neglected, prejudice prevailed everywhere to such an extent that a man who touched the ground barefoot during his octave was censured more severely than one who drowned his wits in wine. He complained that the Church, which God in his mercy wished to be free, was so oppressed that the condition of the Jews was more tolerable (Augustine, Letter 119). Had that holy man lived in our day, in what terms would he have lamented the bondage that now exists? For the number is ten times greater, and each iota is exacted a hundred times more rigidly than it was then. This is the usual course: once those perverse legislators have usurped authority, they make no end of their commands and prohibitions until they reach the extreme of harshness. Paul indicated this elegantly in these words: "If ye be dead with Christ from the rudiments of the world, why, as though living in the world, are ye subject to ordinances? Touch not, taste not, handle not" (Colossians 2:20, 21). For while the word ἅπτεσθαι means both to eat and to touch, it is no doubt taken here in the former sense, so that there may not be a superfluous repetition. Here, therefore, he describes most admirably the progress of the false apostles. The way superstition begins is this: they forbid not only eating, but even chewing gently; once they have gained this, they forbid even tasting. When this too has been yielded to them, they consider it unlawful to touch even with the finger.

14¶

We rightly condemn this tyranny in human constitutions, by which miserable consciences are strangely tormented through countless edicts and the excessive exaction of them. Of the canons relating to discipline we have spoken elsewhere (above, section 12; also chapter 12). What shall I say of ceremonies, whose effect has been that we have almost buried Christ and gone back to Jewish figures? "Our Lord Christ," says Augustine (Letter 118), "bound together the fellowship of his new people by sacraments very few in number, most excellent in meaning, and most easy to observe." How far this simplicity differs from the multitude and variety of rites in which we see the Church entangled at present can hardly be told. I am aware of the device by which some clever men excuse this perversity. They say that there are many among us as rude as any among the people of Israel, and that this tutelage has been introduced for their sakes; and that although the stronger may do without it, they ought not to neglect it, since it is useful to weak brothers. I answer that we are not unaware of what is owed to the weakness of brothers; but on the other hand we object that the way to provide for the weak is not to bury them under a great mass of ceremonies. It was not without reason that God made a distinction between us and his ancient people, training them like children by means of signs and figures, and training us more simply, without so much outward show. Paul's words are, "The heir, as long as he is a child" "is under tutors and governors" (Galatians 4:1, 2). This was the state of the Jews under the law. But we are like adults who, having been freed from guardians and trustees, have no need of childish rudiments. God certainly foresaw what kind of people he would have in his Church and how they were to be governed. Now, he distinguished between us and the Jews in the way that has been described. It is therefore a foolish way of providing for the ignorant to set up the Judaism which Christ has abolished. Christ expressed this difference between his ancient and his new people when he said to the woman of Samaria, "The hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth" (John 4:23). This, no doubt, had always been done; but the new worshipers differ from the old in this: while under Moses the spiritual worship of God was shadowed and, as it were, entangled by many ceremonies, these have been abolished, and worship is now simpler. Those, therefore, who confuse this distinction overturn the order instituted and sanctioned by Christ. You will ask, then, Are no ceremonies to be given to the more ignorant as a help to their ignorance? I do not say so, for I think that help of this kind is very useful to them. All I contend for is the use of such a measure as may illustrate Christ, not obscure him. Hence a few ceremonies have been appointed by God, and these by no means burdensome, in order that they may show forth a present Christ. To the Jews a greater number were given, that they might be images of an absent Christ. In saying he was absent, I mean not in power but in the mode of expression. Therefore, to secure proper moderation, it is necessary to keep that fewness in number, ease of observance, and significance of meaning which consists in clearness. What is the use of saying that this is not done? The fact is plain to every eye.

15¶

I say nothing here of the harmful opinions with which men's minds are filled, such as that these are sacrifices by which propitiation is made to God, by which sins are expiated, and by which righteousness and salvation are obtained. It will be argued that things good in themselves are not corrupted by errors of this kind, since similar errors may be committed in acts expressly commanded by God. Yet nothing is more unfitting than this: that works devised by the will of man are held in such esteem as to be thought worthy of eternal life. The works commanded by God receive a reward because the Lawgiver himself accepts them as marks of obedience. They do not, therefore, take their value from their own dignity or their own merit, but from the fact that God sets this high value on our obedience toward him. I am speaking here of that perfection of works which is commanded by God but is not performed by men. The works of the law are accepted solely by the free kindness of God, because the obedience is weak and defective. But since we are not considering here how far works avail without Christ, let us leave that question aside. I repeat once more, as properly belonging to the present subject, that whatever commendation works have, they have it in respect of obedience, which alone God regards, as he testifies through the prophet: "I spake not unto your fathers, nor commanded them in the day that I brought them out of the land of Egypt, concerning burnt-offerings or sacrifices: but this thing commanded I them, saying, Obey my voice" (Jeremiah 7:22). Of invented works he says elsewhere, "Wherefore do you spend your money for that which is not bread?" (Isaiah 55:2; 29:13). Again, "In vain do they worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men" (Matthew 15:9). They cannot, therefore, clear themselves of the charge of allowing wretched people to seek in these outward frivolities a righteousness which they may present to God and by which they may stand before the heavenly tribunal. Besides, is it not a fault deservedly branded, that they display meaningless ceremonies as a kind of stage play or magical incantation? For it is certain that all ceremonies which do not direct men to Christ are corrupt and harmful. But the ceremonies in use in the Papacy are separated from doctrine, so that they confine men to signs entirely void of meaning. Last of all (as the belly is an ingenious contriver), it is clear that many of their ceremonies have been invented by greedy priests as lures for catching money. But whatever their origin, they are all so prostituted to filthy gain that a great part of them must be abolished if we would prevent a profane and sacrilegious traffic from being carried on in the Church.

16¶

Although I seem not to be setting out the general doctrine concerning human constitutions, but to be fitting my discourse entirely to our own age, nothing has been said that may not be useful to every age. For whenever men begin the superstitious practice of worshiping God with their own inventions, all the laws enacted for this purpose at once degenerate into these gross abuses. For the curse which God pronounces, namely, to strike with stupor and blindness those who worship him with the doctrines of men, is not confined to any one age but applies to all ages. The constant result of this blindness is that there is no kind of absurdity which those escape who, despising the many warnings of God, of their own accord entangle themselves in these deadly fetters. But if, without any regard to circumstances, you would simply know the character belonging at all times to those human traditions which ought to be rejected by the Church and condemned by all the godly, Calvin on the Necessity of Reforming the Church.

17¶

I know what their answer will be, namely, that these traditions are not from themselves but from God. For to keep the Church from erring, it is guided by the Holy Spirit, whose authority resides in them. Once this is conceded, it follows at the same time that their traditions are revelations of the Holy Spirit and cannot be disregarded without impiety and contempt of God. And so that they may not seem to have attempted anything without high authority, they would have it believed that a great part of their observances is derived from the apostles. For they contend that in one instance they have sufficient proof of what the apostles did in other cases. The instance is this: when the apostles, assembled in council, announced to all the Gentiles, as the judgment of the council, that they should "abstain from pollution of idols, and from fornication, and from things strangled, and from blood" (Acts 15:20, 29). We have already explained how, in order to exalt themselves, they falsely assume the name of the Church (chapter 8, sections 10-13). If, in the present case, we strip away all masks and glosses (which indeed ought to be our first care, and is also our highest interest), and consider what kind of Church Christ wishes to have, so that we may form and adapt ourselves to it as a standard, it will readily appear that it is not a property of the Church to disregard the limits of the word of God and to run riot and indulge itself in making new laws. Does not the law which was once given to the Church endure forever? "What things soever I command you, observe to do it: you shall not add thereto, nor diminish from it" (Deuteronomy 12:32). And in another place, "Add not unto his words, lest he reprove you, and you be found a liar" (Proverbs 30:6). Since they cannot deny that this was said to the Church, what else do they proclaim but their own defiance when, in spite of such prohibitions, they profess to add to the doctrine of God and dare to mix their own with it? Far be it from us to assent to the falsehood by which they offer such insult to the Church. Let us understand that the name of the Church is falsely put forward wherever men contend for that rash human license which cannot keep itself within the boundaries prescribed by the word of God, but insolently breaks out and has recourse to its own inventions. In the passage above there is nothing involved, nothing obscure, nothing ambiguous: the whole Church is forbidden to add to or take from the word of God in matters of his worship and his saving precepts. But, they say, that was said merely of the law, which was succeeded by the prophets and the whole gospel dispensation! This I admit; but I add at the same time that these are fulfillments of the law rather than additions or subtractions. Now, if the Lord did not permit anything to be added to or taken from the ministry of Moses, wrapped up though it was, if I may so speak, in many folds of obscurity, until he should supply a clearer doctrine through his servants the prophets, and at last through his beloved Son, why should we not suppose that we are much more strictly forbidden to make any addition to the Law, the Prophets, the Psalms, and the Gospel? The Lord cannot forget himself, and he declared long ago that nothing offends him so much as being worshiped by human inventions. Hence those famous declarations of the prophets, which ought to ring continually in our ears: "I spake not unto your fathers, nor commanded them in the day that I brought them out of the land of Egypt, concerning burnt-offerings or sacrifices; but this thing commanded I them, saying, Obey my voice, and I will be your God, and ye shall be my people: and walk ye in all the ways that I have commanded you" (Jeremiah 7:22, 23). "I earnestly protested unto your fathers, in the day that I brought them out of the land of Egypt, even unto this day, rising early and protesting, saying, Obey my voice" (Jeremiah 11:7). There are other passages of the same kind, but the best known of all is this: "Hath the Lord as great delight in burnt-offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the Lord? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to hearken than the fat of rams. For rebellion is as the sin of witchcraft, and stubbornness is as iniquity and idolatry" (1 Samuel 15:22, 23). It is easy, therefore, to prove that whenever human inventions in this matter are defended by the authority of the Church, they cannot be cleared of the charge of impiety, and that the name of the Church is falsely assumed.

18¶

For this reason we freely denounce that tyranny of human traditions which is arrogantly thrust upon us in the name of the Church. Nor do we hold the Church in derision (as our adversaries unjustly accuse us, in order to stir up ill will); rather, we ascribe to her the praise of obedience, than which she acknowledges no greater praise. It is they, rather, who do grave injury to the Church by representing her as defiant toward her Lord, when they pretend that she goes further than the word of God allows; to say nothing of their combined impudence and malice in continually shouting about the power of the Church while they meanwhile conceal both the command which the Lord has given her and the obedience she owes to that command. But if our desire is, as it ought to be, to agree with the Church, it is of more importance to consider and remember the charge which the Lord has given both to us and to the Church, to obey him with one consent. For there can be no doubt that we shall agree best with the Church when we show ourselves obedient to the Lord in all things. But to ascribe the origin of the traditions by which the Church has hitherto been oppressed to the apostles is sheer imposture, since the whole substance of the apostles' doctrine is that the conscience must not be burdened with new observances, nor the worship of God defiled by our inventions. Further, if any credit is to be given to ancient histories and records, what they attribute to the apostles was not only unknown to them but was never even heard of by them. Nor let them pretend that most of their decrees, though not delivered in writing, were received through use and practice, as things which the apostles could not understand while Christ was in the world, but which they learned after his ascension by the revelation of the Holy Spirit. The meaning of that passage has been explained elsewhere (chapter 8, section 14). In the present question they make themselves truly ridiculous, since it is plain that all those mysteries which remained so long undiscovered by the apostles are partly Jewish or Gentile observances, the former of which had long ago been published among the Jews and the latter among all the Gentiles; and partly absurd gestures and empty ceremonies, which stupid priests who have neither sense nor learning can duly perform; indeed, which children and mountebanks perform so fittingly that it seems impossible to have more suitable priests for such sacrifices. Even if there were no records, men of sense would judge from the very nature of the case that such a mass of rites and observances did not rush into the Church all at once, but crept in gradually. For though the venerable bishops who were nearest in time to the apostles introduced some things pertaining to order and discipline, those who came after them, and those who came after these again, had too little judgment and too much curiosity and greed, each latecomer always competing in foolish rivalry with his predecessors so as not to be outdone in inventing novelties. And because there was a danger that these inventions, for which they expected praise from posterity, might soon fall out of use, they were much more rigorous in insisting on their observance. This false zeal has produced a great part of the rites which these men represent as apostolic. History bears witness to this.

19¶

And so as not to become tedious by giving a catalog of them all, we shall be content with one example. Under the apostles there was great simplicity in administering the Lord's Supper. Their immediate successors added some things to the dignity of the ordinance, which are not to be condemned. Afterward came foolish imitators who, by continually patching various fragments together, have left us those priestly vestments which we see in the Mass, those altar ornaments, those gestures, and the whole jumble of useless observances. French, "Mais depuis sont survenus d'autres singes, qui ont eu une folle affectation de coudre piece sur piece, et ainsi ont composé tant les accoustremens du prestre, que les paremens de l'autel, et le badinage et jeu de farce que nous voyons à present à la Messe, avec tout le reste du borgage." — But other apes have since appeared, who have had a foolish fondness for sewing piece to piece, and so have put together all the furnishings of the priests, as well as the altar ornaments, the trifling and farce play which we now see in the Mass, with all the other trimmings.

20¶

So as not to be tedious, I will give only one example. If anyone asks them where they get their holy water, they will answer at once: from the apostles. As if I did not know who the Roman bishop is to whom history ascribes the invention, and who, if he had admitted the apostles to his counsel, would certainly never have corrupted baptism with a foreign and unseasonable symbol; although it does not seem likely to me that the origin of that consecration is as ancient as is there recorded. For when Augustine says (Letter 118) that certain churches in his day rejected the formal imitation of Christ in the washing of feet, lest that rite should seem to pertain to baptism, he indicates that there was then no kind of washing which bore any resemblance to baptism. However that may be, I will never admit that the apostolic spirit gave rise to that daily sign by which baptism, while it is called back to remembrance, is in a manner repeated. I attach no importance to the fact that Augustine elsewhere ascribes other things to the apostles. For since he has nothing better than conjecture, it is not sufficient for forming a judgment on a matter of such importance. Lastly, even if we were to grant that the things he mentions come from the apostolic age, there is a great difference between instituting some exercise of piety which believers may use with a free conscience, or may abstain from if they think its observance not useful, and enacting a law which brings the conscience into bondage. Now, indeed, whoever the author may be from whom they come, since we see the great abuses to which they have led, nothing prevents us from abolishing them without any reproach to him, since he never commended them in such a way as to lay us under a fixed and unchangeable obligation to observe them.

21¶

It gives them no great help in defending their tyranny to put forward the example of the apostles. According to them, the apostles and elders of the early Church sanctioned a decree without any authority from Christ, by which they commanded all the Gentiles to abstain from meat offered to idols, from things strangled, and from blood (Acts 15:20). If this was lawful for them, why should their successors not be allowed to follow the example as often as occasion requires? Would that they always followed them, both in this and in other matters! For I am ready to prove on solid grounds that nothing new was instituted or decreed here by the apostles. For when Peter declares in that council that God is tempted if a yoke is laid on the necks of the disciples, he overthrows his own argument if he afterward allows a yoke to be imposed on them. But it is imposed if the apostles, on their own authority, forbid the Gentiles to touch meat offered to idols, things strangled, and blood. The difficulty still remains that they nevertheless seem to forbid them. But this will easily be removed by attending more closely to the meaning of their decree. The first thing in order, and the chief thing in importance, is that the Gentiles were to keep their liberty, which was not to be disturbed, and that they were not to be troubled with the observances of the law. So far the decree is entirely in our favor. The reservation which immediately follows is not a new law enacted by the apostles, but a divine and eternal command of God against the violation of charity, which does not take away one iota from that liberty. It only reminds the Gentiles how they are to accommodate themselves to their brothers and not to abuse their liberty as an occasion of offense. Let the second head, therefore, be that the Gentiles are to use a harmless liberty, giving no offense to the brothers. Still, however, they prescribe something definite, namely, they show and point out, as was expedient at the time, which things may give offense to their brothers, so that they may avoid them; but they add nothing new of their own to the eternal law of God which forbids giving offense to the brothers.

22¶

Suppose faithful pastors, presiding over churches not yet well established, were to instruct their flocks not to eat meat on Friday until the weak among whom they live become strong, or not to work on a holy day, or any other similar things. Although these matters, when superstition is laid aside, are in themselves indifferent, still, where offense is given to the brothers, they cannot be done without sin; for there are times when believers cannot set this example before weak brothers without most grievously wounding their consciences. Who but a slanderer would say that a new law is enacted by those who, it is evident, are only guarding against scandals which their Master has expressly forbidden? But nothing more than this can be said of the apostles, who had no other purpose in removing occasions of offense than to enforce the divine law which forbids offense; as if they had said, "The Lord has commanded you not to hurt a weak brother; but you cannot eat meats offered to idols, things strangled, and blood without offending weak brothers; we therefore require you, in the word of the Lord, not to eat with offense." And the best witness to prove that the apostles had this in view is Paul, who writes as follows, no doubt in keeping with the judgment of the council: "As concerning, therefore, the eating of those things which are offered in sacrifice unto idols, we know that an idol is nothing in the world, and that there is none other God but one." "Howbeit, there is not in every man that knowledge: for some with conscience of the idol unto this hour eat it as a thing offered unto an idol; and their conscience being weak is defiled." "But take heed lest by any means this liberty of yours become a stumbling-block to them that are weak" (1 Corinthians 8:4-9). Anyone who rightly considers these things will not be taken in by the gloss these men use when, as a cloak for their tyranny, they pretend that the apostles had begun by their decree to infringe the liberty of the Church. But so that they may not escape without admitting the accuracy of this explanation, let them tell me by what authority they have dared to abolish this very decree. It was, it seems, because there was no longer any danger of those offenses and dissensions which the apostles wished to prevent, and they knew that a law is to be judged by its end. Since, therefore, the law was passed with a view to charity, nothing is prescribed in it except so far as charity requires. In confessing that the transgression of this law is nothing but a violation of charity, do they not acknowledge at the same time that it was not some extraneous supplement to the law of God, but a genuine and simple adaptation of it to the times and customs for which it was intended?

23¶

But though such laws are a hundred times unjust and injurious to us, they still contend that they are to be obeyed without exception; for what is asked of us, they say, is not to consent to errors, but only to submit to the strict commands of those set over us, commands which we are not at liberty to refuse (1 Peter 2:18). But here also the Lord comes to the help of his word and frees us from this bondage by asserting the liberty which he has purchased for us by his sacred blood, and whose benefit he has attested more than once in his word. For what is required of us is not (as they maliciously pretend) to endure some grievous oppression in our body, but to be tortured in our consciences and brought into bondage; in other words, to be robbed of the benefits of Christ's blood. Let us pass over this, however, as if it were beside the point. Do we think it a small matter that the Lord is deprived of his kingdom, which he so strictly claims for himself? Now, he is deprived of it as often as he is worshiped with laws of human invention, since it is his will to be the sole legislator of his worship. And lest anyone should think this of small importance, let us hear how the Lord himself values it: "Forasmuch as this people draw near me with their mouth, and with their lips do honour me, but have removed their heart far from me, and their fear toward me is taught by the precept of men: therefore, behold, I will proceed to do a marvellous work among the people, even a marvellous work and a wonder; for the wisdom of their wise men shall perish, and the understanding of their prudent men shall be hid" (Isaiah 29:13-14). And in another place, "But in vain do they worship me, teaching for doctrines the commandments of men" (Matthew 15:9). And indeed, when the children of Israel defiled themselves with manifold idolatries, the cause of the whole evil is ascribed to that impure mixture which came from their disregarding the commandments of God and framing new modes of worship. Accordingly, sacred history relates that the new inhabitants who had been brought by the king of Assyria from Babylon to inhabit Samaria were torn and destroyed by wild beasts because they did not know the judgment or statutes of the God of that land (2 Kings 17:24-34). Even if they had done nothing wrong in ceremonies, their empty show could not have been approved by God. Meanwhile he did not cease to punish them for the violation of his worship through the introduction of inventions foreign to his word. Hence it is said afterward that, terrified by the punishment, they adopted the rites prescribed in the law; but because they did not yet worship God purely, it is twice repeated that they feared him and did not fear him. From this we infer that part of the reverence due to him consists in worshiping him simply in the way he commands, without mixing in any inventions of our own. And accordingly, godly kings are repeatedly praised (2 Kings 22:1, etc.) for acting according to all his precepts and not turning aside either to the right hand or to the left. I go further: although there may be no open display of impiety in invented worship, it is strictly condemned by the Spirit, inasmuch as it is a departure from the command of God. The altar of Ahaz, a model of which had been brought from Damascus (2 Kings 16:10), might have seemed to give added ornament to the temple, since it was his intention to offer sacrifices there to God alone, and to do so more splendidly than at the first, ancient altar; yet we see how the Spirit detests the audacious attempt, for no other reason than that human inventions in the worship of God are impure corruptions. And the more clearly the will of God has been made known to us, the less excusable is our insolence in attempting anything. Accordingly, the guilt of Manasseh is made heavier by the fact that he erected a new altar at Jerusalem, of which the Lord had said, "In Jerusalem will I put my name" (2 Kings 22:3, 4), because the authority of God was thereby openly rejected.

24¶

Many wonder why God threatens so sternly that he will bring astonishment on the people who worship him with the commandments of men, and declares that it is in vain to worship him with the commandments of men. But if they would consider what it means, in the matter of religion, that is, of heavenly wisdom, to depend on God alone, they would see at the same time that it is not on slight grounds that the Lord abhors perverse service of this kind, offered to him at the whim of the human will. For although there is some show of humility in the obedience of those who obey such laws in worshiping God, yet they are by no means humble, since they prescribe to him the very laws which they observe. This is why Paul would have us beware so carefully of being deceived by the traditions of men and by what is called ἐθελοθρησκεία, that is, voluntary worship, worship devised by men without warrant from God. So it is indeed: we must become fools with respect to our own wisdom and all the wisdom of men, so that we may allow him alone to be wise. This course is by no means kept by those who seek to commend themselves to him by paltry observances of man's devising, and who, as it were, thrust upon him against his will a double-dealing obedience which is rendered to men. This is the course that has been followed for several ages, and within our own memory, and is still followed at present in those places where the power of the creature counts for more than that of the Creator, where religion (if it deserves to be called religion) is defiled with more numerous and more absurd superstitions than paganism ever was. For what could human sense produce but things carnal and foolish, smacking of their authors?

25¶

When the defenders of superstition cloak them by pointing out that Samuel sacrificed in Ramah, and that although he did so contrary to the law he nevertheless pleased God (1 Samuel 7:17), it is easy to answer that he did not set up any second altar in opposition to the only true one; but, since the place for the ark of the covenant had not yet been fixed, he sacrificed in the town where he lived, as being the most convenient. It certainly was never the intention of the holy prophet to make any innovation in sacred things, to which the Lord had so strictly forbidden any addition or subtraction. The case of Manoah I consider to have been extraordinary and special. Though a private man, he offered sacrifice to God, and did so not without approval, because he did it not from a rash impulse of his own mind but by divine inspiration (Judges 13:19). How greatly God abhors all the devices of men in his worship, we have a striking proof in the case of one not inferior to Manoah, namely, Gideon, whose ephod brought ruin not only on himself and his family but on the whole people (Judges 8:27). In short, every extraneous invention by which men wish to worship God is nothing else than a defilement of true holiness.

26¶

Why then, they ask, did Christ say that the intolerable burdens imposed by the scribes and Pharisees were to be borne (Matthew 23:3)? Indeed, rather, why did he say in another place that we were to beware of the leaven of the Pharisees (Matthew 16:6)? By leaven, as the evangelist Matthew explains it, he meant whatever human doctrine is mixed with the pure word of God. What could be plainer than that we are commanded to shun and beware of their whole doctrine? From this it is entirely certain that in the other passage our Lord never meant that the consciences of his people were to be harassed by the mere traditions of the Pharisees. And the words themselves, unless they are twisted, have no such meaning. Our Lord, indeed, beginning to denounce the conduct of the Pharisees, first instructs his hearers simply that, although they saw nothing to follow in the lives of the Pharisees, they should not cease to do what the Pharisees taught by word when they sat in the seat of Moses, that is, when they expounded the law. All he meant, therefore, was to guard the common people against being led by the bad example of their teachers to despise doctrine. But since some are not moved at all by reason and always demand authority, I will quote a passage from Augustine in which the very same thing is expressed: "The Lord's sheepfold has persons set over it, of whom some are faithful and others hirelings. Those who are faithful are true shepherds; learn, however, that hirelings also are necessary. For many in the Church, pursuing temporal advantages, preach Christ, and the voice of Christ is heard through them, and the sheep follow not a hireling, but the shepherd by means of a hireling. Learn that hirelings were pointed out by the Lord himself. The scribes and Pharisees, he says, sit in Moses' seat; what they tell you, do, but what they do, do not do. What is this but to say, Hear the voice of the shepherd by means of hirelings? Sitting in the chair, they teach the law of God, and therefore God teaches through them; but if they choose to teach their own, do not hear, do not do." Thus far Augustine. (Augustine, On John, Tractate 46.)

27¶

But since very many ignorant people, on hearing that it is impious to bind the conscience and vain to worship God with human traditions, apply one blot to all the laws by which the order of the Church is established, it will be proper to correct their error. Here, indeed, the danger of mistake is great, for it is not easy to see at first sight how widely the two things differ. But I will make the matter so clear in a few words that no one will be deceived by the resemblance. First, then, let us understand this: if in every human society some kind of government is necessary to secure the common peace and maintain concord; if in conducting business some form must always be observed which public decency, and so humanity itself, requires us not to disregard; then this ought especially to be observed in churches, which are best sustained by a constitution well ordered in every respect, and without which concord cannot exist. Therefore, if we would provide for the safety of the Church, we must always pay careful attention to Paul's command that all things be done decently and in order (1 Corinthians 14:40). But since there is such diversity in the customs of men, such variety in their minds, such conflict in their judgments and dispositions, no polity is firm enough unless it is fortified by certain laws, nor can any rite be observed without a fixed form. So far, therefore, are we from condemning the laws that serve this end, that we hold that removing them would unnerve the Church, disfigure it, and scatter it entirely. For Paul's command that all things be done decently and in order cannot be observed unless order and decency are secured by adding ordinances as a kind of bonds. In these ordinances, however, we must always keep the exception in view: they must not be thought necessary to salvation, nor lay the conscience under a religious obligation; they must not be compared to the worship of God, nor substituted for piety.

28¶

We have, therefore, a most excellent and sure mark for distinguishing between those impious constitutions (by which, as we have said, true religion is overthrown and the conscience subverted) and the lawful observances of the Church, if we remember that one of two things, or both together, are always intended, namely, that in the sacred assembly of the faithful all things may be done decently and with fitting dignity, and that human society may be kept in order by certain bonds, as it were, of moderation and humanity. For when a law is understood to have been made for the sake of public decency, there is no room for the superstition into which those fall who measure the worship of God by human inventions. On the other hand, when a law is known to be intended for common use, that false notion of its obligation and necessity, which greatly alarms the conscience when traditions are thought necessary to salvation, is overthrown, since nothing is sought here but the maintenance of charity by a common duty. But it may be proper to explain more clearly what is meant by the decency which Paul commends, and also what is included under order. The aim of decency is partly that, by the use of rites which produce reverence in sacred matters, we may be stirred up to piety, and partly that the modesty and gravity which ought to be seen in all honorable actions may be especially conspicuous here. In order, the first thing is that those who preside know the law and rule of right government, while those who are governed are accustomed to obedience and right discipline. The second thing is that, by rightly arranging the state of the Church, provision may be made for peace and tranquility.

29¶

We shall not, therefore, give the name of decency to what only supplies an empty pleasure, such as is seen, for example, in that theatrical display which the Papists put on in their public worship, where nothing appears but a mask of useless splendor and luxury without any fruit. But we give the name of decency to what, being suited to the reverence of sacred mysteries, forms a fit exercise for piety, or at least provides an ornament suited to the action, and is not without fruit, but reminds believers of the great modesty, seriousness, and reverence with which sacred things ought to be handled. Moreover, ceremonies, in order to be exercises of piety, must lead us directly to Christ. In the same way, we shall not make order consist in that trifling pomp which offers nothing but fleeting splendor, but in that arrangement which removes all confusion, barbarism, defiance, all turbulence and dissension. Of the first class we have examples (1 Corinthians 11:5, 21) where Paul says that profane feasting must not be mixed with the sacred Supper of the Lord, and that women must not appear in public uncovered. And there are many other things which we have in daily practice, such as praying on our knees and with our heads uncovered; administering the sacraments of the Lord not shabbily but with some degree of dignity; using some degree of solemnity in the burial of our dead; and so forth. In the other class are the hours set apart for public prayer, preaching, and solemn services; quiet and silence during the sermon; fixed places; the singing of hymns; days set apart for the celebration of the Lord's Supper; Paul's prohibition of women teaching in the Church; and the like. To the same list may especially be referred those things which preserve discipline, such as catechizing, ecclesiastical censures, excommunication, fasting, and so on. Thus all ecclesiastical constitutions which we admit to be sacred and wholesome may be reduced to two heads, the one relating to rites and ceremonies, the other to discipline and peace.

30¶

But since there is a danger here, on the one hand, that false bishops may draw from this a pretext for their impious and tyrannical laws, and, on the other, that some who are too quick to take alarm may, for fear of the evils described above, leave no room for laws, however holy, it may be proper to declare here that I approve only of those human constitutions which are founded on the authority of God and drawn from Scripture, and are therefore wholly divine. Let us take as an example the kneeling that is done in public prayer. It is asked whether this is a human tradition which anyone is at liberty to reject or neglect. I say that it is human and at the same time divine. It is of God inasmuch as it is a part of that decency whose care and observance the apostle commends; and it is of men inasmuch as it specifically determines what was indicated in general rather than spelled out. From this one example we may judge what is to be thought of the whole class, namely, that the whole sum of righteousness, all the parts of divine worship, and everything necessary to salvation the Lord has faithfully included and clearly unfolded in his sacred oracles, so that in these he alone is the one Master to be heard. But since in outward discipline and ceremonies he has not been pleased to prescribe in detail every particular we ought to observe (he foresaw that this depended on the circumstances of the times, and that one form would not suit all ages), in these we must have recourse to the general rules he has given, using them to test whatever the needs of the Church may require to be enjoined for order and decency. Lastly, since he has not given any express command, because things of this kind are not necessary to salvation and, for the edification of the Church, ought to be adapted to the changing circumstances of each age and nation, it will be proper, as the interest of the Church may require, both to change and abolish old forms and to introduce new ones. I admit, indeed, that we are not to innovate rashly or constantly, or for trivial reasons. Charity is the best judge of what tends to harm or to edify; if we allow her to be our guide, all things will be safe.

31¶

Things which have been appointed according to this rule the Christian people are bound to observe with a free conscience, indeed, and without superstition, but also with a pious and ready disposition to obey. They are not to hold them in contempt, nor to pass them over with careless indifference, much less to violate them openly in pride and defiance. You will ask, What liberty of conscience will there be in such careful observances? Indeed, this liberty will show itself admirably when we hold that these are not fixed and perpetual obligations to which we are bound, but outward rudiments for human weakness, which, though we do not all need them, we all nevertheless use, because we are bound to cherish mutual charity toward one another. We may recognize this in the examples given above. What? Is religion placed in a woman's bonnet, so that it is unlawful for her to go out with her head uncovered? Is her silence fixed by a decree which cannot be broken without the greatest wickedness? Is there any mystery in kneeling, or in burying a dead body, which cannot be omitted without a crime? By no means. For if a woman must make such haste in helping a neighbor that she has no time to cover her head, she does not sin in running out with her head uncovered. And there are some occasions on which it is no less fitting for her to speak than on others to be silent. Nothing, moreover, forbids a man who cannot bend his knees because of disease from praying standing. In short, it is better to bury a dead man quickly than, for lack of grave clothes or for the absence of those who should attend the funeral, to wait until the body rots away unburied. Nevertheless, in these matters the custom and institutions of the country, in short, humanity and the rules of modesty itself, declare what is to be done or avoided. Here, if any error is committed through carelessness or forgetfulness, no crime is committed; but if it is done out of contempt, such defiance must be condemned. In the same way, it is of no consequence what the days and hours are, what kind of buildings are used, and what psalms are sung on each day. But it is proper that there should be certain days and set hours, and a place fit for receiving everyone, if any regard is had for the preservation of peace. For what a seedbed of quarrels confusion in such matters will be if everyone is allowed to alter at his pleasure what pertains to common order! All will not be satisfied with the same course if matters placed, as it were, on debatable ground are left to the decision of individuals. But if anyone here becomes clamorous and would be wiser than he ought, let him consider how he will justify his peevishness to the Lord. Paul's answer ought to satisfy us: "If any man seem to be contentious, we have no such custom, neither the churches of God."

32¶

Moreover, we must use the utmost diligence to prevent any error from creeping in that may either taint or tarnish this pure use. In this we shall succeed if whatever observances we use are plainly useful and very few in number, especially if to this is added the teaching of a faithful pastor, which may shut out erroneous opinions. The effect of this procedure is that in all these matters each one keeps his freedom, and yet at the same time voluntarily subjects it to a kind of necessity, so far as the decency of which we have spoken, or charity, requires. Next, so that in the observance of these things we may not fall into any superstition, nor rigidly demand too much from others, let us not imagine that the worship of God is improved by a multitude of ceremonies; and let not church despise church because of a difference in outward discipline. Lastly, instead of laying down any perpetual law for ourselves here, let us refer the whole end and use of observances to the edification of the Church; and at the Church's request let us, without offense, allow not only something to be changed, but even observances formerly in use to be overturned. For the present age is proof that the nature of the times allows certain rites, not otherwise impious or unbecoming, to be abolished as circumstances require. Such was the ignorance and blindness of former times, with such erroneous ideas and stubborn zeal did churches formerly cling to ceremonies, that they can scarcely be purged of monstrous superstitions without the removal of many ceremonies which were formerly established, not without cause, and which in themselves are not chargeable with any impiety.

A Word for 2026 · Uniting, Serving, Mentoring and Counseling Ministries

God alone is the Lawgiver of the conscience, and Calvin labors here to free the believer from every fetter that men would bind upon the soul in the name of religion. The Church may order her common life decently and in order, but she may not invent worship or make her traditions necessary to salvation — for Christ has set the conscience free, and to bind it again is to invade his kingdom. This rings with peculiar force in 2026, where worship is endlessly multiplied into spectacle and the simple ordinances Christ gave can be buried under human additions and the relentless pressure to perform. Calvin's standard is liberating and exacting at once: few rites, easy to observe, clear in meaning, all leading directly to Christ. In the home, worship God as he has commanded, not as the age prefers; teach your children the difference between what charity and decency require and what only God may impose. Let charity be your guide in things indifferent, and your conscience bow to no master but the Lord who bought it.
