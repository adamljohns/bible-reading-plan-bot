Of Bearing the Cross — One Branch of Self-Denial

The four divisions of this chapter are,—I. The nature of the cross, its necessity and dignity, sec. 1, 2. II. The manifold advantages of the cross described, sec. 3-6. III. The form of the cross the most excellent of all, and yet it by no means removes all sense of pain, sec. 7, 8. IV. A description of warfare under the cross, and of true patience (not that of philosophers), after the example of Christ, sec. 9-11. 1. What the cross is. By whom, and on whom, and for what cause imposed. Its necessity and dignity. 2. The cross necessary. 1. To humble our pride. 2. To make us apply to God for aid. Example of David. 3. To give us experience of God’s presence. 3. Manifold uses of the cross. 1. Produces patience, hope, and firm confidence in God, gives us victory and perseverance. Faith invincible. 4. 2. Frames us to obedience. Example of Abraham. This training how useful. 5. The cross necessary to subdue the wantonness of the flesh. This portrayed by an apposite simile. Various forms of the cross. 6. 3. God permits our infirmities, and corrects past faults, that he may keep us in obedience. This confirmed by a passage from Solomon and an Apostle. 7. Singular consolation under the cross, when we suffer persecution for righteousness. Some parts of this consolation. 8. This form of the cross most appropriate to believers, and should be borne willingly and cheerfully. This cheerfulness is not unfeeling hilarity, but, while groaning under the burden, waits patiently for the Lord. 9. A description of this conflict. Opposed to the vanity of the Stoics. Illustrated by the authority and example of Christ. 10. Proved by the testimony and uniform experience of the elect. Also by the special example of the Apostle Peter. The nature of the patience required of us. 11. Distinction between the patience of Christians and philosophers. The latter pretend a necessity which cannot be resisted. The former hold forth the justice of God and his care of our safety. A full exposition of this difference.

1¶

The godly mind must rise still higher, namely, to where Christ calls his disciples when he says that every one of them must "take up his cross" (Matthew 16:24). Those whom the Lord has chosen and honored with his fellowship must prepare for a hard, laborious, troubled life, a life full of many and various kinds of evils; for it is the will of our heavenly Father to exercise his people in this way while he puts them to the proof. Having begun this course with Christ, the firstborn, he continues it toward all his children. For though that Son was dear to him above all others, the Son in whom he was "well pleased," yet we see that, far from being treated gently and indulgently, he was not only subjected to a perpetual cross while he lived on earth, but his whole life was, we may say, nothing else than a kind of perpetual cross. The Apostle gives the reason: "Though he was a Son, yet learned he obedience by the things which he suffered" (Hebrews 5:8). Why, then, should we exempt ourselves from the condition to which Christ our Head had to submit, especially since he submitted to it for our sake, so that in his own person he might show us a model of patience? This is why the Apostle declares that all the children of God are destined to be conformed to him. Hence it gives us great comfort in hard and difficult circumstances, which men count evil and adverse, to think that we are sharing in the sufferings of Christ; that as he passed to heavenly glory through a labyrinth of many woes, so we too are led there through various tribulations. For in another passage Paul himself speaks this way: "we must through much tribulation enter the kingdom of God" (Acts 14:22); and again, "that I may know him, and the power of his resurrection, and the fellowship of his sufferings, being made conformable unto his death" (Romans 8:29). How powerfully it should soften the bitterness of the cross to think that the more we are afflicted with adversity, the surer we are made of our fellowship with Christ; for by communion with him our sufferings are not only blessed to us, but also greatly further our salvation.

2¶

We may add that the only thing which made it necessary for our Lord to take up the cross was to testify to and prove his obedience to the Father; whereas there are many reasons that make it necessary for us to live constantly under the cross. Feeble as we are by nature, and prone to ascribe all perfection to our flesh, unless we are given, as it were, a demonstration of our weakness before our eyes, we readily rate our virtue above its proper worth, and do not doubt that, whatever happens, it will stand unshaken and invincible against every difficulty. So we indulge a stupid and empty confidence in the flesh, and then, trusting in it, we grow proud against the Lord himself, as if our own faculties were sufficient without his grace. This arrogance cannot be better checked than when he proves to us by experience not only how great our weakness is, but also how great our frailty. Therefore he visits us with disgrace, or poverty, or bereavement, or disease, or other afflictions. Feeling altogether unable to bear them, we at once give way, so far as we ourselves are concerned, and, humbled in this way, we learn to call on his strength, which alone can enable us to hold up under a weight of affliction. Indeed, even the holiest of men, however well they know that they stand not in their own strength but by the grace of God, would feel too secure in their own fortitude and constancy if they were not brought to a more thorough knowledge of themselves by the trial of the cross. This feeling crept up even on David: "In my prosperity I said, I shall never be moved. Lord, by thy favour thou hast made my mountain to stand strong: thou didst hide thy face, and I was troubled" (Psalm 30:6, 7). He confesses that in prosperity his feelings were dulled and blunted, so that, neglecting the grace of God, on which alone he ought to have depended, he leaned on himself and promised himself permanence. If this happened to so great a prophet, which of us should not fear and take care? Though in times of calm they flatter themselves with the idea of greater constancy and patience, yet, when humbled by adversity, they discover the deception. Believers, I say, warned by such proofs of their diseases, make progress in humility, and, stripping themselves of a depraved confidence in the flesh, take refuge in the grace of God; and when they have taken refuge there, they experience the presence of the divine power, in which there is ample protection.

3¶

This is what Paul teaches when he says that tribulation worketh patience, and patience experience. Since God has promised that he will be with believers in tribulation, they feel the truth of the promise; while they are upheld by his hand, they endure patiently. This they could never do by their own strength. Patience, therefore, gives the saints proof from experience that God really supplies the help he has promised whenever there is need. Hence their faith is also confirmed, for it would be very ungrateful not to expect that in the future the truth of God will be what they have already found it to be, firm and constant. We now see how many benefits the cross produces at once. Overturning the inflated opinion we form of our own virtue, and exposing the hypocrisy in which we delight, it removes our ruinous carnal confidence and teaches us, when we are humbled in this way, to rest on God alone, so that we are neither crushed nor despondent. Then victory is followed by hope, inasmuch as the Lord, by performing what he has promised, establishes his truth with regard to the future. Even if these were the only reasons, it is surely plain how necessary it is for us to bear the cross. It is of no small importance to be rid of your self-love and made fully aware of your weakness; so impressed with a sense of your weakness as to learn to distrust yourself — to distrust yourself so as to transfer your confidence to God, resting on him with such heartfelt confidence as to trust in his help and remain invincible to the end, standing by his grace so as to see that he is true to his promises, and so assured of the certainty of his promises as to be strong in hope.

4¶

Another end the Lord has in afflicting his people is to test their patience and train them to obedience — not that they can render obedience to him except so far as he enables them; but he is pleased in this way to attest and display striking proofs of the graces he has conferred on his saints, so that they do not remain hidden within, unseen and unused. Accordingly, by bringing out into the open the strength and constancy of endurance with which he has furnished his servants, he is said to test their patience. Hence the expressions that God tempted Abraham (Genesis 21:1, 12), and made proof of his godliness by his not refusing to sacrifice his only son. Hence, too, Peter tells us that our faith is proved by tribulation, just as gold is tried in a furnace of fire. But who will say it is not fitting that the most excellent gift of patience, which the believer has received from his God, should be put to use by being made certain and manifest? Otherwise men would never value it according to its worth. But if God himself, to keep the virtues he has conferred on believers from lurking in obscurity, indeed from lying useless and perishing, does rightly in supplying the material for calling them forth, then there is the best of reasons for the afflictions of the saints, since without them their patience could not exist. I say that by the cross they are also trained to obedience, because in this way they are taught to live not according to their own wish but at the disposal of God. Indeed, if everything went as they wished, they would not know what it is to follow God. Seneca mentions (De Vit. Beata, chapter 15) that there was an old proverb, when anyone was exhorted to endure adversity: "Follow God"; meaning by this that men truly submitted to the yoke of God only when they gave their back and hand to his rod. But if it is most right that we should prove our obedience to our heavenly Father in all things, we certainly ought not to refuse any method by which he trains us to obedience.

5¶

Still, we do not see how necessary that obedience is unless we also consider how prone our carnal nature is to throw off the yoke of God whenever it has been treated with some measure of gentleness and indulgence. It fares with it just as with stubborn horses, which, if kept idle for a few days in the stall and at the manger, become unmanageable and no longer recognize the rider whose command they used to obey without question. And we invariably become what God complains of in the people of Israel: growing gross and fat, we kick against him who reared and nourished us (Deuteronomy 32:15). The kindness of God should draw us to ponder and love his goodness; but since our malice is such that we are invariably corrupted by his indulgence, it is more than necessary for us to be held back by discipline from breaking out into such insolence. So, lest we grow bold through an overabundance of wealth; lest, lifted up by honor, we grow proud; lest, puffed up by other advantages of body, or mind, or fortune, we grow insolent, the Lord himself steps in, as he sees fit, by means of the cross, subduing and curbing the arrogance of our flesh, and that in various ways, as the good of each requires. For as we do not all suffer equally from the same disease, so we do not all need the same harsh cure. Hence we see that not all are exercised with the same kind of cross. While the heavenly Physician treats some more gently, with others he uses harsher remedies, his purpose being to provide a cure for all. Yet no one is left free and untouched, because he knows that all, without a single exception, are diseased.

6¶

We may add that our most merciful Father needs not only to forestall our weakness, but often to correct our past faults, so that he may keep us in due obedience. Therefore, whenever we are afflicted, we ought at once to call our past life to mind. In this way we will find that the faults we have committed deserve such chastening. And yet the exhortation to patience is not to be founded chiefly on the acknowledgment of sin. For Scripture supplies a far better consideration when it says that in adversity "we are chastened of the Lord, that we should not be condemned with the world" (1 Corinthians 11:32). Therefore, in the very bitterness of tribulation we ought to recognize the kindness and mercy of our Father, since even then he does not cease to further our salvation. For he afflicts us not to ruin or destroy us, but rather to deliver us from the condemnation of the world. Let this thought lead us to what Scripture teaches elsewhere: "My son, despise not the chastening of the Lord; neither be weary of his correction: For whom the Lord loveth he correcteth; even as a father the son in whom he delighteth" (Proverbs 3:11, 12). When we feel our Father's rod, is it not our part to behave as obedient, teachable sons, rather than rebelliously to imitate desperate men who are hardened in wickedness? God gives us over to destruction if he does not call us back by correction when we have fallen away from him, so that it is truly said, "If ye be without chastisement," "then are ye bastards, and not sons" (Hebrews 12:8). We are most perverse, then, if we cannot bear with him while he is showing his goodwill toward us and the care he takes for our salvation. Scripture states the difference between believers and unbelievers to be this: the latter, as slaves of inveterate and deep-rooted iniquity, only become worse and more obstinate under the lash, whereas the former, like freeborn sons, turn to repentance. Now, therefore, choose your class. But since I have already spoken of this subject, it is enough to have touched on it briefly here.

7¶

There is singular comfort, moreover, when we are persecuted for righteousness' sake. For our thought should then be: How high is the honor God bestows on us in marking us out with the special badge of his soldiers! By suffering persecution for righteousness' sake I mean not only striving in defense of the Gospel, but in defense of righteousness in any way. Whether, therefore, in maintaining the truth of God against the lies of Satan, or in defending the good and innocent against the injuries of the wicked, we are forced to incur the offense and hatred of the world, so as to put life, fortune, or honor at risk, let us not grieve or refuse to spend ourselves so far for God; let us not think ourselves wretched in those things in which he with his own lips has pronounced us blessed (Matthew 5:10). Poverty, indeed, considered in itself, is misery; so are exile, contempt, imprisonment, and disgrace; in short, death itself is the last of all calamities. But when the favor of God breathes on us, there is none of these things that may not turn out to our happiness. Let us, then, be content with the testimony of Christ rather than with the false estimate of the flesh, and then, following the example of the Apostles, we will rejoice in being "counted worthy to suffer shame for his name" (Acts 5:41). Why so? If, while conscious of our innocence, we are stripped of our substance by the wickedness of men, we are no doubt, humanly speaking, reduced to poverty; but in truth our riches in heaven are increased. If we are driven from our homes, we have a warmer welcome into the family of God; if harassed and despised, we are more firmly rooted in Christ; if branded with disgrace and shame, we have a higher place in the kingdom of God; and if we are slain, entrance into eternal life is thereby given us. Since the Lord has set such a price on us, let us be ashamed to value ourselves at less than the shadowy and fleeting allurements of the present life.

8¶

Since by these and similar considerations Scripture abundantly comforts us for the disgrace or calamities we endure in defense of righteousness, we are very ungrateful if we do not willingly and cheerfully receive them from the hand of the Lord, especially since this form of the cross is the one most fitting for believers, being that by which Christ desires to be glorified in us, as Peter also declares (1 Peter 4:11, 14). But since to noble natures it is more bitter to suffer disgrace than a hundred deaths, Paul expressly reminds us that not only persecution but also disgrace awaits us, "because we trust in the living God" (1 Timothy 4:10). So in another passage he bids us, after his example, to walk "by evil report and good report" (2 Corinthians 6:8). The cheerfulness required, however, does not mean a total insensibility to pain. The saints could show no patience under the cross if they were not both tormented with pain and grievously troubled. If there were no hardship in poverty, no pain in disease, no sting in disgrace, no fear in death, where would be the fortitude and moderation in enduring them? But while every one of these, by its own inherent bitterness, naturally distresses the mind, the believer shows his fortitude in this: that though he fully feels the bitterness and labors grievously, he still holds out and struggles boldly. He shows his patience in this: that though sharply stung, he is nevertheless restrained by the fear of God from breaking out into any excess. He shows his cheerfulness in this: that though weighed down with sorrow and sadness, he rests content with spiritual comfort from God.

9¶

This conflict which believers carry on against the natural feeling of pain, while they strive for moderation and patience, Paul describes elegantly in these words: "We are troubled on every side, yet not distressed; we are perplexed, but not in despair; persecuted, but not forsaken; cast down, but not destroyed" (2 Corinthians 4:8, 9). You see that to bear the cross patiently is not to have your feelings altogether deadened and to be utterly insensible to pain, according to the absurd picture which the Stoics of old drew of their wise man, as one who, stripped of humanity, was affected in the same way by adversity and prosperity, by grief and joy; or rather, like a stone, was affected by nothing at all. And what did they gain by that lofty wisdom? They displayed a shadow of patience which never did, and never can, exist among men. Indeed, by aiming at a too exact and rigid patience, they banished it from human life altogether. Now we also have among Christians a new kind of Stoics, who hold it a vice not only to groan and weep, but even to be sad and anxious. These paradoxes are usually started by idle men who, occupying themselves more with speculation than with action, can do nothing else for us than breed such paradoxes. But we have nothing to do with that iron philosophy which our Lord and Master condemned, not only in word but also by his own example. For he both grieved and shed tears over his own woes and those of others. Nor did he teach his disciples otherwise: "Ye shall weep and lament, but the world shall rejoice" (John 16:20). And lest anyone should regard this as a vice, he expressly declares, "Blessed are they that mourn" (Matthew 5:4). And no wonder. If all tears are condemned, what shall we think of our Lord himself, whose "sweat was as it were great drops of blood falling down to the ground" (Luke 22:44; Matthew 26:38)? If every kind of fear is a mark of unbelief, what place shall we assign to the dread which, we are told, overwhelmed him in no slight degree? If all sadness is condemned, how shall we justify him when he confesses, "My soul is exceeding sorrowful, even unto death"?

10¶

I wished to make these observations to keep godly minds from despair, lest, finding it impossible to strip themselves of the natural feeling of grief, they should abandon the pursuit of patience altogether. This must necessarily be the result for those who turn patience into stupor, and a brave and steadfast man into a block of wood. Scripture praises the saints for endurance when, though afflicted by the hardships they suffer, they are not crushed; though they feel bitterly, they are at the same time filled with spiritual joy; though weighed down with anxiety, they breathe again, refreshed by the comfort of God. Still there is a certain resistance in their hearts, because natural sense shuns and dreads what is opposed to it, while godly affection, even through these difficulties, strives to obey the divine will. The Lord expressed this resistance when he spoke to Peter in this way: "Verily, verily, I say unto thee, When thou wast young, thou girdedst thyself and walkedst whither thou wouldst; but when thou shalt be old, thou shalt stretch forth thy hands, and another shall gird thee; and carry thee whither thou wouldest not" (John 21:18). It is not likely, indeed, that when it became necessary to glorify God by death he was driven to it unwilling and resisting; had that been so, little praise would have been due to his martyrdom. But though he obeyed the divine appointment with the greatest eagerness of heart, yet, since he had not stripped himself of humanity, he was torn by a double will. When he thought of the bloody death he was to die, he was struck with horror and would gladly have escaped it; on the other hand, when he considered that it was God who called him to it, his fear was overcome and suppressed, and he met death cheerfully. It must therefore be our aim, if we would be disciples of Christ, to fill our minds with such reverence for God and obedience to him as may tame and subdue all affections contrary to his appointment. In this way, whatever kind of cross we are put under, we shall firmly maintain our patience in the greatest straits. Adversity will have its bitterness and will sting us. When afflicted with disease, we shall groan and be troubled and long for health; pressed by poverty, we shall feel the stings of anxiety and sadness; we shall feel the pain of disgrace, contempt, and injury, and pay the tears that nature owes at the death of our friends. But our conclusion will always be: The Lord so willed it; therefore let us follow his will. Indeed, amid the sharpness of grief, among groans and tears, this thought will necessarily suggest itself and incline us to endure cheerfully the things by which we are so afflicted.

11¶

But since the chief reason for enduring the cross has been drawn from a consideration of the divine will, we must explain in a few words where the difference lies between philosophical and Christian patience. Indeed, very few of the philosophers advanced so far as to perceive that the hand of God tests us by means of affliction, and that we ought to obey God in this matter. The only reason they offer is that so it must be. But is this not simply to say that we must yield to God because it is useless to struggle against him? For if we obey God only because it is necessary, we shall cease to obey him as soon as we can escape. But what Scripture calls us to consider in the will of God is very different, namely, first, justice and equity, and then a regard for our own salvation. Hence Christian exhortations to patience are of this kind: whether poverty, or exile, or imprisonment, or insult, or disease, or bereavement, or any such evil befalls us, we must think that none of them happens except by the will and providence of God; and, moreover, that everything he does is done in the most perfect order. What! Do not our countless daily faults deserve to be chastised more severely, and with a heavier rod, than his mercy lays on us? Is it not most right that our flesh should be subdued and, as it were, accustomed to the yoke, so that it may not rage and run wild as it pleases? Are not the justice and the truth of God worthy of our suffering for their sake? See the end of section 4, and sections 5, 7, 8.

Calvin will not let us be surprised by suffering: the Father who did not spare his own Son leads every child through the cross, not in wrath but to humble our proud confidence in the flesh, to drive us to his strength, to school us in obedience, and to bind us to the fellowship of Christ's own sufferings. This is medicine, not malice — the heavenly Physician prescribing to each the cure his disease requires. In 2026 a faith that promises ease sells fast and disappoints utterly; teach the family instead to read affliction rightly, to recognize the Father's rod and answer as sons rather than rebels. And mark Calvin's tender realism against every brand of Stoic hardness: Christ wept and sweat blood, so the believer may groan and grieve and still not be crushed. We are not called to feel nothing — we are called to say, through the tears, the Lord so willed it, therefore let us follow his will.
