The Righteousness of Works Improperly Inferred From Rewards

There are three divisions in this chapter,—I. A solution of two general objections which are urged in support of justification by works. First, That God will render to every one according to his works, sec. 1. Second, That the reward of works is called eternal, sec. 2-6. II. Answer to other special objections derived from the former, and a perversion of passages of Scripture, sec. 6-9. III. Refutation of the sophism that faith itself is called a work, and, therefore, justification by it is by works, sec. 10. 1. Two general objections. The former solved and explained. What meant by the term working . 2. Solution of the second general objection. 1. Works not the cause of salvation. This shown from the name and nature of inheritance. 2. A striking example that the Lord rewards the works of believers with blessings which he had promised before the works were thought of. 3. First reason why eternal life said to be the reward of works. This confirmed by passages of Scripture. The concurrence of Ambrose. A rule to be observed. Declarations of Christ and an Apostle. 4. Other four reasons. Holiness the way to the kingdom, not the cause of obtaining it. Proposition of the Sophists. 5. Objection that God crowns the works of his people. Three answers from Augustine. A fourth from Scripture. 6. First special objection—viz. that we are ordered to lay up treasure in heaven. Answer, showing in what way this can be done. 7. Second objection—viz. that the righteous enduring affliction are said to be worthy of the kingdom of heaven. Answer. What meant by righteousness. 8. A third objection founded on three passages of Paul. Answer. 9. Fourth objection founded on our Savior’s words, “If ye would enter into life, keep the commandments.” Answer, giving an exposition of the passage. 10. Last objection—viz. that faith itself is called a work. Answer—it is not as a work that faith justifies.

1¶

Let us now go on to the passages which affirm that God will render to everyone according to his deeds. Of this kind are the following: "We must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ; that every one may receive the things done in his body, according to that he has done, whether it be good or bad"; "Who will render to every man according to his deeds: to them who by patient continuance in well-doing seek for glory, and honor, and immortality, eternal life"; but "tribulation and anguish upon every soul of man that does evil"; "They that have done good, unto the resurrection of life; and they that have done evil, unto the resurrection of damnation"; "Come, ye blessed of my Father"; "For I was an hungered, and ye gave me meat; I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink," and so on. To these we may add the passages which describe eternal life as the reward of works, such as these: "The recompense of a man's hands shall be rendered unto him"; "He that feareth the commandment shall be rewarded"; "Rejoice and be exceeding glad, for great is your reward in heaven"; "Every man shall receive his own rewards according to his own labor." Matthew 16:27; 2 Corinthians 5:10; Romans 2:6; John 5:29; Matthew 25:34; Proverbs 12:14; 13:13; Matthew 5:12; Luke 6:23; 1 Corinthians 3:8.

French, "mais seulement emporte zele et estude"; but it only imports zeal and study.

2¶

There is nothing in the word reward to justify the inference that our works are the cause of salvation. First, let it be fixed in our hearts as a settled principle that the kingdom of heaven is not the wages of servants but the inheritance of sons (Ephesians 1:18): an inheritance obtained only by those whom the Lord has adopted as sons, and obtained for no other reason than this adoption. "The son of the bondwoman shall not be heir with the son of the freewoman" (Galatians 4:30). And so, in the very passages in which the Holy Spirit promises eternal glory as the reward of works, by expressly calling it an inheritance he shows that it comes to us from some other source. Thus Christ lists the works for which he bestows heaven as a recompense while he is calling his elect to take possession of it, but at the same time he adds that it is to be possessed by right of inheritance (Matthew 25:34). Paul, too, encourages servants who faithfully do their duty to hope for reward from the Lord, but he adds, "of the inheritance" (Colossians 3:24). You see how, in almost formal terms, they carefully warn us to attribute eternal blessedness not to works but to the adoption of God. Why, then, do they mention works at the same time? This question will be made clear by an example from Scripture (Genesis 15:5; 17:1). Before the birth of Isaac, Abraham had received the promise of a seed in whom all the families of the earth would be blessed, and of the increase of a seed that in number would equal the stars of heaven and the sand of the sea, and so on. Many years afterward, in obedience to a divine command, he prepares to sacrifice his son. Having done this act of obedience, he receives the promise: "By myself have I sworn, saith the Lord, for because you have done this thing, and have not withheld your son, your only son; that in blessing I will bless you, and in multiplying I will multiply your seed as the stars of the heaven, and as the sand which is upon the sea-shore, and your seed shall possess the gate of his enemies; and in your seed shall all the nations of the earth be blessed, because you have obeyed my voice" (Genesis 22:16-18). What is it that we hear? Did Abraham by his obedience merit the blessing which had been promised him before the command was given? Here, surely, we see without any ambiguity that God rewards the works of believers with blessings which he had given them before the works were ever thought of, since there is still no cause for the blessings he bestows except his own mercy.

3¶

And yet the Lord does not act in vain or mock us when he says that he renders to works what he had freely given before works. Since he wants us to be exercised in good works while we aspire to the manifestation, or, if I may put it so, the enjoyment, of the things he has promised, and by means of them to press on toward the blessed hope set before us in heaven, the fruit of the promises is rightly ascribed to those things by which it is brought to maturity. The apostle expressed both points elegantly when he told the Colossians to give themselves to the duties of love "for the hope which is laid up for you in heaven, whereof ye heard before in the word of the truth of the gospel" (Colossians 1:5). For when he says that the gospel told them of the hope that was stored up for them in heaven, he declares that it depends on Christ alone and not at all on works. With this agrees the saying of Peter, that believers "are kept by the power of God through faith unto salvation, ready to be revealed in the last time" (1 Peter 1:5). When he says that they strive on account of it, he indicates that believers must keep running through the whole course of their lives in order to attain it. But to keep us from supposing that the reward which is promised becomes a kind of merit, our Lord set forth a parable in which he represented himself as a householder who sent all the laborers he met to work in his vineyard, some at the first hour of the day, others at the second, others at the third, some even at the eleventh; and in the evening he paid them all alike. The interpretation of this parable is given briefly and truly by that ancient writer (whoever he was) who wrote the book De Vocatione Gentium, which goes under the name of Ambrose. I will give it in his words rather than my own: French, "Pource que c'est un Docteur ancien, j'aime mieux user de ses paroles que des miennes"; because he is an ancient Doctor, I prefer to use his words rather than my own.

4¶

Let us not suppose, then, that by this promise the Holy Spirit commends the worth of our works, as if they deserved such a reward. For Scripture leaves us nothing in which we may glory in the sight of God. On the contrary, its whole aim is to restrain, humble, cast down, and utterly crush our pride. But in this way help is given to our weakness, which would at once give way if it were not upheld by this expectation and soothed by this comfort. First, let every man consider for himself how hard it is not only to leave all things, but to leave and renounce oneself. And yet this is the training by which Christ initiates his disciples, that is, all the godly. Secondly, he keeps them all their lifetime under the discipline of the cross, so that they will not let their hearts long for or trust in present good. In short, his treatment of them is usually such that wherever they turn their eyes, as far as this world extends, they see nothing before them but despair; and so Paul says, "If in this life only we have hope in Christ, we are of all men most miserable" (1 Corinthians 15:19). So that they may not fail in these great straits, the Lord is at hand, reminding them to lift their heads higher and to look farther, so that in him they may find a happiness which they do not see in the world. To this happiness he gives the name of reward, wages, recompense, not as weighing the merit of works, but as indicating that it is a compensation for their straits, sufferings, insults, and the like. Therefore nothing prevents us from calling eternal life a recompense, following the example of Scripture, because in it the Lord brings his people from labor to rest, from affliction to a prosperous and desirable condition, from sorrow to joy, from poverty to abundance, from disgrace to glory; in short, he exchanges all the evils they have endured for blessings. So there will be nothing improper in regarding holiness of life as the way, not indeed the way that gives access to the glory of the heavenly kingdom, but a way by which God leads his elect to the manifestation of that kingdom, since it is his good pleasure to glorify those whom he has sanctified (Romans 8:30). Only let us not imagine that merit and wages are correlative terms, a point on which the Sophists absurdly insist, because they do not attend to the purpose we have pointed out. How perverse it is, when the Lord calls us to one end, to look to another! Nothing is clearer than that a reward is promised to good works in order to support the weakness of our flesh with some measure of comfort, but not to puff up our minds with vainglory. Whoever, therefore, infers reward from merit, or weighs works and reward in the same balance, strays very far from the end that God has in view.

5¶

Accordingly, when Scripture speaks of "a crown of righteousness which God the righteous Judge shall give" "at that day" (2 Timothy 4:8), I not only say with Augustine, "To whom could the righteous Judge give the crown if the merciful Father had not given grace? And how could there have been righteousness but for the precedence of grace which justified the ungodly? How could these be paid as things due, if things not due had not first been given?" (Augustine, To Valentinus, On Grace and Free Will); but I also add: how could he impute righteousness to our works if his indulgence did not hide the unrighteousness that is in them? How could he judge them worthy of reward if he did not, in his boundless goodness, wipe out what is unworthy in them? Augustine is accustomed to call eternal life grace, because, while it is the recompense of works, it is bestowed by the free gifts of God. But Scripture humbles us more, and at the same time lifts us up. For besides forbidding us to glory in works, because they are the free gifts of God, it tells us that they are always stained with some degree of impurity, so that they cannot satisfy God when they are tested by the standard of his justice; but that, lest our diligence should be destroyed, they please him only through pardon. But though Augustine speaks somewhat differently from us, it is plain from his words that the difference is more apparent than real. After drawing a contrast between two persons, the one with a life holy and perfect almost to a miracle, the other honest indeed and of pure morals, yet not so perfect as to leave no room for wishing for better, he at length concludes: "He who seems inferior in conduct, yet on account of the true faith in God by which he lives (Habakkuk 2:4), and in keeping with which he accuses himself in all his faults, praises God in all his good works, takes the shame to himself and gives the glory to God, from whom he receives both forgiveness for his sins and the love of doing well, is carried into the fellowship of Christ the moment he is set free from this life. Why, except on account of his faith? For though it saves no one without works (such a faith being reprobate and not working by love), yet by means of it sins are forgiven; for the just lives by faith. Without it, works which seem good are turned into sins" (Augustine, To Boniface, book 3, chapter 5). Here he acknowledges plainly what we so strongly maintain, that the righteousness of good works depends on their being approved by God by way of pardon. The French adds, "C'est à dire, en misericorde, et non pas en jugement"; that is to say, in mercy, and not in judgment.

6¶

In a sense similar to the passages above, our opponents quote the following: "Make to yourselves friends of the mammon of unrighteousness; that when ye fail, they may receive you into everlasting habitations" (Luke 16:9). "Charge them that are rich in this world, that they be not high-minded, nor trust in uncertain riches, but in the living God, who giveth us richly all things to enjoy: that they do good, that they be rich in good works, ready to distribute, willing to communicate; laying up in store for themselves a good foundation against the time to come, that they may lay hold on eternal life" (1 Timothy 6:17-19). For the good works which we enjoy in eternal blessedness are compared to riches. I answer that we will never reach a true understanding of these passages unless we pay attention to the Spirit's purpose in speaking them. If it is true, as Christ says, that "where your treasure is, there will your heart be also" (Matthew 6:21), then, just as the children of the world are intent on providing the things that make up the delight of the present life, so it is the duty of believers, once they have learned that this life will soon pass away like a dream, to take care that the things they would truly enjoy are sent on ahead to the place where their whole life is to be spent. We must therefore do as those do who begin to move to some place where they mean to settle. They send their goods on ahead and do not mind going without them for a time, because they think that the more they have in their future home, the happier they will be. So, if we think that heaven is our country, we ought to send our wealth there rather than keep it here, where at our sudden departure it will be lost to us. But how shall we send it there? By contributing to the needs of the poor, since the Lord counts as given to himself whatever is given to them. Hence that excellent promise, "He that has pity on the poor lendeth to the Lord" (Proverbs 19:17; Matthew 25:40); and again, "He which soweth bountifully shall reap also bountifully" (2 Corinthians 9:6). What we give to our brothers in the exercise of love is a deposit with the Lord, who, as a faithful trustee, will in the end restore it with abundant interest. Are our duties, then, of such value with God that they are a kind of treasure placed in his hand? Who can hesitate to say so, when Scripture so often and so plainly testifies to it? But if anyone would leap from the mere kindness of God to the merit of works, French, "Mais si quelcun pour obscurcir la benignité de Dieu veut establir la dignité des œuvres"; but if anyone, to obscure the kindness of God, would establish the worth of works.

7¶

But they press harder on the words of the apostle when, comforting the Thessalonians in their tribulations, he tells them that these were sent "that ye may be counted worthy of the kingdom of God, for which ye also suffer; seeing it is a righteous thing with God to recompense tribulation to them that trouble you; and to you who are troubled, rest with us, when the Lord Jesus shall be revealed from heaven with his mighty angels" (2 Thessalonians 1:6-7). The author of the Epistle to the Hebrews says, "God is not unrighteous to forget your work and labour of love, which ye have showed towards his name, in that ye have ministered to the saints, and do minister" (Hebrews 6:10). To the first passage I answer that the worthiness spoken of is not that of merit. Rather, since God the Father wills that those whom he has chosen as sons should be conformed to Christ the firstborn, and since it behooved Christ first to suffer and then to enter into his glory, so we also enter the kingdom of heaven through much tribulation. Therefore, while we suffer tribulation for the name of Christ, we in a sense receive the marks with which God is accustomed to stamp the sheep of his flock (Galatians 6:17). So we are counted worthy of the kingdom of God because we bear in our body the marks of our Lord and Master, these being the insignia of the children of God. In this sense we are to understand these passages: "Always bearing about in the body the dying of the Lord Jesus, that the life also of Jesus might be made manifest in our body" (2 Corinthians 4:10); "That I may know him and the power of his resurrection, and the fellowship of his sufferings, being made conformable unto his death" (Philippians 3:10). The reason which is added is meant not to prove any merit but to confirm our hope of the kingdom of God; as if he had said, As it befits the just judgment of God to take vengeance on your enemies for the tribulation they have brought upon you, so it also befits it to give you release and rest from these tribulations. The other passage, which speaks as if it befitted the justice of God not to overlook the services of his people, and almost suggests that it would be unjust to forget them, is to be explained in this way: God, to rouse us from sloth, assures us that no labor we undertake for the glory of his name will be in vain. Let us always remember that this promise, like all other promises, will be of no use unless it is preceded by the free covenant of mercy, on which the whole certainty of our salvation depends. Relying on that covenant, however, we ought to feel secure that, however unworthy our services may be, the generosity of God will not let them pass unrewarded. To confirm us in this expectation, the apostle declares that God is not unrighteous, but will act consistently with the promise he has once given. Righteousness here, therefore, refers to the truthfulness of the divine promise rather than to the fairness of paying what is due. In this sense there is a celebrated saying of Augustine which, because it contains a memorable thought, that holy man did not hesitate to use repeatedly, and which I think worthy of being constantly remembered: "Faithful is the Lord, who has made himself our debtor, not by receiving anything from us, but by promising us all things" (Augustine, on Psalms 32, 109, and elsewhere).

8¶

Our opponents also bring forward these passages from Paul: "Though I have all faith, so that I could remove mountains, and have not charity, I am nothing" (1 Corinthians 13:2). Again, "Now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity" (1 Corinthians 13:13). "Above all these things put on charity, which is the bond of perfectness" (Colossians 3:14). From the first two passages our Pharisees See Calvin's Answer to Sadolet, who had said that charity is the first and principal cause of our salvation.

9¶

I do not wish to discuss all the things which the foolish Sorbonnists have rashly seized on in Scripture, just as they happened to come their way, and hurl against us. Some of them are so ridiculous that I cannot mention them without exposing myself to the charge of trifling. I will therefore close with an explanation of one saying of our Savior with which they are wonderfully pleased. When the lawyer asked him, "Good Master, what good thing shall I do, that I may have eternal life?" he answers, "If you will enter into life, keep the commandments" (Matthew 19:16, 17). What more, they ask, could we want, when the very author of grace bids us gain the kingdom of heaven by keeping the commandments? As if it were not plain that Christ suited his answers to the character of those he was addressing! Here he is questioned by a doctor of the Law about the means of obtaining eternal life; and the question is not put simply, but is: What can men do to attain it? Both the character of the speaker and his question led our Lord to give this answer. Steeped in a conviction of legal righteousness, the lawyer had a blind confidence in works. So all he was asking was: What are the works of righteousness by which salvation is obtained? Rightly, therefore, he is referred to the law, in which there is a perfect mirror of righteousness. We too declare plainly that if life is sought in works, the commandments must be kept. And the knowledge of this doctrine is necessary for Christians; for how would they betake themselves to Christ unless they saw that they had fallen from the path of life over the precipice of death? Or how could they understand how far they have wandered from the way of life unless they first understand what that way is? Only then do they feel that their refuge of safety is in Christ, when they see how far their conduct is at odds with the divine righteousness, which consists in keeping the law. The sum of the whole is this: if salvation is sought in works, we must keep the commandments, by which we are instructed in perfect righteousness. But we cannot stop here unless we mean to halt in the middle of our course; for none of us is able to keep the commandments. Being thus shut out from the righteousness of the law, we must betake ourselves to another remedy, namely, to faith in Christ. Therefore, just as our Lord here directed to the law a teacher of the law whom he knew to be puffed up with a vain confidence in works, so that he might learn that he was a sinner exposed to the fearful sentence of eternal death, so others, who were already humbled by this knowledge, he elsewhere comforts with the promise of grace, without making any mention of the law: "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." "Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls" (Matthew 11:28, 29).

10¶

At length, after they have worn themselves out twisting Scripture, they fall back on subtleties and sophisms. One cavil is that faith is somewhere called a work (John 6:29); from this they infer that we are wrong in setting faith against works. As if faith, considered as obedience to the divine will, could procure our justification by its own merit, and did not rather, by embracing the mercy of God, seal upon our hearts the righteousness of Christ which is offered to us in the preaching of the gospel! My readers will pardon me if I do not stop to dispose of such absurdities; their own weakness, without any attack from outside, is enough to destroy them. One objection, however, which has some appearance of reason, it will be well to dispose of in passing, so that it may not trouble those with less experience. Since common sense dictates that opposites must be tried by the same rule, and since each sin is charged against us as unrighteousness, it is right, say our opponents, that each good work should receive the praise of righteousness. The answer which some give, that the condemnation of men rests on unbelief alone and not on particular sins, does not satisfy me. I agree with them, indeed, that unbelief is the fountain and root of all evil; for it is the first act of revolt from God, and it is afterward followed by particular transgressions of the law. But since they seem to hold that, in judging righteousness and unrighteousness, the same rule is to be applied to good and bad works, here I dissent from them. French, "Mais touchant ce qu'ils semblent advis contrepoiser en une mesme balance les bonnes œuvres et les mauvaises, pour estimer la justice ou l'injustice de l'homme, en cela je suis contreint de leur repugner"; but as they seem disposed to put good and bad works into the opposite scales of the same balance, in order to judge the righteousness or unrighteousness of man, in this I am compelled to dissent from them.

A Word for 2026 — U.S.M.C. Ministries Calvin will not let the language of reward become a doctrine of merit: heaven is the inheritance of sons, not the wage of servants, and when Scripture promises a recompense to good works, it does so to support our weak flesh through a hard pilgrimage, never to inflate us with the notion that we have purchased anything. God crowns his own gifts. Abraham received the blessing before the work that "earned" it was ever conceived. This is a sober comfort for the believer in 2026 who labors under the cross, gives quietly to the poor, endures affliction for Christ's name, and sees no earthly return — your reward is sure, but it is grace upon grace, not a debt God owes you. Teach the children that we keep the commandments not to buy life but to be driven, by our own failure to keep them, back to Christ. The faithful Lord has made himself our debtor by promising us all things, having received nothing from us; let that, and not the arithmetic of merit, steady the hand at its long labor.
