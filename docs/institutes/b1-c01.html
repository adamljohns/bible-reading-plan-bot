Book 1 — Argument. ARGUMENT. The First Book treats of the knowledge of God the Creator. But as it is in the creation of man that the divine perfections are best displayed, so man also is made the subject of discourse. Thus the whole book divides itself into two principal heads—the former relating to the knowledge of God, and the latter to the knowledge of man. In the first chapter, these are considered jointly; and in each of the following chapters, separately: occasionally, however, intermingled with other matters which refer to one or other of the heads; e.g. , the discussions concerning Scripture and images, falling under the former head, and the other three concerning the creation of the world, the holy angels and devils, falling under the latter. The last point discussed—viz. the method of the divine government, relates to both. With regard to the former head—viz. the knowledge of God, it is shown, in the first place, what the kind of knowledge is which God requires, Chap. 2. And, in the second place (Chap. 3-9), where this knowledge must be sought, namely, not in man; because, although naturally implanted in the human mind, it is stifled, partly by ignorance, partly by evil intent, Chap. 3 and 4; not in the frame of the world: because, although it shines most clearly there, we are so stupid that these manifestations, however perspicuous, pass away without any beneficial result, Chap. 5; but in Scripture (Chap. 6), which is treated of, Chap. 7-9. In the third place, it is shown what the character of God is, Chap. 10. In the fourth place, how impious it is to give a visible form to God (here images, the worship of them, and its origin, are considered), Chap. 11. In the fifth place, it is shown that God is to be solely and wholly worshipped, Chap. 12. Lastly, Chap. 13 treats of the unity of the divine essence, and the distinction of three persons. With regard to the latter head—viz. the knowledge of man, first, Chap. 14 treats of the creation of the world, and of good and bad angels (these all having reference to man). And then Chap. 15, taking up the subject of man himself, examines his nature and his powers. The better to illustrate the nature both of God and man, the three remaining Chapters—viz. 16-18, proceed to treat of the general government of the world, and particularly of human actions, in opposition to fortune and fate, explaining both the doctrine and its use. In conclusion, it is shown, that though God employs the instrumentality of the wicked, he is pure from sin and from taint of every kind.

The Knowledge of God and of Ourselves Mutually Connected — Nature of the Connection

▶ Listen — read by Alan (modernized for 2026)
Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. The sum of true wisdom—viz. the knowledge of God and of ourselves. Effects of the latter.
  2. 2. Effects of the knowledge of God, in humbling our pride, unveiling our hypocrisy, demonstrating the absolute perfections of God, and our own utter helplessness.
  3. 3. Effects of the knowledge of God illustrated by the examples, 1. of holy patriarchs; 2. of holy angels; 3. of the sun and moon.
1¶

Nearly all the wisdom we truly possess — wisdom that is real and solid — comes down to two things: the knowledge of God and the knowledge of ourselves. But the two are so tightly bound together that it is hard to say which comes first and gives rise to the other. For one thing, no one can take an honest look at himself without his thoughts turning at once to the God in whom he lives and moves; it is perfectly plain that the gifts we have could never have come from ourselves — indeed, that our very existence is nothing but our being sustained, moment by moment, in God alone. And the blessings that rain down on us without ceasing are like streams leading us back to their fountain. Here again, the sheer abundance of good that dwells in God stands out all the more against the backdrop of our own poverty. The wretched ruin into which the fall of the first man has plunged us forces us to look upward — not only so that, hungry and starving, we may ask there for what we lack, but so that, shaken by fear, we may learn humility. For there is in man something like a whole world of misery; ever since we were stripped of our God-given garments, our naked shame has exposed an endless train of disgraceful things. Every one of us, stung by the awareness of his own unhappiness, gains in this way at least some knowledge of God. So our sense of ignorance, emptiness, and weakness — in short, of our depravity and corruption — reminds us that in the Lord, and in him alone, dwell the true light of wisdom, real strength, and overflowing goodness. We are driven by our own evils to consider the good things of God; and in truth we cannot seek him in earnest until we have begun to be displeased with ourselves. For what man would not rather settle down content in himself? And who does not do exactly that, so long as he does not yet know himself — satisfied with his own gifts, and either unaware of his misery or unwilling to remember it? This is why coming to know ourselves does not merely prompt us to seek God; it leads us by the hand to find him.

2¶

On the other hand, no one ever arrives at a true knowledge of himself until he has first looked upon the face of God, and then turned from that sight to examine himself. For such is our inborn pride that we always imagine ourselves to be righteous, upright, wise, and holy — until clear evidence convinces us of our injustice, vileness, folly, and impurity. And we will never be convinced if we look only at ourselves and not also at the Lord, who is the one standard by which alone such conviction can come. Because we are all naturally prone to hypocrisy, any hollow appearance of righteousness is more than enough to satisfy us in place of the real thing. Since nothing within us or around us is free from deep corruption, as long as we keep our minds within the limits of human pollution, anything even slightly less filthy pleases us as though it were spotless — just as an eye that has seen nothing but black will judge something off-white, or even brownish, to be pure white. Our bodily sight gives an even sharper picture of how badly we deceive ourselves about the powers of the mind. At midday, when we look down at the ground or at the things around us, we think our eyesight strong and sharp; but when we lift our eyes to the sun and look straight at it, the vision that did so well on earth is instantly so dazzled and overwhelmed that we must admit our keenness for earthly things is mere dimness next to the sun. It is the same when we weigh our spiritual qualities. As long as we look no higher than the earth, we are quite pleased with our own righteousness, wisdom, and strength; we flatter ourselves and feel we are little less than gods. But once we begin to lift our thoughts to God and consider who he is — and how perfect and absolute is the righteousness, wisdom, and power to which we are bound to conform — what once delighted us under the name of righteousness will be exposed as the worst kind of wickedness; what passed for wisdom will disgust us as utter folly; and what looked like strength will be condemned as pitiful weakness. So far are even our most polished qualities from matching the purity of God.

3¶

This is why, as Scripture consistently records, holy men were struck and overwhelmed with dread and amazement whenever they beheld the presence of God. When we see men who had stood firm and secure suddenly shaking with terror — gripped by the fear of death, all but swallowed up and undone — the lesson is plain: no one is ever truly humbled by a sense of his own smallness until he has measured himself against the majesty of God. Examples of this terror appear again and again, in the Book of Judges and in the Prophets.

But although the knowledge of God and the knowledge of ourselves are bound together, the proper order is to treat the knowledge of God first, and then come down to the knowledge of ourselves.

Calvin opens the whole work with a hinge: there is no true knowledge of God that does not also lay us bare, and no honest knowledge of ourselves that does not drive us upward to him in whom we live and move. The two are bound together, and self-flattery is broken only when we set our lives against his blazing holiness. In 2026 this cuts against the air we breathe — a culture that prizes self-esteem, curates an image, and measures itself against the dimmest standard available. Bring a man before the face of God, and his comfortable verdict on himself collapses. So teach your children, before you teach them anything else, that they are creatures who owe their very being to their Maker; let your family worship begin not with our achievements but with his majesty. The patriarchs and prophets fell on their faces in that presence. A church that recovers such trembling will not chase relevance — it will know itself, and so begin to know God.
