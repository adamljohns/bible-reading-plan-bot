Book 1 — Argument. ARGUMENT. The First Book treats of the knowledge of God the Creator. But as it is in the creation of man that the divine perfections are best displayed, so man also is made the subject of discourse. Thus the whole book divides itself into two principal heads—the former relating to the knowledge of God, and the latter to the knowledge of man. In the first chapter, these are considered jointly; and in each of the following chapters, separately: occasionally, however, intermingled with other matters which refer to one or other of the heads; e.g. , the discussions concerning Scripture and images, falling under the former head, and the other three concerning the creation of the world, the holy angels and devils, falling under the latter. The last point discussed—viz. the method of the divine government, relates to both. With regard to the former head—viz. the knowledge of God, it is shown, in the first place, what the kind of knowledge is which God requires, Chap. 2. And, in the second place (Chap. 3-9), where this knowledge must be sought, namely, not in man; because, although naturally implanted in the human mind, it is stifled, partly by ignorance, partly by evil intent, Chap. 3 and 4; not in the frame of the world: because, although it shines most clearly there, we are so stupid that these manifestations, however perspicuous, pass away without any beneficial result, Chap. 5; but in Scripture (Chap. 6), which is treated of, Chap. 7-9. In the third place, it is shown what the character of God is, Chap. 10. In the fourth place, how impious it is to give a visible form to God (here images, the worship of them, and its origin, are considered), Chap. 11. In the fifth place, it is shown that God is to be solely and wholly worshipped, Chap. 12. Lastly, Chap. 13 treats of the unity of the divine essence, and the distinction of three persons. With regard to the latter head—viz. the knowledge of man, first, Chap. 14 treats of the creation of the world, and of good and bad angels (these all having reference to man). And then Chap. 15, taking up the subject of man himself, examines his nature and his powers. The better to illustrate the nature both of God and man, the three remaining Chapters—viz. 16-18, proceed to treat of the general government of the world, and particularly of human actions, in opposition to fortune and fate, explaining both the doctrine and its use. In conclusion, it is shown, that though God employs the instrumentality of the wicked, he is pure from sin and from taint of every kind.

The Knowledge of God and of Ourselves Mutually Connected — Nature of the Connection

Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. The sum of true wisdom—viz. the knowledge of God and of ourselves. Effects of the latter.
  2. 2. Effects of the knowledge of God, in humbling our pride, unveiling our hypocrisy, demonstrating the absolute perfections of God, and our own utter helplessness.
  3. 3. Effects of the knowledge of God illustrated by the examples, 1. of holy patriarchs; 2. of holy angels; 3. of the sun and moon.
1¶

Our wisdom, in so far as it ought to be counted true and solid wisdom, consists almost entirely of two parts: the knowledge of God and the knowledge of ourselves. But since these are bound together by many ties, it is not easy to determine which of the two comes first and gives birth to the other. For, in the first place, no one can look at himself without immediately turning his thoughts toward the God in whom he lives and moves; because it is perfectly plain that the gifts we possess cannot possibly come from ourselves; indeed, that our very being is nothing else than a subsisting in God alone. In the second place, those blessings which flow down to us drop by drop from heaven without ceasing are like streams leading us to the fountain. Here again, the infinite good that dwells in God becomes more evident from our own poverty. In particular, the miserable ruin into which the revolt of the first man has plunged us compels us to lift our eyes upward; not only so that, hungry and starving, we may ask from there what we lack, but so that, roused by fear, we may learn humility. For as there exists in man something like a world of misery, and ever since we were stripped of the divine clothing our naked shame lays bare an immense train of disgraceful qualities, every man, stung by the awareness of his own unhappiness, necessarily gains in this way at least some knowledge of God. Thus our sense of ignorance, vanity, want, weakness, and in short of depravity and corruption, reminds us (see Calvin on John 4:10) that in the Lord, and in none but him, dwell the true light of wisdom, solid virtue, and overflowing goodness. We are therefore driven by our own evils to consider the good things of God; and indeed, we cannot aspire to him in earnest until we have begun to be displeased with ourselves. For what man is not inclined to rest in himself? Who, in fact, does not rest in himself in this way, so long as he does not know himself; that is, so long as he is content with his own gifts, and unaware or forgetful of his misery? Every person, therefore, on coming to the knowledge of himself, is not only urged to seek God, but is also led as if by the hand to find him.

2¶

On the other hand, it is evident that man never arrives at a true knowledge of himself until he has first looked upon the face of God, and has come down from that contemplation to look into himself. For (such is our inborn pride) we always seem to ourselves righteous, upright, wise, and holy, until we are convinced by clear evidence of our unrighteousness, vileness, folly, and impurity. Yet we are not convinced of this if we look to ourselves only, and not to the Lord as well, since he is the only standard by which this conviction can be produced. For since we are all naturally prone to hypocrisy, any empty appearance of righteousness is quite enough to satisfy us in place of righteousness itself. And since nothing appears within us or around us that is not stained with very great impurity, so long as we keep our minds within the limits of human pollution, anything that is in some small degree less defiled delights us as though it were perfectly pure; just as an eye to which nothing but black has previously been shown judges an object of a whitish, or even a brownish, color to be perfectly white. Indeed, the bodily senses may furnish a still stronger illustration of how far we are deceived in judging the powers of the mind. If at midday we either look down at the ground or at the objects around us that lie open to view, we think ourselves gifted with very strong and piercing eyesight; but when we look up at the sun and gaze at it directly, the sight that did excellently well for the earth is instantly so dazzled and confounded by the brightness that we are forced to confess that our keenness in seeing earthly things is mere dimness when turned toward the sun. So too it happens when we judge our spiritual qualities. So long as we do not look beyond the earth, we are quite pleased with our own righteousness, wisdom, and virtue; we speak to ourselves in the most flattering terms, and seem only a little less than demigods. But if we once begin to raise our thoughts to God, and to reflect what kind of Being he is, and how absolute is the perfection of that righteousness, wisdom, and virtue to which, as to a standard, we are bound to be conformed, then what formerly delighted us with its false show of righteousness will appear polluted with the greatest iniquity; what so strangely deceived us under the name of wisdom will disgust us by its extreme folly; and what wore the appearance of virtuous strength will be condemned as the most miserable weakness. So far are those qualities in us that seem most perfect from corresponding to the purity of God.

3¶

Hence that dread and amazement with which, as Scripture consistently relates, holy men were struck and overwhelmed whenever they beheld the presence of God. When we see those who before stood firm and secure so shaken with terror that the fear of death takes hold of them, and they are, as it were, swallowed up and reduced to nothing, the conclusion to be drawn is that men are never rightly touched and impressed with a conviction of their own insignificance until they have compared themselves with the majesty of God. Frequent examples of this dismay occur both in the Book of Judges and in the Prophets: Judges 13:22; Isaiah 6:5; Ezekiel 1:28; 3:14; Job 9:4, etc.; Genesis 18:27; 1 Kings 19:13.

But although the knowledge of God and the knowledge of ourselves are bound together by a mutual tie, right order requires that we treat the former first, and then come down to the latter.

Calvin opens the whole work with a hinge: there is no true knowledge of God that does not also lay us bare, and no honest knowledge of ourselves that does not drive us upward to him in whom we live and move. The two are bound together, and self-flattery is broken only when we set our lives against his blazing holiness. In 2026 this cuts against the air we breathe — a culture that prizes self-esteem, curates an image, and measures itself against the dimmest standard available. Bring a man before the face of God, and his comfortable verdict on himself collapses. So teach your children, before you teach them anything else, that they are creatures who owe their very being to their Maker; let your family worship begin not with our achievements but with his majesty. The patriarchs and prophets fell on their faces in that presence. A church that recovers such trembling will not chase relevance — it will know itself, and so begin to know God.
