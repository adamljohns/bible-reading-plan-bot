Book 1 · Chapter 5 · USMC modern English

The Knowledge of God Conspicuous in the Creation, and Continual Government of the World

This chapter consists of two parts: 1. The former, which occupies the first ten sections, divides all the works of God into two great classes, and elucidates the knowledge of God as displayed in each class. The one class is treated of in the first six, and the other in the four following sections: 2. The latter part of the chapter shows, that, in consequence of the extreme stupidity of men, those manifestations of God, however perspicuous, lead to no useful result. This latter part, which commences at the eleventh section, is continued to the end of the chapter.

Sections in this chapter
  1. 1. The invisible and incomprehensible essence of God, to a certain extent, made visible in his works.
  2. 2. This declared by the first class of works—viz. the admirable motions of the heavens and the earth, the symmetry of the human body, and the connection of its parts; in short, the various objects which are presented to every eye.
  3. 3. This more especially manifested in the structure of the human body.
  4. 4. The shameful ingratitude of disregarding God, who, in such a variety of ways, is manifested within us. The still more shameful ingratitude of contemplating the endowments of the soul, without ascending to Him who gave them. No objection can be founded on any supposed organism in the soul.
  5. 5. The powers and actions of the soul, a proof of its separate existence from the body. Proofs of the soul’s immortality. Objection that the whole world is quickened by one soul. Reply to the objection. Its impiety.
  6. 6. Conclusion from what has been said—viz. that the omnipotence, eternity, and goodness of God, may be learned from the first class of works, i.e. , those which are in accordance with the ordinary course of nature.
  7. 7. The second class of works—viz. those above the ordinary course of nature, afford clear evidence of the perfections of God, especially his goodness, justice, and mercy.
  8. 8. Also his providence, power, and wisdom.
  9. 9. Proofs and illustrations of the divine Majesty. The use of them—viz. the acquisition of divine knowledge in combination with true piety.
  10. 10. The tendency of the knowledge of God to inspire the righteous with the hope of future life, and remind the wicked of the punishments reserved for them. Its tendency, moreover, to keep alive in the hearts of the righteous a sense of the divine goodness.
  11. 11. The second part of the chapter, which describes the stupidity both of learned and unlearned, in ascribing the whole order of things, and the admirable arrangements of divine Providence, to fortune.
  12. 12. Hence Polytheism, with all its abominations, and the endless and irreconcilable opinions of the philosophers concerning God.
  13. 13. All guilty of revolt from God, corrupting pure religion, either by following general custom, or the impious consent of antiquity.
  14. 14. Though irradiated by the wondrous glories of creation, we cease not to follow our own ways.
  15. 15. Our conduct altogether inexcusable, the dullness of perception being attributable to ourselves, while we are fully reminded of the true path, both by the structure and the government of the world.
1¶

Since the height of all happiness lies in knowing God, he has been pleased to make sure that no one is shut out from the way to that happiness. To that end he has not only planted in our minds the seed of religion that we have already spoken of, but he has also displayed his perfections throughout the whole structure of the universe, setting himself before our eyes every day, so that we cannot open them without being compelled to see him. His essence, it is true, is beyond our grasp; it utterly exceeds anything the human mind can conceive. But on every one of his works his glory is engraved in letters so bright, so clear, and so striking that no one, however slow-witted or unschooled, can use ignorance as an excuse. With perfect truth, then, the Psalmist cries out, "He covers himself with light as with a garment" (Psalm 104:2). It is as though he said that God first clothed himself in visible dress when, in creating the world, he raised those glorious banners on which, whichever way we turn, we see his perfections plainly displayed. In the same passage the Psalmist fittingly compares the open heavens to God's royal tent, saying, "He lays the beams of his chambers in the waters, makes the clouds his chariot, and walks on the wings of the wind," sending out the winds and the lightning as his swift messengers. And because the glory of his power and wisdom shines more brilliantly in the sky, the heavens are often called his palace. First of all, then, wherever you turn your eyes, there is no part of the world, however tiny, that does not show at least some sparks of beauty; and it is impossible to take in the vast and beautiful structure that stretches all around us without being overwhelmed by the sheer weight of its glory. This is why the author of the letter to the Hebrews finely describes the visible worlds as images of the invisible (Hebrews 11:3): the elegant design of the world serves us as a kind of mirror in which we can behold God, who is otherwise invisible. For the same reason the Psalmist gives a voice to the heavenly bodies, a voice that every nation understands (Psalm 19:1), since the showing forth of the Godhead is too clear to escape the notice of any people, no matter how dull. Stating this even more plainly, the apostle Paul says, "What may be known of God is plain to them, for God has shown it to them. For his invisible attributes, ever since the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood through the things he has made — namely, his eternal power and Godhead" (Romans 1:20).

2¶

As witnesses to his marvelous wisdom, both the heavens and the earth offer us countless proofs — not only the more hidden ones that astronomy, medicine, and all the natural sciences are meant to bring to light, but proofs that press themselves on the attention of the most unlearned farmer, who cannot open his eyes without seeing them. It is true that those who are more or less familiar with these branches of learning are helped by them and enabled to gain a deeper insight into the secret workings of God's wisdom. Yet no one, even if he knows nothing of these studies, is unable to discern such proofs of the Creator's wisdom as may well make him break out in wonder at his Maker. To trace the motions of the heavenly bodies, to fix their positions, measure their distances, and determine their properties takes skill and careful study; and where such study is pursued, then — since God's providence is thereby more fully unfolded — it is reasonable to suppose that the mind soars higher and gains brighter views of his glory. As Augustine said, the study of the heavens is a great help to the devout, but a torment to the merely curious.

3¶

This is why certain of the philosophers, in ancient times, gave careful attention to the structure of the human body. (See Aristotle, History of Animals, book 1, chapter 17; Macrobius on the Dream of Scipio, book 2, chapter 12; and Boethius, On Definition.)

4¶

But here the shameful ingratitude of human beings comes to light. Within their own persons they carry a workshop where countless operations of God are carried on, and a storehouse stocked with treasures beyond all price. Yet instead of bursting out in his praise, as they are bound to do, they only grow more inflated and swollen with pride. They feel how wonderfully God is at work within them, and their own experience tells them of the vast variety of gifts they owe to his generosity. Whether they like it or not, they cannot help knowing that these are proofs of his Godhead, and yet inwardly they smother that knowledge. They have no need to look any farther than themselves — provided they do not claim as their own what has been given them from heaven, and so put out the light meant to set God plainly before their minds. Even now, however, the earth bears on her surface many monstrous minds — minds that are not afraid to use the seed of deity planted in human nature as a tool for suppressing the name of God. Can anything be more detestable than this madness in a man who, finding God a hundred times over in both his body and his soul, makes that very excellence an excuse for denying that there is a God? He will not say that mere chance has made him different from the beasts that perish; but he sets up nature as the architect of the universe and so does away with the name of God. The swift motions of the soul, its noble faculties and rare gifts, declare the working of God so clearly that suppressing it would be impossible — were it not that the Epicureans, like so many Cyclops, use it as a stronghold from which to wage even bolder war against God. Are so many treasures of heavenly wisdom employed in directing such a worm as man, and shall the whole universe be denied that same care? To hold that the soul has organs corresponding to each of its faculties does nothing to dim the glory of God; rather, it displays it. Let Epicurus tell us what gathering of atoms, cooking food and drink, can turn one part of it into waste and another part into blood, and make every limb separately carry out its task as carefully as if there were so many souls acting in common consent to govern one body.

5¶

But my concern at present is not with that pigsty. I would rather deal with those who, led astray by absurd hair-splitting, are inclined to give a twist to the cold doctrine of Aristotle and use it both to disprove the immortality of the soul and to rob God of his rights. On the pretext that the soul's faculties are tied to bodily organs, they chain the soul to the body as though it could not exist on its own, and they try as hard as they can — by heaping praise on nature — to suppress the name of God. But there is no ground for claiming that the soul's powers are limited to carrying out bodily functions. What has the body to do with your measuring the heavens, counting the number of the stars, determining their sizes, their distances from one another, the speed at which they move, and the orbits they trace? I do not deny that astronomy has its use; all I mean to show is that these lofty investigations are not carried out by some arrangement of bodily organs, but by the faculties of the soul itself, entirely apart from the body. This single example will suggest many others to the reader. The swift and nimble movements of the soul as it darts from heaven to earth, links the future with the past, holds onto the memory of years gone by — indeed, fashions creations of its own — together with its skill in making astonishing discoveries and inventing so many wonderful arts, are sure signs of God's working in man. And what shall we say of the soul's activity while the body sleeps — its many turning thoughts, its many useful insights, its many sound arguments, even its foresight of things yet to come? What shall we say, except that man carries about with him a mark of immortality that can never be erased? But how can man be a thing so nearly divine and yet not acknowledge his Creator? Shall we, by a power of judging planted in our hearts, tell justice from injustice, and yet there be no judge in heaven? Shall some remnants of intelligence stay with us even in sleep, and yet no God keep watch in heaven? Shall we be reckoned the inventors of so many arts and useful skills, so that God may be cheated of his praise — when experience tells us plainly enough that whatever we possess is handed out to us in unequal measures by another? The talk of certain people about a secret inspiration that gives life to the whole world is not only silly but downright profane. Such people delight in the famous lines of Virgil (Aeneid, book 6, lines 724ff.; see Calvin on Acts 17:28; Manilius, Astronomica, book 1).

Here we see how poorly that thin speculation — of a universal mind animating and invigorating the world — is suited to give birth to godliness in our minds, or to nourish it. We have an even clearer proof of this in the profane verses that the immoral Lucretius wrote as a conclusion drawn from the very same principle. At the beginning of his first book he maintains that nothing comes from nothing, but that all things are formed out of certain original materials. He also twists the ordinary course of generation into an argument against the existence of God. In his fifth book, however, he admits that the world was born and will die.

6¶

Let each of us, therefore, as we consider our own nature, remember that there is one God who governs all natures — and who, in governing them, wishes us to have regard for himself, to make him the object of our faith, our worship, and our adoration. Nothing could be more absurd than to enjoy the noble gifts that point to the divine presence within us while neglecting the one who, of his own good pleasure, bestows them on us. As for his power, how glorious are the displays by which he presses us to contemplate himself — unless, of course, we pretend not to know whose energy it is that sustains the boundless fabric of the universe by a word. Sometimes he makes heaven ring with thunder, hurls the scorching lightning, and sets the whole sky ablaze; at other times he stirs up raging storms, and then in a single moment, whenever it pleases him, brings a perfect calm. He keeps the sea — which seems forever threatening to flood the earth — suspended as though in midair; now he whips it into fury with the force of the winds, now he soothes its rage and stills all its waves. Here we might point to those glowing descriptions of divine power displayed through events in nature that run all through Scripture, but especially in the book of Job and the prophecies of Isaiah. I deliberately leave these aside for now, because a better place to bring them in will come when I treat the scriptural account of creation (see chapter 14, sections 1, 2, 20, and following). I only want to note here that this way of searching out God's perfections — tracing the features of his face as outlined in the sky and on the earth — is open both to those inside the Church and to those outside it. From the power of God we are naturally led to consider his eternity, since that from which all other things take their origin must necessarily be self-existent and eternal. Furthermore, if we ask what reason moved him to create all things in the beginning, and what now moves him to preserve them, we shall find that there could be no other reason than his own goodness. And if this is the only reason, nothing more should be needed to draw out our love toward him — for, as the Psalmist reminds us, every creature shares in his mercy: "His tender mercies are over all his works" (Psalm 145:9).

7¶

In the second class of God's works — namely, those that rise above the ordinary course of nature — the evidence of his perfections is in every respect just as clear. For in directing the affairs of human beings, he so arranges the course of his providence that day by day he declares, by the plainest displays, that although all people share in his bounty in countless ways, the righteous are the special objects of his favor and the wicked and profane the special objects of his severity. There is no doubting his punishment of crimes; and at the same time he declares in no uncertain way that he is the protector — even the avenger — of innocence, by showering blessings on the good, supplying their needs, soothing and comforting their griefs, easing their sufferings, and providing for their safety in every way. And though he often lets the guilty exult for a time without punishment, and the innocent be driven back and forth in adversity — even wickedly and unjustly oppressed — this should not raise any doubt about the unfailing justice of all that he does. The very opposite conclusion should be drawn. When some single crime calls forth visible signs of his anger, it must be because he hates all crimes; and on the other hand, his leaving many crimes unpunished only proves that a judgment is held in reserve, when the punishment now delayed will at last be carried out. In the same way, how richly he gives us cause to consider his mercy when — as often happens — he keeps on visiting wretched sinners with tireless kindness, until he overcomes their depravity and woos them back with more than a parent's tenderness.

8¶

To this end the Psalmist (Psalm 107) describes how God, in a wonderful way, often brings sudden and unlooked-for help to those in misery when they are almost at the edge of despair. He protects them when they wander in deserts and at last leads them back to the right path; he supplies them with food when they are starving for want; he frees them from iron chains and foul dungeons when they are captive; he guides them safely into harbor after shipwreck; he brings them back from the gates of death by curing their diseases; after scorching the fields with heat and drought, he makes them fertile again with the river of his grace; he lifts up the lowest of the people and casts down the mighty from their high seats. After offering examples of this kind, the Psalmist concludes that the things people call chance events are in fact so many proofs of divine providence — and especially of God's fatherly kindness — giving the righteous ground for joy and at the same time silencing the ungodly. But since the greater part of humanity, enslaved by error, walks about blindfolded in this glorious theater, he exclaims that it takes rare and singular wisdom to ponder carefully these works of God, which many who seem most sharp-sighted in other matters look upon without profit. It is indeed true that the brightest display of divine glory finds scarcely one genuine spectator in a hundred. Still, neither his power nor his wisdom is hidden in darkness. His power is strikingly shown when the rage of the wicked, which seemed unstoppable, is crushed in a single moment — their arrogance humbled, their strongest defenses overthrown, their armor shattered, their strength broken, their schemes thwarted without effort, and the audacity that set itself above the heavens is hurled down to the lowest depths of the earth. On the other hand, the poor are raised up out of the dust and the needy lifted out of the dunghill (Psalm 113:7); the oppressed and afflicted are rescued in their extremity, the despairing are filled with hope, the unarmed defeat the armed, the few defeat the many, the weak defeat the strong. And the excellence of God's wisdom is shown in dealing out everything at the right season, in confounding the wisdom of the world and catching the wise in their own craftiness (1 Corinthians 3:19) — in short, in guiding all things in perfect accordance with reason.

9¶

We see, then, that there is no need for a long and laborious chain of argument in order to find proofs that display and uphold the divine majesty. The few we have merely touched on show them to be so immediately within our reach on every side that we can trace them with the eye or point to them with the finger. And here we must note again (see chapter 2, section 2) that the knowledge of God we are urged to cultivate is not the kind that, content with empty speculation, merely flutters in the brain. It is a knowledge that proves solid and fruitful wherever it is rightly grasped and rooted in the heart. The Lord is made known by his perfections. When we feel their power within us and are aware of their benefits, that knowledge must impress us far more vividly than if we merely imagined a God whose presence we had never felt. From this it is plain that in seeking God the most direct path and the fittest method is not to try, with presumptuous curiosity, to pry into his essence — which is to be adored rather than minutely examined — but to contemplate him in his works, by which he draws near, becomes familiar to us, and in a manner gives himself to us. This is what the apostle had in mind when he said that we need not go far in search of him (Acts 17:27), since by the continual working of his power he dwells in every one of us. So too David (Psalm 145), after acknowledging that God's greatness cannot be searched out, goes on to list his works, declaring that through them his greatness is unfolded. It becomes us, then, to pursue diligently that searching out of God which so fills the soul with wonder and at the same time makes a deep and effective impression on it. And as Augustine puts it (on Psalm 144), since we are unable to comprehend him and are, as it were, overwhelmed by his greatness, our proper course is to contemplate his works and so refresh ourselves with his goodness.

10¶

By the knowledge we gain in this way we ought to be stirred not only to worship God but also to be roused and lifted up to the hope of the life to come. For when we observe that the displays the Lord gives of both his mercy and his severity are only begun and left incomplete, we ought to conclude that these are surely just a prelude to higher displays, whose full unveiling is reserved for another state. And conversely, when we see the righteous brought low by the ungodly, attacked with injuries, buried under slanders, and torn by insult and abuse — while, on the other hand, the wicked flourish, prosper, and gain ease and honor, all without punishment — we ought at once to conclude that there will be a life to come in which wickedness will receive its punishment and righteousness its reward. Moreover, when we observe that the Lord often lays his chastening rod on the righteous, we may be all the more sure that the unrighteous will in the end by no means escape the lashes of his anger. There is a well-known passage in Augustine (City of God, book 1, chapter 8): "If every sin were now openly punished, it might be thought that nothing was held back for the final judgment; and on the other hand, if no sin were now openly punished, it might be supposed there was no divine providence at all." We must acknowledge, then, that in each of God's works — and still more in all of them taken together — the divine perfections are painted as in a picture, and the whole human race is thereby invited and drawn to come to the knowledge of God, and through that knowledge to true and complete happiness. And while his perfections are displayed so vividly, the only way to grasp how they actually work and what they aim at is to descend into ourselves and consider how the Lord there shows his wisdom, power, and energy — how he there displays his justice, goodness, and mercy. For although David (Psalm 92:6) rightly complains of the deep folly of the ungodly in not pondering the deep counsels of God as shown in his governing of the human race, what he says elsewhere (Psalm 40) is most true: that the wonders of divine wisdom in this respect outnumber the hairs of our head. But I leave this subject for now, since it will be considered more fully later in its own place (Book 1, chapter 16, sections 6–9).

11¶

Yet bright as the display is that God gives of both himself and his eternal kingdom in the mirror of his works, such is our stupidity, and so dull are we toward these bright displays, that we draw no benefit from them. For when it comes to the structure and wonderful arrangement of the universe, how few of us are there who, in lifting our eyes to the heavens or looking out over the various regions of the earth, ever think of the Creator? Do we not rather overlook him and lazily content ourselves with a view of his works? And as for supernatural events, though they happen every day, how few credit them to the ruling providence of God — and how many imagine they are merely chance results produced by the blind turning of the wheel of fortune? Even when, guided and directed by such events, we are in a manner forced to consider God (something everyone must experience now and then), and are thus led to form some impression of the Deity, we immediately fly off into carnal dreams and corrupt fictions, and so by our own emptiness corrupt heavenly truth. We differ from one another in this, that each of us claims some particular error as his own; but we are all alike in this, that we put monstrous fictions in the place of the one living and true God. This disease is not confined to slow and common minds; it affects the noblest, and those who in other respects are remarkably sharp. How lavishly, in this matter, the whole body of philosophers has betrayed its stupidity and lack of sense! To say nothing of the others, whose absurdities are even grosser, how completely does Plato — the soberest and most religious of them all — lose himself in his round globe (Plato in the Timaeus; see also Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods, book 1; Plutarch, On the Opinions of the Philosophers, book 1)?

12¶

From this comes that vast flood of error in which the whole world is drowned. Since every individual mind is a kind of labyrinth, it is no wonder that not only has each nation adopted a variety of fictions, but almost every single person has had his own god. To the darkness of ignorance have been added presumption and recklessness, so that there is scarcely anyone to be found without some idol or phantom set up as a substitute for the Deity. Like water gushing from a vast and abundant spring, immense crowds of gods have poured out of the human mind, every man giving himself free rein and inventing some peculiar form of divinity to suit his own views. There is no need to attempt here a catalogue of the superstitions that overran the world. The task would be endless; and the corruptions themselves, even if not a word were said about them, furnish abundant evidence of the blindness of the human mind. I say nothing of the rude and unlettered common people; but even among the philosophers, as Cicero observes, those who have said that gods exist are in such variety and disagreement that it is wearisome to count up their opinions (Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods, books 1 and 2; Lactantius, Divine Institutes, book 1, and so on).

13¶

We must hold, then, that whoever corrupts pure religion — and this is bound to happen with all who cling to their own ideas — departs from the one God. No doubt they will claim that they have a different intention; but it matters little what they intend or persuade themselves to believe, since the Holy Spirit pronounces all of them apostates who, in the blindness of their minds, put demons in the place of God. For this reason Paul declares that the Ephesians were "without God" (Ephesians 2:12) until they learned from the Gospel what it is to worship the true God. And this must not be limited to one people only, since in another place he declares in general that all men "became vain in their imaginations" after the majesty of the Creator was made known to them in the structure of the world. Accordingly, in order to make way for the only true God, he condemns as lying and false all the gods celebrated among the Gentiles, leaving no deity anywhere except on Mount Zion, where the special knowledge of God was professed (Habakkuk 2:18, 20). Among the Gentiles in the time of Christ, the Samaritans no doubt came nearest to true godliness; yet we hear from his own mouth that they worshiped they knew not what (John 4:22) — from which it follows that they were deceived by empty errors. In short, though not all gave way to gross vice or rushed headlong into open idolatry, there was no pure and authentic religion founded merely on common belief. A few individuals may not have gone to all the insane lengths of the crowd; still, Paul's statement remains true, that the wisdom of God was not grasped by the rulers of this world (1 Corinthians 2:8). But if the most distinguished men wandered in darkness, what shall we say of the rabble? No wonder, then, that all worship devised by man is rejected by the Holy Spirit as degenerate. Any opinion a man can form about heavenly mysteries, even if it does not breed a long train of errors, is still the parent of error. And even if nothing worse should follow, this alone is no light sin — to worship at random a God one does not know. Of this very sin we hear from our Savior's own mouth (John 4:22): all are guilty of it who have not been taught from the law who the God is whom they ought to worship. Indeed, even Socrates, in Xenophon (Memorabilia, book 1), praises the response of Apollo, which charged every man to worship the gods according to the customs of his own country and the particular practice of his own city. But what right have mortals to decide on their own authority in a matter that is far above the world? Or who can so rely on the will of his forefathers, or the decrees of the people, as to receive a god at their hands without hesitation? Everyone will stick to his own judgment sooner than submit to the dictates of others. Since, then, in regulating the worship of God, the custom of a city or the agreement of the ancients is far too weak and fragile a bond of godliness, it remains that God himself must bear witness to himself from heaven.

14¶

It is in vain for us, therefore, that creation displays so many bright lamps lit to set forth the glory of its Author. Though they shine upon us from every quarter, by themselves they are wholly unable to lead us into the right path. They do throw out some sparks, to be sure; but these are quenched before they can give off a brighter glow. So the apostle, in the very passage where he says that the worlds are images of invisible things, adds that it is by faith we understand that they were framed by the word of God (Hebrews 11:3). By this he means that the invisible Godhead is indeed represented by such displays, but that we have no eyes to perceive it until they are opened through faith by an inward revelation from God. When Paul says that what may be known of God is made plain through the creation of the world, he does not mean a kind of disclosure that the human mind can comprehend (Romans 1:19); on the contrary, he shows that it goes no further than to leave us without excuse (Acts 17:27). And though he says elsewhere that we do not have to seek far for God, since he dwells within us, he shows in yet another passage how far this nearness to God actually gets us. God, he says, "in times past allowed all nations to walk in their own ways. Nevertheless, he did not leave himself without witness, in that he did good, gave us rain from heaven and fruitful seasons, and filled our hearts with food and gladness" (Acts 14:16, 17). But though God does not leave himself without a witness, wooing men to the knowledge of himself with countless and varied acts of kindness, still they do not stop following their own ways — that is to say, their deadly errors.

15¶

Yet even though we lack the natural powers that might enable us to rise to a pure and clear knowledge of God, still — since the dullness that blocks us is within us — there is no room for excuse. We cannot plead ignorance without at the same time being convicted by our own consciences of both laziness and ingratitude. It would indeed be a strange defense for a man to pretend that he has no ears to hear the truth, when dumb creatures have voices loud enough to declare it; to claim that he is unable to see what creatures without eyes plainly demonstrate; to excuse himself on the ground of feebleness of mind, when creatures without any reason at all are able to teach him. So when we wander and go astray, we are rightly shut out from every kind of excuse, because all things point to the right path. But while man must bear the guilt of corrupting the seed of divine knowledge so wonderfully planted in his mind, and keeping it from bearing good and genuine fruit, it is still perfectly true that we are not sufficiently taught by that bare and simple — yet magnificent — testimony which the creatures bear to the glory of their Creator. For no sooner do we, from a survey of the world, gain some slight knowledge of the Deity, than we pass by the true God and set up in his place the dream and phantom of our own brain, drawing the praise of justice, wisdom, and goodness away from the fountainhead and transferring it somewhere else. What is more, by the mistaken estimate we form, we either so obscure or so distort his daily works as at once to rob them of their glory and rob their author of his rightful praise.

Creation, Calvin says, is a glorious theatre — the heavens, the wonders of the body, the steady government of providence all engrave God's power, wisdom, and goodness in letters too bright for any to plead ignorance. Yet such is our dullness that scarcely one spectator in a hundred truly beholds him; we admire the works and overlook the Author, crediting chance, nature, or fortune, and so stand inexcusable. Our generation has only multiplied the irony — we map galaxies and sequence the genome, and call it all an accident. The fault, Calvin presses, is not in dim evidence but in blind eyes; the testimony is sufficient, the dullness is ours. Train your family to read the world rightly — to see the sunrise, the newborn's frame, the timely rain as the speech of a Father, not the noise of a machine. Still, this witness only renders us guilty; it cannot save. Let it drive us, then, to the Scripture and the Christ where God is known not merely as Maker but as Redeemer.
