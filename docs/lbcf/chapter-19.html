Chapter 19

Of the Law of God

The moral law forever binds all; the ceremonial and judicial laws given to Israel are abrogated in Christ.

1

God gave to Adam a law of universal obedience written in his heart, together with a particular precept forbidding him to eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. By these he bound him and all his posterity to personal, entire, exact, and perpetual obedience, promised life upon the fulfilling thereof, and threatened death upon the breach of it. He endued him also with power and ability to keep it.

Proof-texts
Gen 1:27 · Ecc 7:29 · Rom 10:5 · Gal 3:10,12
2

The same law that was first written in the heart of man continued to be a perfect rule of righteousness after the fall, and was delivered by God upon Mount Sinai in ten commandments, written in two tables — the first four containing our duty toward God, and the other six our duty to man.

Proof-texts
Rom 2:14-15 · Deut 10:4
3

Besides this law, commonly called moral, God was pleased to give to the people of Israel ceremonial laws, containing several typical ordinances — partly of worship, prefiguring Christ, his graces, actions, sufferings, and benefits; and partly holding forth various instructions of moral duties. All these ceremonial laws, being appointed only to the time of reformation, are by Jesus Christ the true Messiah and only law-giver, who was furnished with power from the Father for that end, abrogated and taken away.

Proof-texts
Heb 10:1 · Col 2:17 · 1 Cor 5:7 · Col 2:14,16-17 · Eph 2:14,16
4

To Israel he also gave sundry judicial laws, which expired together with the state of that people, not obliging any now by virtue of that institution. Their general equity only is of moral use.

Proof-texts
1 Cor 9:8-10
5

The moral law forever binds all, as well justified persons as others, to the obedience thereof, and that not only in regard to the matter contained in it, but also in respect of the authority of God the Creator, who gave it. Neither does Christ in the gospel in any way dissolve this obligation, but much strengthen it.

Proof-texts
Rom 13:8-10 · Jas 2:8,10-12 · Matt 5:17-19 · Rom 3:31
6

Although true believers are not under the law as a covenant of works, to be thereby justified or condemned, yet it is of great use to them as well as to others. As a rule of life, informing them of the will of God and their duty, it directs and binds them to walk accordingly. It discovers also the sinful pollutions of their nature, hearts, and lives, so that examining themselves by it they may come to further conviction of, humiliation for, and hatred against sin — together with a clearer sight of their need of Christ and the perfection of his obedience. It is likewise of use to the regenerate to restrain their corruptions, in that it forbids sin; and the threatenings of it serve to show what even their sins deserve, and what afflictions in this life they may expect for them, although freed from the curse and unallayed rigor thereof. The promises of it likewise show them God's approbation of obedience and what blessings they may expect upon the performance of it — though not as due to them by the law as a covenant of works. So that a man's doing good and refraining from evil because the law encourages to the one and deters from the other is no evidence of his being under the law and not under grace.

Proof-texts
Rom 6:14 · Gal 2:16 · Rom 8:1 · Rom 10:4 · Rom 3:20 · Rom 7:7 · Rom 6:12-14 · 1 Pet 3:8-13
7

Neither are the forementioned uses of the law contrary to the grace of the gospel, but do sweetly comply with it — the Spirit of Christ subduing and enabling the will of man to do that freely and cheerfully which the will of God, revealed in the law, requires to be done.

Proof-texts
Gal 3:21 · Ezek 36:27
