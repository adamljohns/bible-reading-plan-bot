G2564 · Greek · New Testament
καλέω
Kaleō
Verb
To call, to name

Definition

The Greek verb kaleo means to call — properly aloud, Strong's says, and then in every direction that sense will stretch. The KJV renders it call, bid, and the whole family of naming expressions: whose name was called, he shall be called. Two senses run side by side in the New Testament and both matter. To call someone is to summon them, and to call someone something is to name them. Scripture keeps both in play, because in the Bible a name given by God is not a label attached after the fact but a declaration of what a person is and will do.

Usage & Theological Significance

Matthew opens with the naming sense used at full weight. The angel tells Joseph he shall call his name JESUS, for he shall save his people from their sins (Matthew 1:21) — the name is the job. The prophecy alongside it gives another, they shall call his name Emmanuel (Matthew 1:23), and Joseph obeys and calls his name Jesus (Matthew 1:25). The same verb then carries the childhood: out of Egypt have I called my son (Matthew 2:15), and He came to dwell in Nazareth so that He should be called a Nazarene (Matthew 2:23). Later the summoning sense takes over and turns sharp. The king sends forth his servants to call them that were bidden to the wedding, and they would not come (Matthew 22:3); the call is issued, refused, and reissued. When the householder pays his labourers he tells his steward to call them and give them their hire (Matthew 20:8), beginning with the last. And Jesus warns the twelve that if men have called the master of the house Beelzebub, they will not spare his household (Matthew 10:25) — being named by God does not stop men naming you otherwise.
