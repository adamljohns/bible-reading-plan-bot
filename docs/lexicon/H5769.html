H5769 · Hebrew · Old Testament
עוֹלָם
Olam
Noun, masculine
Everlasting, age-long time

Definition

The Hebrew noun olam points to the vanishing point of time. Strong's derives it from the idea of what is concealed, and that is the sense: a stretch of time whose far end the eye cannot reach, whether it runs backward into the ancient past or forward without stopping. The KJV renders it everlasting, for ever, perpetual, of old, and always, choosing by context rather than by rule. Hebrew is not making a philosophical claim about timelessness here so much as a practical one about horizon — olam is duration that outlasts the one measuring it.

Usage & Theological Significance

The word does its heaviest work in the covenant God cuts with David. The throne of his kingdom is established for ever (2 Samuel 7:13), his house and kingdom are made sure for ever (2 Samuel 7:16), and God establishes Israel to Himself for a people for ever (2 Samuel 7:24). David hears it and answers in the same register, asking that the word be established for ever (2 Samuel 7:25) so that God's name be magnified for ever (2 Samuel 7:26). At the end of his life he names the whole arrangement an everlasting covenant, ordered in all things and sure (2 Samuel 23:5), and that is the ground he dies on rather than his own record. The same word cuts the other direction when the judgment is pronounced that the sword shall never depart from his house (2 Samuel 12:10), a reminder that olam attaches to consequence as readily as to promise. What the believer takes from it is not an abstraction about eternity but a settled floor: God's commitments outlast the people who receive them.
